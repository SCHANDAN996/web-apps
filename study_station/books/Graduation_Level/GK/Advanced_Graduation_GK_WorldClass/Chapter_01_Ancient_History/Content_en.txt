# Ancient Indian History

## The Hook: A King Who Gave Up War

In 261 BCE, on the battlefield of Kalinga, Emperor Ashoka won the war — and lost his appetite for war forever. Over 1,00,000 people died, and the victor himself became history's most famous convert to peace. He then did something no ruler had done before: he carved his regrets and his laws onto rocks and pillars across the subcontinent, in a script (Brahmi) that India forgot for 2,000 years — until James Prinsep deciphered it in **1837**. That single act of decipherment unlocked the entire Mauryan age for us.

Ancient Indian History is not a list of dead kings. It is the story of how cities, religions, empires, and ideas were born on this soil — and examiners love it precisely because students mix up its similar-sounding names and dates. Let's make sure you never do.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
1. Arrange the major periods — Indus Valley, Vedic, Mahajanapadas, Mauryas, Guptas — in correct chronological order.
2. Match rulers, texts, travellers, and sites without confusion.
3. Spot the examiner's favourite traps: near-correct statements, swapped dynasties, and hidden "NOT" questions.

## 1. The Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2500–1750 BCE)

Also called the **Harappan Civilization** (after Harappa, the first site excavated, in 1921), this was a **Bronze Age** civilization spread across present-day Pakistan, Punjab, Haryana, Gujarat, and Rajasthan. It was the largest of the contemporary civilizations (Egypt, Mesopotamia, China) in area.

Key sites and what they are famous for:
- **Harappa** (Ravi river) — granaries, cemetery R-37
- **Mohenjo-daro** (Indus river) — the **Great Bath**, the bronze **Dancing Girl**, the **Pashupati seal**
- **Dholavira** (Gujarat) — sophisticated water management, three-part city division
- **Lothal** (Gujarat) — the **dockyard**, a port city
- **Kalibangan** (Rajasthan) — ploughed field, fire altars
- **Chanhudaro** — bead-making factory; the only major site **without a citadel**

The cities had grid-pattern streets, covered drains, and standardized burnt bricks (ratio 1:2:4). The people worshipped a mother goddess and a proto-Shiva figure, used an **undeciphered script**, and traded with Mesopotamia (where Harappan seals have been found). Notably, **iron was unknown** to them, and no clear temples or palaces have been found.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which site had a dockyard? Which had the Great Bath? Which site lacked a citadel? Say the answers aloud before reading on.

## 2. The Vedic Age (c. 1500–600 BCE)

The **Aryans** entered north-west India around 1500 BCE. Their literature — the **Vedas** — is our main source.

**Early (Rigvedic) Period, c. 1500–1000 BCE:** centred on the **Sapta Sindhu** region (land of seven rivers). Society was tribal, pastoral, and relatively egalitarian; the **Battle of Ten Kings (Dasarajna)** on the river Parushni (Ravi) was won by the Bharata king **Sudas**. The famous **Gayatri Mantra** comes from the Rigveda.

**Later Vedic Period, c. 1000–600 BCE:** the Aryans moved east into the Ganga plains. Iron (called *shyama* or *krishna ayas*) helped clear forests. Society hardened into the four **varnas**; large sacrifices like **Ashvamedha** and **Rajasuya** appeared; kingdoms like Kuru and Panchala rose.

The four Vedas: **Rigveda** (oldest, hymns), **Samaveda** (melodies, root of Indian music), **Yajurveda** (rituals), **Atharvaveda** (spells and medicine). Each Veda has Brahmanas, Aranyakas, and Upanishads attached. The **Upanishads** (108 traditionally counted) contain philosophy — the doctrine of **Atman and Brahman** — and the phrase *Satyameva Jayate* comes from the **Mundaka Upanishad**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which Veda is the oldest? Where does "Satyameva Jayate" come from? What metal defines the Later Vedic shift eastward?

**Mnemonic 1 — Vedas in order:** "**R**eally **S**mart **Y**outh **A**chieve" → **R**ig, **S**ama, **Y**ajur, **A**tharva.

## 3. Buddhism and Jainism (6th century BCE)

The 6th century BCE saw a protest against ritualism, caste rigidity, and costly sacrifices — producing Buddhism and Jainism.

**Gautama Buddha** (born 563 BCE, **Lumbini**; enlightenment at **Bodh Gaya** under a peepal tree; first sermon at **Sarnath** — the *Dharmachakra Pravartana*; death — **Mahaparinirvana** — at **Kushinagar**, 483 BCE). His teaching: the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Eightfold Path (Ashtangika Marga)**. Buddhist texts are in **Pali** (the **Tripitakas**: Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma).

**Mahavira** (born 540 BCE, Kundagrama near Vaishali; died at **Pavapuri**, 468 BCE) was the **24th Tirthankara**; the first was **Rishabhdev (Adinath)** and the 23rd was **Parshvanath**. Jain texts are in **Prakrit (Ardhamagadhi)**. Core vows: ahimsa, satya, asteya, aparigraha (Mahavira added **brahmacharya**).

**Buddhist Councils** (a favourite exam area):
1. **First** — Rajgriha, 483 BCE, under Ajatashatru; compilation of the Tripitakas began.
2. **Second** — Vaishali, 383 BCE, under Kalasoka; the first schism.
3. **Third** — Pataliputra, c. 250 BCE, under **Ashoka**; Abhidhamma Pitaka added.
4. **Fourth** — Kundalvana (Kashmir), under **Kanishka**; split into **Hinayana and Mahayana**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Where was Buddha's first sermon? Under which ruler was the Fourth Council held? Who was the 23rd Tirthankara?

**Mnemonic 2 — Buddha's life events:** "**L**ittle **B**oy **S**ings **K**indly" → **L**umbini (birth), **B**odh Gaya (enlightenment), **S**arnath (first sermon), **K**ushinagar (death).

## 4. The Mahajanapadas and the Rise of Magadha

The **Anguttara Nikaya** lists **16 Mahajanapadas** (great kingdoms) around the 6th century BCE — including **Magadha** (capital Rajgriha, later Pataliputra), **Kosala** (Shravasti), **Vatsa** (Kaushambi), **Avanti** (Ujjain), and the **Vajji** confederacy (Vaishali), a republic.

**Magadha** won the race because of fertile land, iron ore, elephants, and ambitious rulers: **Bimbisara** (founder of Haryanka dynasty, contemporary of Buddha), **Ajatashatru**, then the **Shishunaga** and **Nanda** dynasties. **Alexander invaded India in 326 BCE**, fought **Porus** at the **Battle of Hydaspes (Jhelum)**, and turned back at the Beas.

🧠 **Active Recall:** How many Mahajanapadas? Which dynasty immediately preceded the Mauryas? In which year did Alexander invade?

## 5. The Mauryan Empire (322–185 BCE)

**Chandragupta Maurya** founded the empire in **322 BCE**, overthrowing the Nandas with the guidance of **Chanakya (Kautilya)**, author of the **Arthashastra**. The Greek ambassador **Megasthenes** wrote **Indica**, describing Pataliputra. Chandragupta defeated **Seleucus Nicator** (305 BCE) and, per Jain tradition, died at **Shravanabelagola** (Karnataka) by *sallekhana*.

**Bindusara** (known to Greeks as Amitraghata) followed; then **Ashoka** (reigned c. 268–232 BCE). The **Kalinga War (261 BCE)** — described in **Rock Edict XIII** — transformed him. His inscriptions use **Brahmi** (mostly), **Kharoshthi** (north-west), and Greek/Aramaic (Afghanistan). The **Lion Capital of Sarnath** is our national emblem; the Ashoka Chakra sits on our flag. Ashoka sent his son **Mahendra** and daughter **Sanghamitra** to Sri Lanka with Buddhism.

The last Maurya, **Brihadratha**, was killed by his commander **Pushyamitra Shunga** in **185 BCE** — founding the **Shunga dynasty**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Who wrote Arthashastra? Who wrote Indica? Which edict describes Kalinga? Who founded the Shunga dynasty?

**Mnemonic 3 — Mauryan order:** "**C**handra **B**inds **A**shoka" → **C**handragupta → **B**indusara → **A**shoka.

## 6. Post-Mauryan and the Age of the Guptas

After the Mauryas came the **Shungas**, **Kanvas**, **Satavahanas** (Deccan, capital Pratishthana/Paithan), and foreign dynasties: **Indo-Greeks** (Menander/Milinda — the *Milindapanho* records his dialogue with monk Nagasena), **Shakas**, and **Kushanas**. **Kanishka** (started the **Shaka era, 78 CE**) patronized Mahayana Buddhism and the scholars **Ashvaghosha** and **Nagarjuna**, plus the physician **Charaka**.

The **Gupta Empire** (founded by **Sri Gupta**; real founder **Chandragupta I**, who started the **Gupta Era in 319–320 CE**) is called the **Golden Age**:
- **Samudragupta** — the "Napoleon of India" (V.A. Smith); his conquests are recorded in the **Allahabad Pillar Inscription (Prayaga Prashasti)** by **Harisena**.
- **Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya)** — defeated the Shakas; the Chinese pilgrim **Fa-Hien** visited in his reign; his court had the **Navaratnas** including **Kalidasa**.
- **Aryabhata** (Aryabhatiya — zero's use, earth's rotation), **Varahamihira**, and the **Iron Pillar of Mehrauli** (rust-free metallurgy) belong to this age. **Nalanda University** was founded by **Kumaragupta I**.

Later, **Harshavardhana** (606–647 CE) ruled from Kannauj; the Chinese pilgrim **Hiuen Tsang** visited his court, and **Banabhatta** wrote the *Harshacharita* and *Kadambari*.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which inscription records Samudragupta's campaigns? Which pilgrim came under Chandragupta II, and which under Harsha? Who founded Nalanda?

## 7. Sangam Age and South India (brief)

South Indian history rests on **Sangam literature** (Tamil, three Sangams at Madurai). Three crowned kings: **Cheras** (bow emblem), **Cholas** (tiger), **Pandyas** (fish). Key texts: **Silappadikaram** (by Ilango Adigal) and **Manimekalai** (by Sattanar). The **Pallavas** (Kanchipuram — Mahabalipuram rathas) and **Chalukyas** (Badami — **Pulakeshin II** defeated Harsha on the Narmada; the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang and the **Aihole inscription** by Ravikirti record this) dominate the early medieval Deccan.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Chronological Confusion (कालानुक्रमिक भ्रम):** "The Fourth Buddhist Council was held under Ashoka at Pataliputra." Sounds right — but the **Third** Council was Ashoka's; the **Fourth** was under **Kanishka** in Kashmir. Examiners routinely swap the two most famous patrons of Buddhism. Fix: Ashoka → Third → Pataliputra; Kanishka → Fourth → Kashmir → Hinayana/Mahayana split.

**Trap 2 — Near-Correct Statement (लगभग सही कथन):** "Fa-Hien visited India during the reign of Harshavardhana." Everything about Fa-Hien being a Chinese pilgrim is correct — but he came under **Chandragupta II**; **Hiuen Tsang** came under Harsha. The two Chinese travellers are the single most swapped pair in ancient history questions.

**Trap 3 — Negative Phrasing (नकारात्मक वाक्यांश):** "Which of the following was **NOT** known to the Indus Valley people?" Options: copper, bronze, iron, gold. Students who skim miss the "NOT" and pick a metal they *did* know. The answer is **iron**. Always circle the NOT/EXCEPT before reading options.
