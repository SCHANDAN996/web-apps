Card 1
Front: Who presents the Union Budget in Parliament, and under which Article of the Constitution?
Back: The Union Finance Minister presents it under Article 112, which mandates the laying of the "Annual Financial Statement" before both Houses of Parliament.

Card 2
Front: The word "Budget" never appears in the Indian Constitution — so what is the Budget constitutionally called?
Back: The "Annual Financial Statement" (Article 112). The word "budget" comes from the French word "bougette," meaning a small leather bag.

Card 3
Front: On what date is the Union Budget now presented, and since which year did this change happen?
Back: 1st February, since Budget 2017–18 (earlier it was the last working day of February). The railway budget was also merged with the general budget from 2017–18.

Card 4
Front: What is the key difference between Fiscal Deficit and Revenue Deficit?
Back: Revenue Deficit = Revenue expenditure − Revenue receipts (day-to-day mismatch). Fiscal Deficit = Total expenditure − Total receipts excluding borrowings (shows total borrowing need). Fiscal deficit is the broader and most-watched figure.

Card 5
Front: Which deficit measure is called the "true indicator" of government borrowing?
Back: Fiscal Deficit — it equals the government's total borrowing requirement for the year. Mnemonic: "Fiscal = Full borrowing picture."

Card 6
Front: What is a "Primary Deficit," and what does a zero primary deficit imply?
Back: Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest payments. Zero primary deficit means the government is borrowing only to pay interest on past loans, not for fresh spending.

Card 7
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you remember the order of deficits from broadest to narrowest?
Back: "F-P-R" — Fiscal (broadest, includes everything), Primary (Fiscal minus interest), Revenue (only current/revenue account). Remember: "First Pay Rent" — Fiscal → Primary → Revenue.

Card 8
Front: Who prepares the Economic Survey, and when is it tabled?
Back: It is prepared by the Department of Economic Affairs, Ministry of Finance, under the guidance of the Chief Economic Adviser, and is tabled in Parliament one day before the Union Budget (31st January). It was presented in two volumes until 2021–22; from 2022–23 onward it is a single-volume document.

Card 9
Front: Examiner's trap: "The Economic Survey is a constitutional document tabled under Article 112." True or false?
Back: False. The Economic Survey has NO constitutional backing — it is an executive document of the Finance Ministry. Article 112 covers only the Annual Financial Statement (Budget). Examiners swap the two to trap candidates.

Card 10
Front: What is the "Halwa Ceremony" associated with the Budget?
Back: A tradition where halwa is served to officials involved in Budget preparation, marking the start of the "lock-in" period — officials stay isolated in the Budget press (North Block) to maintain secrecy until the Budget is presented.

Card 11
Front: What is the difference between "Vote on Account" and "Interim Budget"?
Back: Vote on Account only grants Parliament's approval for essential expenditure for a few months (usually 2). An Interim Budget (presented in an election year) is a full financial statement with income and expenditure estimates, but a new government presents the full Budget later.

Card 12
Front: What are the three stages of the Budget process in Parliament after presentation?
Back: (1) General Discussion, (2) Voting on Demands for Grants (Lok Sabha only), (3) Passing of the Appropriation Bill and then the Finance Bill. Mnemonic: "Discuss → Demand → Disburse."

Card 13
Front: Which Bill gives legal effect to the government's taxation proposals, and what type of Bill is it?
Back: The Finance Bill — it is a Money Bill under Article 110, so it can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, and the Rajya Sabha can only recommend changes within 14 days.

Card 14
Front: What is "Disinvestment" vs "Strategic Disinvestment"?
Back: Disinvestment = selling part of government equity in PSUs (government may retain control). Strategic Disinvestment = selling a substantial stake along with transfer of management control (usually above 50%).

Card 15
Front: Examiner's trap: "Capital expenditure includes interest payments on government debt." True or false?
Back: False. Interest payments are revenue expenditure (they create no asset). Capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities — e.g., building roads, loans to states, equity in PSUs. This swap is a classic trap.

Card 16
Front: What does "FRBM Act, 2003" mandate, and what was its original fiscal deficit target?
Back: The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act mandates fiscal discipline — originally targeting fiscal deficit of 3% of GDP and elimination of revenue deficit (targets later revised; the 2018 amendment set a debt-to-GDP anchor of 40% for the Centre by 2024–25).

Card 17
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you remember what goes into "Capital Receipts"?
Back: "B-D-R" — Borrowings, Disinvestment, Recovery of loans. Capital receipts either create a liability or reduce an asset. Remember: "Banks Demand Returns."

Card 18
Front: What is the "Effective Capital Expenditure" of the government?
Back: Capital expenditure plus grants-in-aid given to states/others for creation of capital assets. It captures asset creation even when the asset is built by states using central grants.

Card 19
Front: What is "Outcome Budget" and why was it introduced?
Back: Introduced from 2005–06, it links budgetary allocations to measurable outputs and outcomes of schemes, so Parliament and citizens can judge not just how much was spent but what was achieved.

Card 20
Front: Which Article deals with the "Contingency Fund of India," and who operates it?
Back: Article 267(1). It is placed at the disposal of the President (operated by the Finance Secretary on the President's behalf) to meet unforeseen expenditure pending parliamentary authorization. Its corpus was raised from ₹500 crore to ₹30,000 crore in 2020.
