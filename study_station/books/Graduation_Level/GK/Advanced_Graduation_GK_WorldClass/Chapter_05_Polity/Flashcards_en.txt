Card 1
Front: Which Article is called the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar?
Back: Article 32 — Right to Constitutional Remedies. It empowers citizens to move the Supreme Court directly for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

Card 2
Front: Which schedule of the Constitution was added by the very first amendment, and what does it protect?
Back: The Ninth Schedule, added by the 1st Amendment (1951). Laws placed in it were meant to be shielded from judicial review (mainly land reform laws), though after I.R. Coelho (2007) they are open to review if they violate the basic structure.

Card 3
Front: A Money Bill can be introduced only in which House, and who certifies it as a Money Bill?
Back: Only in the Lok Sabha (Article 109), with the prior recommendation of the President. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill, and that decision is final.

Card 4
Front: Which country did India borrow the concept of Fundamental Duties from, and which amendment inserted them?
Back: From the USSR (erstwhile Soviet Union). They were inserted by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, as Part IV-A (Article 51A), on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee.

Card 5
Front: Trick card: How can you quickly recall the 6 Fundamental Rights using a mnemonic?
Back: Use "E-F-E-R-C-R": E — Equality (Articles 14–18), F — Freedom (Articles 19–22), E — against Exploitation (Articles 23–24), R — Religion (Articles 25–28), C — Cultural & Educational (Articles 29–30), R — Remedies (Article 32). Hook: "Every Free Indian Enjoys Rights & Culture, Remedied."

Card 6
Front: Which Fundamental Right was deleted by the 44th Amendment, 1978, and what is it now?
Back: The Right to Property (originally Articles 19(1)(f) and 31). It is now only a legal right under Article 300A, not a Fundamental Right.

Card 7
Front: Examiner's trap: The President can proclaim a National Emergency only on the written advice of whom — the Prime Minister or the Cabinet?
Back: The Union Cabinet (not just the Prime Minister). The 44th Amendment made written Cabinet advice mandatory. Questions often swap "Cabinet" with "Prime Minister" to trap candidates.

Card 8
Front: Under which Article can the President impose President's Rule in a state, and what is the maximum duration with parliamentary approval?
Back: Article 356. Initially 6 months; extendable by Parliament every 6 months, up to a maximum of 3 years (with special conditions beyond one year).

Card 9
Front: Which amendment is called the "Mini-Constitution" and why?
Back: The 42nd Amendment, 1976. It made sweeping changes: added "Socialist, Secular, Integrity" to the Preamble, inserted Fundamental Duties, and altered the balance between Parliament and the judiciary.

Card 10
Front: Trick card: What mnemonic helps recall the three words added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment?
Back: "SSI" — Socialist, Secular, Integrity. Remember: "SSI joined the Preamble in 1976" (the only amendment ever made to the Preamble).

Card 11
Front: Who presides over a joint sitting of Parliament summoned under Article 108?
Back: The Speaker of the Lok Sabha. In the Speaker's absence, the Deputy Speaker; then the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. Note: a joint sitting cannot be summoned for a Money Bill or a Constitutional Amendment Bill.

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: Does the Rajya Sabha have the power to reject a Money Bill?
Back: No. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations within 14 days, which the Lok Sabha may accept or reject. Trap options often say the Rajya Sabha can "amend" or "withhold" a Money Bill — both are wrong.

Card 13
Front: Which Article deals with the amendment of the Constitution, and which case established the "basic structure" doctrine?
Back: Article 368. The basic structure doctrine was laid down in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) — Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure.

Card 14
Front: How are the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) different from Fundamental Rights in enforceability?
Back: DPSPs (Part IV, Articles 36–51) are non-justiciable — courts cannot enforce them. Fundamental Rights (Part III) are justiciable. DPSPs were borrowed from the Irish Constitution.

Card 15
Front: Which body conducts elections to Panchayats and Municipalities, and under which Articles?
Back: The State Election Commission — Article 243K (Panchayats) and Article 243ZA (Municipalities), both inserted by the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992).

Card 16
Front: What is the minimum age to become the President of India, and what is the term of office?
Back: Minimum age: 35 years (Article 58). Term: 5 years (Article 56), though the President continues until the successor takes charge.

Card 17
Front: Which writ literally means "to have the body of", and against whom can it be issued?
Back: Habeas Corpus. It can be issued against both the State and private individuals to produce a detained person before the court and test the legality of detention.

Card 18
Front: Examiner's trap: Are Fundamental Rights available only against the State, or also against private persons?
Back: Mostly against the State (Article 12), but some apply against private persons too — e.g., Article 17 (abolition of untouchability), Article 23 (prohibition of human trafficking and forced labour), and Article 15(2). Trap options claim "all FRs apply only against the State."

Card 19
Front: Which Articles deal with the office of the Vice-President, and what is the Vice-President's ex-officio role?
Back: Articles 63–71. The Vice-President is the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Article 64) and acts as President during a vacancy (maximum 6 months until a new President is elected).

Card 20
Front: Trick card: How to remember the sources of the Indian Constitution's key features?
Back: "UK-Parliament, USA-FR & Judiciary, Ireland-DPSP, USSR-Duties, Germany-Emergency, Canada-Federation, Australia-Concurrent List." Hook: "Uncle Sam's Irish friend met a Russian in Germany, Canada and Australia" — each country maps to one borrowed feature.
