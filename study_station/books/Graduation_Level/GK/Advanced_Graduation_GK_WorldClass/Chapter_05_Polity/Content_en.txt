# Indian Polity — The Rulebook of the Republic

## 🪝 The Hook: A Book Written by 299 People

On 9 December 1946, in the middle of a freezing Delhi winter, 207 members gathered in the Constitution Hall for the first sitting of the Constituent Assembly. India was not yet independent. Partition was months away. Yet these men and women sat down to write a rulebook for a nation that did not fully exist yet.

It took them **2 years, 11 months and 18 days**, **11 sessions**, and roughly **₹64 lakh** to produce the longest national constitution in the world — handwritten and illuminated by Nandalal Bose's team at Santiniketan, calligraphed by Prem Behari Narain Raizada, who refused payment and asked only that his name appear on every page.

Why does this matter to you? Because examiners love this story's details — dates, people, numbers — and they twist them mercilessly. This chapter gives you the facts *and* the traps.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Trace the making of the Constitution and identify its borrowed features.
2. Decode the Preamble, Fundamental Rights, and DPSPs — and the conflicts between them.
3. Map the Union Executive, Parliament, and Judiciary with exact articles.
4. Distinguish between similar-sounding bodies (constitutional vs. statutory).
5. Spot the examiner's favourite traps before they spot you.

## 1. Making of the Constitution

**Key milestones:**

- **1934** — M.N. Roy first put forward the idea of a Constituent Assembly.
- **1940** — The **August Offer** first accepted the demand in principle.
- **1942** — Cripps Mission; **1946** — **Cabinet Mission** plan created the actual scheme for the Assembly.
- **9 Dec 1946** — First meeting; **Sachchidananda Sinha** was the *temporary* (interim) president.
- **11 Dec 1946** — **Dr. Rajendra Prasad** elected permanent President; **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** chaired the **Drafting Committee** (set up 29 Aug 1947, 7 members).
- **13 Dec 1946** — Nehru moved the **Objectives Resolution** (later became the Preamble's base).
- **26 Nov 1949** — Constitution **adopted**; **26 Jan 1950** — fully **enforced** (chosen because of Purna Swaraj Day, 26 Jan 1930).

**Borrowed features (a classic exam table):**

| Source | What we borrowed |
|---|---|
| UK | Parliamentary system, Rule of Law, single citizenship, Cabinet system |
| USA | Fundamental Rights, judicial review, impeachment of President, Preamble's spirit |
| Ireland | DPSPs, nomination of Rajya Sabha members, method of electing President |
| Canada | Federation with a strong Centre, residuary powers with Centre |
| Australia | Concurrent List, joint sitting of both Houses |
| Germany (Weimar) | Emergency provisions |
| USSR | Fundamental Duties, Five-Year Plan ideals |
| South Africa | Amendment procedure, election of Rajya Sabha members |
| France | Republic, ideals of liberty-equality-fraternity in the Preamble |
| Japan | Procedure established by law |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Who was the *temporary* president of the Constituent Assembly, and who chaired the Drafting Committee? From which country did we borrow the Concurrent List?

> 🧠 **Mnemonic 1 — "U-I-C-A-G" for borrowed features:** **U**K (Parliament), **I**reland (DPSP), **C**anada (strong-Centre federation), **A**ustralia (Concurrent List), **G**ermany (Emergency). Say it as "You-I-See-A-Government."

## 2. The Preamble — The Identity Card

The Preamble declares India a **Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic** and secures **Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity**.

- The words **"Socialist", "Secular", and "Integrity"** were added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976** (the "Mini-Constitution").
- In **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**, the Supreme Court held the Preamble is **part of the Constitution** — and laid down the **Basic Structure doctrine**: Parliament can amend anything *except* the basic structure.
- The Preamble is **not enforceable** in courts (it is non-justiciable).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which amendment added "Secular" to the Preamble? Is the Preamble justiciable?

## 3. Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

Originally **seven** rights; now **six** — the Right to Property was removed by the **44th Amendment, 1978** and made a legal right under **Article 300A**.

| Right | Articles |
|---|---|
| Right to Equality | 14–18 |
| Right to Freedom | 19–22 |
| Right against Exploitation | 23–24 |
| Right to Freedom of Religion | 25–28 |
| Cultural & Educational Rights | 29–30 |
| Constitutional Remedies | 32 |

- **Article 32** — the "heart and soul" of the Constitution (Ambedkar). Writs: **Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto**.
- **Article 21** — Right to Life; expanded by courts to include privacy (**Puttaswamy judgment, 2017**), clean environment, education (later Article 21A, 86th Amendment 2002).
- **Article 20** — protection against ex-post-facto law, double jeopardy, self-incrimination.

> 🧠 **Mnemonic 2 — Writs: "H-M-P-C-Q"** — "**H**e **M**ust **P**lease **C**ourt **Q**uickly": Habeas Corpus (produce the body), Mandamus (command to a public official), Prohibition (higher court stops lower court *before* judgment), Certiorari (quashes a lower court's order *after* judgment), Quo Warranto (by what authority do you hold office?).

**Fundamental Duties** — Article 51A, Part IVA, added by the **42nd Amendment (1976)** on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation; originally 10, now **11** (the 11th — education of one's child — added by the 86th Amendment, 2002). They are **non-justiciable**.

**DPSPs** — Part IV, Articles 36–51; borrowed from Ireland; non-justiciable but "fundamental in the governance of the country" (Article 37). Key ones: Article 40 (village panchayats), Article 44 (Uniform Civil Code), Article 48A (environment — added by 42nd Amendment).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which article makes DPSPs non-enforceable yet fundamental in governance? How many Fundamental Duties exist today?

## 4. The Union Executive & Parliament

**President (Articles 52–62):**
- Elected indirectly by an **Electoral College** = elected MPs + elected MLAs of states and UTs of Delhi & Puducherry (Article 54).
- **Impeachment** (Article 61) for "violation of the Constitution" — the only ground; quasi-judicial process.
- **Article 72** — pardoning power, including death sentences (Governor under Article 161 *cannot* pardon death sentences, only commute/suspend — a classic trap).
- **Article 74** — Council of Ministers headed by the PM to aid and advise; the **42nd Amendment** made advice binding, the **44th** allowed one reconsideration.

**Parliament (Articles 79–122):**
- **Lok Sabha** — maximum strength 552 (as originally fixed; currently 543 elected seats after Anglo-Indian nomination was abolished by the **104th Amendment, 2020**). Term 5 years; minimum age 25.
- **Rajya Sabha** — maximum 250 (238 elected + 12 nominated); permanent body, one-third retire every 2 years; minimum age 30.
- **Money Bill** (Article 110) — only Lok Sabha; Speaker's certification is final; Rajya Sabha can only suggest within 14 days.
- **Joint sitting** (Article 108) — summoned by the President, presided over by the **Speaker**; NOT applicable to Money Bills or Constitutional Amendment Bills.
- **Article 123** — President's ordinance power; maximum life = 6 months + 6 weeks.

**Judiciary:**
- Supreme Court (Article 124) — established 28 Jan 1950; originally 8 judges, now **34** (1 + 33, since 2019).
- Judges appointed by the President through the **Collegium system** (evolved via the Three Judges Cases, 1981/1993/1998) — the **NJAC (99th Amendment)** was struck down in 2015.
- High Courts (Article 214); **Article 226** writ power is *wider* than Article 32 (can be issued for legal rights too, not just FRs).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Who presides over a joint sitting? Can a Governor pardon a death sentence? Which is wider — Article 32 or Article 226?

## 5. Federal Structure, Emergency & Amendments

- **Three Lists** (7th Schedule): Union (100 entries), State (61), Concurrent (52). Residuary powers → Centre (Article 248).
- **42nd Amendment (1976)** moved 5 subjects (including **education** and **forests**) from State to Concurrent List.
- **Emergency provisions (Part XVIII):**
  - **National Emergency (Article 352)** — proclaimed thrice: 1962 (China war), 1971 (Bangladesh war), 1975 (internal disturbance). "Armed rebellion" replaced "internal disturbance" via the 44th Amendment.
  - **President's Rule (Article 356)** — maximum 3 years with parliamentary approval every 6 months; **S.R. Bommai case (1994)** made it subject to judicial review.
  - **Financial Emergency (Article 360)** — **never** proclaimed.
- **Amendment (Article 368):** three routes — simple majority (outside 368), special majority, special majority + ratification by half the states (e.g., election of President, distribution of legislative powers).

**Local self-government:** **73rd Amendment (1992)** → Panchayati Raj (Part IX, 11th Schedule, 29 subjects); **74th Amendment (1992)** → Municipalities (Part IXA, 12th Schedule, 18 subjects). Both gave constitutional status and 5-year terms.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which amendment shifted education to the Concurrent List? How many times has Financial Emergency been declared?

## 6. Constitutional vs. Non-Constitutional Bodies

| Constitutional (find the article) | Statutory / Executive |
|---|---|
| Election Commission (Art. 324) | NITI Aayog (executive resolution, 2015) |
| UPSC (Art. 315) | CBI (DSPE Act, 1946) |
| Finance Commission (Art. 280) | NHRC (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993) |
| CAG (Art. 148) | Lokpal (Lokpal Act, 2013) |
| Attorney General (Art. 76) | Central Vigilance Commission (statutory since 2003) |
| SC/ST Commissions (Art. 338, 338A) | NITI Aayog replaced Planning Commission (1950, executive) |

> 🧠 **Mnemonic 3 — "E-U-F-C-A" are constitutional:** **E**lection Commission, **U**PSC, **F**inance Commission, **C**AG, **A**ttorney General. If a body was born by an Act of Parliament or a Cabinet resolution, it is *not* constitutional — no matter how powerful it looks.

## 🔗 Static–Dynamic Link

- The **106th Amendment (2023)** — the *Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam* — reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies, to take effect after the next delimitation and census. Connect it with Article 330A/332A.
- The **104th Amendment (2020)** ended the 70-year practice of nominating Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha and extended SC/ST seat reservation in legislatures for 10 more years.
- The **101st Amendment (2016)** created the GST Council (Article 279A) — a favourite "which amendment?" question.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — "लगभग सही कथन" (Almost-true statement):**
*"The Governor can pardon a death sentence under Article 161."* — 90% true (Governor does have pardoning power), but the killer detail: a Governor **cannot pardon death sentences** — only the President can (Article 72). The Governor may only suspend, remit or commute them.

**Trap 2 — "कालानुक्रमिक भ्रम" (Chronological confusion):**
Questions swap **26 November 1949** (adoption) with **26 January 1950** (enforcement), or Sachchidananda Sinha (temporary president) with Dr. Rajendra Prasad (permanent president). Always ask: *adopted or enforced? temporary or permanent?*

**Trap 3 — "नकारात्मक वाक्यांश" (Hidden negative):**
*"Which of the following is NOT a constitutional body?"* — options: Election Commission, Finance Commission, NITI Aayog, UPSC. Students who skim miss the "NOT" and pick the first familiar name. NITI Aayog (executive resolution, 2015) is the answer. Underline every NOT/EXCEPT in the question paper.
