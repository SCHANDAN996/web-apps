# 🧒 The Feynman Corner — Physics in Everyday Life

## The Most Confusing Concept: Why Does a Pressure Cooker Cook Faster?

Most students memorise "pressure cooker increases pressure, so food cooks faster" — but when the examiner asks *why*, they freeze. Let's fix that forever.

### Explain it like you're 12

Imagine water is a crowd of people trying to leave a cinema hall (boiling = escaping as steam). The doors open at 100°C — that's the "exit temperature" at normal air pressure.

Now, a pressure cooker **locks the doors and pushes back** on the crowd. The steam can't escape, so pressure inside builds up. When pressure goes up, water refuses to boil at 100°C — it now needs about **120°C** before it can turn into steam.

So inside the cooker, the *liquid water itself* gets hotter than 100°C. Hotter water = faster cooking. That's it. The food isn't cooked "by pressure" — it's cooked by **hotter-than-normal boiling water**.

**The chain to remember:**
Lid sealed → steam trapped → pressure rises → boiling point rises (100°C → ~120°C) → water gets hotter → food cooks faster.

### The daily-life proof you already know

- On **Mount Everest**, air pressure is low, so water boils at about **70°C**. You can boil an egg forever and it stays half-raw — the water simply isn't hot enough. This is the *reverse* of the pressure cooker, and examiners love pairing the two.
- A **whistle** blows when pressure crosses the safety valve's limit — proof that pressure really is building inside.

### The Unforgettable Trick 🪄

> **"Pahaad par pakana mushkil, Cooker mein aasaan"**
> (On mountains, cooking is hard; in a cooker, it's easy.)
> High place → low pressure → low boiling point → slow cooking.
> Cooker → high pressure → high boiling point → fast cooking.
> **Pressure and boiling point always move TOGETHER.**

### Examiner's Trap Alert ⚠️

- ❌ "Food cooks faster because pressure directly cooks it" — WRONG. It's the higher temperature of the water.
- ❌ "Boiling point decreases inside a pressure cooker" — WRONG. It *increases*.
- ❌ "Water boils at 100°C everywhere" — WRONG. Boiling point depends on pressure; it's below 100°C at high altitudes.
