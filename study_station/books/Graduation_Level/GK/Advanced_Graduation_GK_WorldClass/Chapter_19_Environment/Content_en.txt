# Environment & Ecology (Basic)

## 🌱 The Hook: A Lake That Caught Fire — Twice

In 2015 and again in 2017, Bengaluru's Bellandur Lake literally caught fire. A lake — made of water — burning. The cause? Decades of untreated sewage and industrial effluents created a thick layer of froth rich in methane and chemicals, and a single spark set it ablaze. This is not science fiction; it is what happens when an **ecosystem's carrying capacity** is crossed. Every year, examiners at SSC, Banking, and State PCS exams ask questions rooted in exactly these fundamentals — food chains, biomes, nutrient cycles, protected areas — because a candidate who understands ecology understands how India (and the planet) actually functions.

Master this chapter, and the ecology questions in almost every GK paper become free marks.

## 1. What is Ecology? The Science of Relationships

The word **Ecology** comes from two Greek words: *Oikos* (house/habitat) + *Logos* (study). It is the scientific study of the **relationships between organisms and their environment**.

- The term was coined by German biologist **Ernst Haeckel in 1866**.
- **Eugene Odum** is called the "Father of Modern Ecology" and wrote the classic textbook *Fundamentals of Ecology* (1953).
- In India, **Ramdeo Misra** is regarded as the "Father of Indian Ecology."

**Levels of ecological organisation** (smallest → largest):
**Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biome → Biosphere**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Who coined the term "ecology" and in which year? Arrange the six levels of organisation from smallest to largest without looking.

## 2. Habitat vs Niche — The Classic Confusion

- **Habitat** = the *address* of an organism (where it lives). Example: a pond is the habitat of a fish.
- **Niche** = the *profession* of an organism (its functional role — what it eats, how it reproduces, how it interacts). Example: the fish's niche includes eating algae and being prey for herons.

> **Memory Hook:** *Habitat = Home address; Niche = Job description.* Two species can share a habitat, but no two species can permanently occupy the **same niche** — this is **Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle**.

## 3. The Ecosystem: Structure and Function

An **ecosystem** is a self-sustaining unit where living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) components interact. The term was coined by **A.G. Tansley in 1935**.

**Abiotic components:** sunlight, temperature, water, soil, minerals, air.
**Biotic components:**

| Component | Role | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Producers (Autotrophs) | Convert solar energy into food via photosynthesis | Green plants, algae, phytoplankton |
| Consumers (Heterotrophs) | Eat other organisms | Herbivores, carnivores, omnivores |
| Decomposers (Saprotrophs) | Break down dead matter, recycle nutrients | Bacteria, fungi |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Who coined "ecosystem"? Name one biotic and one abiotic component of a pond ecosystem.

## 4. Energy Flow and the 10% Law

Energy flows **one way only** through an ecosystem: Sun → Producers → Herbivores → Carnivores. It is never recycled (unlike nutrients).

**The 10% Law (Lindeman, 1942):** Only about **10% of energy** is transferred from one trophic level to the next; the remaining ~90% is lost as heat, respiration, and waste.

Example: 10,000 J captured by grass → 1,000 J to deer → 100 J to tiger.

This is why food chains rarely exceed **4–5 trophic levels** — there simply isn't enough energy left at the top.

**Ecological Pyramids:**
- **Pyramid of Energy** — *always upright* (energy decreases at every level). Never inverted. This is a favourite exam fact.
- **Pyramid of Numbers** — can be inverted (e.g., one tree supporting thousands of insects).
- **Pyramid of Biomass** — upright on land, but **inverted in oceans/ponds** (phytoplankton reproduce so fast that their standing biomass can be less than that of zooplankton at a given moment).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which ecological pyramid is ALWAYS upright? Which one is inverted in aquatic ecosystems? State the 10% law in one sentence.

## 5. Food Chains and Food Webs

- **Food chain:** a linear sequence of who-eats-whom. Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle.
- **Food web:** many interconnected food chains — far more realistic, and it gives an ecosystem **stability**.

**Two types of food chains:**
1. **Grazing Food Chain (GFC):** starts from living green plants. Grass → Deer → Tiger.
2. **Detritus Food Chain (DFC):** starts from dead organic matter. Dead leaves → Bacteria/Fungi → Earthworms → Birds. In forests, the DFC carries *more* energy than the GFC.

**Trophic levels:** T1 = producers, T2 = primary consumers (herbivores), T3 = secondary consumers (carnivores), T4 = tertiary consumers (top carnivores).

**Biomagnification:** Non-biodegradable toxins (DDT, mercury) become *more concentrated* at higher trophic levels. Top predators (eagles, humans) accumulate the most. This is why DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey.

> **Mnemonic #1 — "GFC vs DFC":** **G**razing = **G**reen (living) start; **D**etritus = **D**ead start. G for Green, D for Dead.

## 6. Biogeochemical Cycles: Nature's Recycling System

Unlike energy, **nutrients are recycled**. Key cycles:

**Carbon Cycle:** Carbon moves via photosynthesis (in) and respiration, decomposition, combustion (out). Oceans are the largest carbon reservoir; CO₂ is the main greenhouse gas.

**Nitrogen Cycle:** Atmosphere is ~78% nitrogen, but plants can't use it directly. Steps:
1. **Nitrogen fixation** — by bacteria (*Rhizobium* in legume root nodules, *Azotobacter*, blue-green algae) and lightning.
2. **Nitrification** — ammonia → nitrites (*Nitrosomonas*) → nitrates (*Nitrobacter*).
3. **Assimilation** — plants absorb nitrates.
4. **Denitrification** — *Pseudomonas* converts nitrates back to N₂ gas.

> **Mnemonic #2 — Nitrification order:** "**N**ever **S**kip **N**ice **B**iryani" → **N**itro**s**omonas first, **N**itro**b**acter second. (S comes before B — nitrites before nitrates.)

**Phosphorus Cycle:** A **sedimentary cycle** — no gaseous phase. Rocks weather → phosphate enters soil/water → plants → animals → back to sediments. Excess phosphorus in water bodies causes **eutrophication** (algal blooms → oxygen depletion → fish death).

**Water (Hydrological) Cycle:** Evaporation → Condensation → Precipitation → Runoff/Infiltration. Transpiration from plants adds water vapour.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which cycle has NO gaseous phase? Which bacteria convert ammonia to nitrites? What is eutrophication?

## 7. Ecological Succession: How Nature Heals

**Succession** is the gradual, predictable replacement of one community by another until a stable **climax community** forms.

- **Primary succession:** begins on bare rock/lava/sand where no soil existed (very slow — hundreds of years). Pioneer species: **lichens and mosses**.
- **Secondary succession:** begins where soil already exists after disturbance (fire, flood, abandoned farmland) — much faster.
- **Hydrosere:** succession in water (pond → marsh → forest).
- **Xerosere:** succession on dry rock (rock → lichen → moss → herbs → shrubs → forest).

## 8. Biomes and India's Major Ecosystems

A **biome** is a large regional ecosystem defined by climate and dominant vegetation. Major world biomes:

| Biome | Climate | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical Rainforest | Hot, wet year-round | Highest biodiversity (e.g., Amazon, Western Ghats) |
| Tropical Grassland (Savanna) | Seasonal rain | Grasses + scattered trees; large herbivores |
| Temperate Grassland | Cold winters | Steppes, Prairies, Pampas |
| Desert | Very low rainfall | Hot (Sahara, Thar) and cold (Ladakh) deserts |
| Taiga (Boreal forest) | Long cold winters | Coniferous forests |
| Tundra | Permafrost | No trees; mosses, lichens |

**India's special ecosystems to remember:**
- **Mangroves** (Sundarbans — world's largest mangrove forest) — salt-tolerant trees, natural cyclone barriers.
- **Coral reefs** — Gulf of Mannar, Andaman & Nicobar, Lakshadweep, Gulf of Kutch. Corals are *animals* (cnidarians) living with symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae). Coral bleaching = loss of these algae due to warm water.
- **Wetlands** — Ramsar sites; Chilika Lake (Odisha) and Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur, Rajasthan) were among India's first Ramsar sites, designated in 1981.

## 9. Protected Area Network of India

| Category | Established under | Human activity | Notified by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **National Park** | Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 | Strictly restricted | State Government |
| **Wildlife Sanctuary** | Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 | Some activities permitted | State Government |
| **Biosphere Reserve** | UNESCO's MAB Programme (1971) | Zoned: core → buffer → transition | Central Government |
| **Conservation Reserve / Community Reserve** | WPA Amendment 2002 | Community participation | State Government |

**Key facts:**
- India's **first National Park: Jim Corbett National Park** (established 1936 as *Hailey* National Park, Uttarakhand).
- **Project Tiger** launched in **1973** (initially 9 tiger reserves).
- **Project Elephant** launched in **1992**.
- Biosphere reserves have **three zones**: Core (no human activity), Buffer (research, limited tourism), Transition (settlements, agriculture). India's first biosphere reserve: **Nilgiri (1986)**.

> **Mnemonic #3 — Biosphere zones:** "**C**ats **B**ite **T**igers" → **C**ore, **B**uffer, **T**ransition (inside → outside).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which was India's first National Park and when was it established? What are the three zones of a biosphere reserve? In which year was Project Tiger launched?

## 10. Biodiversity: Levels and Hotspots

**Biodiversity** exists at three levels: **Genetic → Species → Ecosystem**.

A **biodiversity hotspot** (concept by **Norman Myers**, 1988) must have:
1. At least **1,500 endemic vascular plant species**, AND
2. Lost at least **70% of its original habitat**.

**India has 4 biodiversity hotspots:**
1. **Himalaya** (Eastern Himalaya region)
2. **Indo-Burma** (North-East India)
3. **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**
4. **Sundaland** (Nicobar Islands)

> **Mnemonic #4 — India's hotspots:** "**H**e **I**s **W**earing **S**andals" → **H**imalaya, **I**ndo-Burma, **W**estern Ghats, **S**undaland.

**Red Data Book:** Published by **IUCN** (founded 1948, HQ Gland, Switzerland). Categories: Extinct → Extinct in the Wild → Critically Endangered → Endangered → Vulnerable → Near Threatened → Least Concern.

## 11. Key Environmental Laws and Events (Static Anchors)

- **Wildlife Protection Act:** 1972
- **Water (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act:** 1974
- **Air (Prevention & Control of Pollution) Act:** 1981
- **Environment (Protection) Act:** 1986 (after Bhopal Gas Tragedy, 1984)
- **Chipko Movement:** 1973, Gaura Devi / Sunderlal Bahuguna, Uttarakhand (then UP)
- **Stockholm Conference (UN Conference on Human Environment):** 1972 — first global environment conference; June 5 declared **World Environment Day**
- **Rio Earth Summit:** 1992 — gave birth to UNFCCC, CBD, Agenda 21
- **Montreal Protocol:** 1987 — ozone layer (CFC phase-out); considered the most successful environmental treaty
- **Kyoto Protocol:** 1997 (in force 2005) — binding emission cuts for developed nations
- **Paris Agreement:** 2015 — limit warming well below 2°C, pursue 1.5°C

## 🔗 Static-Dynamic Link

The **Paris Agreement (2015)** is static treaty knowledge, but it stays in the news: countries submit updated **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)** and COP summits are held every year. When you revise the treaty table, connect it to the latest COP outcome from your current-affairs source — examiners love pairing a static treaty fact with a recent summit.

Similarly, **Ramsar sites** (static concept, 1971 convention) keep growing in number in India — always check the latest count from current affairs rather than memorising a fixed number.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — "लगभग सही कथन" (Almost-Correct Statement):**
*"The pyramid of biomass is always upright in all ecosystems."* — Sounds 90% right (it IS upright on land), but it is **inverted in aquatic ecosystems**. Examiners insert one word ("always," "all," "only") to flip a true statement into a false one. **Rule: treat absolute words (always/never/all/only) as red flags.**

**Trap 2 — "समान नाम/वर्ष" (Similar Names/Years):**
Confusing **Montreal Protocol (1987 — ozone)** with **Kyoto Protocol (1997 — climate)**; or **Stockholm 1972** with **Rio 1992**. Both are "protocols"/"summits" with similar-sounding years. **Rule: anchor each treaty to its SUBJECT (ozone vs climate), not just the year.**

**Trap 3 — "विपरीत कार्य-कारण" (Reverse Causation):**
*"Eutrophication is caused by oxygen depletion in water."* — Reversed! **Nutrient overload (N, P) → algal bloom → decomposition → oxygen depletion.** Oxygen depletion is the *effect*, not the cause. **Rule: in cause-effect statements, ask "which came first?"**
