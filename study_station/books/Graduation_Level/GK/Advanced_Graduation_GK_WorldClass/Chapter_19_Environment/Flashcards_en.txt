Card 1
Front: What is the difference between a food chain and a food web?
Back: A food chain is a single linear sequence of who-eats-whom; a food web is the network of many interlinked food chains. Real ecosystems are food webs — one species usually eats and is eaten by several others.

Card 2
Front: Why does energy decrease at each trophic level, and by how much?
Back: Only about 10% of energy is transferred to the next trophic level (Lindeman's 10% law); the rest is lost as heat, respiration, and waste. This is why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 levels.

Card 3
Front: Which ecological pyramid can never be inverted?
Back: The pyramid of energy — it is always upright because energy flow is unidirectional and decreases at every level. Pyramids of number and biomass can be inverted (e.g., parasites on a tree; phytoplankton vs. zooplankton in a pond).

Card 4
Front: What is the difference between a biome and an ecosystem?
Back: A biome is a large regional unit defined by climate and dominant vegetation (e.g., tundra, tropical rainforest); an ecosystem is a functional unit of living organisms interacting with their environment, and can be any size — even a pond.

Card 5
Front: Who coined the term "ecosystem" and who coined "ecology"?
Back: "Ecosystem" — A.G. Tansley (1935). "Ecology" — Ernst Haeckel (1866). Examiners love swapping these two names.

Card 6
Front: What is the difference between a detritus food chain and a grazing food chain?
Back: Grazing food chain starts from living green plants (grass → deer → tiger); detritus food chain starts from dead organic matter (leaf litter → bacteria/fungi → detritivores). In forests, the detritus chain dominates.

Card 7
Front: What is biomagnification, and which pollutant is its classic example?
Back: The increasing concentration of a non-degradable substance (like DDT or mercury) at successively higher trophic levels. Classic example: DDT accumulating in fish-eating birds, thinning their eggshells.

Card 8
Front: What is the difference between eutrophication and biomagnification?
Back: Eutrophication = nutrient enrichment (nitrates/phosphates) of a water body causing algal blooms and oxygen depletion. Biomagnification = toxin concentration rising up the food chain. Examiners frequently interchange these in options.

Card 9
Front: What is an ecological niche, and how does it differ from habitat?
Back: Habitat is the "address" — where an organism lives. Niche is the "profession" — its functional role: what it eats, when it's active, how it interacts. Two species cannot indefinitely share the exact same niche (competitive exclusion).

Card 10
Front: What are the stages of ecological succession on bare rock?
Back: Lichens (pioneers) → mosses → herbs → shrubs → trees (climax community). This is xerarch succession. Succession beginning in water is hydrarch.

Card 11
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you remember the order of succession on bare rock?
Back: "Lions Make Huge Strong Tigers" — Lichens → Mosses → Herbs → Shrubs → Trees. Pioneer species are always lichens on rock.

Card 12
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you remember the four main biogeochemical cycles asked in exams?
Back: "CHON" — Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen, Nitrogen (plus Phosphorus and Sulphur as sedimentary cycles). Remember: the phosphorus cycle has NO gaseous phase — a favourite trap.

Card 13
Front: Examiner's trap: "The phosphorus cycle has a significant atmospheric (gaseous) component." True or false?
Back: False. Phosphorus cycle is purely sedimentary — it moves through rocks, soil, water, and organisms, with no gaseous phase. Carbon and nitrogen cycles are gaseous-type cycles.

Card 14
Front: Examiner's trap: "Decomposers occupy the top trophic level of the food chain." True or false?
Back: False. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) act on dead matter from ALL trophic levels; they are not assigned a single trophic level. They recycle nutrients back to the soil.

Card 15
Front: What is the difference between producers, consumers, and decomposers in terms of nutrition?
Back: Producers (autotrophs) make food via photosynthesis; consumers (heterotrophs) eat other organisms; decomposers (saprotrophs) break down dead organic matter and release inorganic nutrients.

Card 16
Front: What is an ecotone, and what is the edge effect?
Back: An ecotone is a transition zone between two ecosystems (e.g., mangrove between land and sea). The edge effect is the tendency of ecotones to have greater species diversity and density than either adjoining ecosystem.

Card 17
Front: What is the difference between primary and secondary succession?
Back: Primary succession starts on a bare surface with no soil (new rock, lava flow) — very slow. Secondary succession starts where soil already exists after a disturbance (abandoned farm, burnt forest) — much faster.

Card 18
Front: Which gas is the largest contributor to the natural greenhouse effect, and which human-added gas is most targeted by climate policy?
Back: Water vapour is the largest natural greenhouse gas; CO₂ is the main anthropogenic gas targeted by policy. Methane (CH₄) is ~25 times more potent than CO₂ per molecule over 100 years.

Card 19
Front: What is the ozone hole, and which chemicals cause it?
Back: Severe depletion of stratospheric ozone over Antarctica, caused mainly by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) releasing chlorine radicals that destroy O₃. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs — the most successful environmental treaty.

Card 20
Front: What is the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conservation?
Back: In-situ = conserving species in their natural habitat (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves). Ex-situ = conservation outside natural habitat (zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks).
