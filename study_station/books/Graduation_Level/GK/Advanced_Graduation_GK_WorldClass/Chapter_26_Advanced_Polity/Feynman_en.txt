# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Advanced Indian Polity

## The Hardest Concept: The Basic Structure Doctrine (Article 368 vs. Article 13)

Ask any aspirant what confuses them most in Polity, and the answer is usually this: *"Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368… but it cannot amend the 'basic structure'… but nobody wrote down what the basic structure is… so who decides?"* Let's untangle it like you're 12 years old.

---

## 🏠 The House Analogy

Imagine the Constitution is a **house** that belongs to all of us, and Parliament is the **caretaker** we hired.

- The caretaker **can** repaint the walls, change the curtains, add a new room, even build another floor. That's an **amendment under Article 368** — Parliament changing the Constitution by a special majority.
- But the caretaker **cannot** demolish the foundation, remove the load-bearing pillars, or sell the land. If the foundation goes, it's not the same house anymore — it's a pile of bricks.

The **foundation and pillars** of our constitutional house are things like: democracy, secularism, federalism, judicial review, free and fair elections, separation of powers, rule of law. Together, these are the **Basic Structure**.

In **Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)**, a 13-judge bench of the Supreme Court ruled (7–6): *Parliament's power to amend is wide, but not unlimited — it cannot destroy or damage the basic structure of the Constitution.* And who decides what counts as "basic structure"? The **judiciary**, case by case. That's why the list keeps growing — there is no fixed, written list anywhere in the Constitution.

---

## 🎯 The Examiner's Trap (Read Twice)

- The Basic Structure Doctrine came from a **judgment (1973)**, not from the text of the Constitution. If an option says "Article 368 itself bars basic-structure amendments" — it's **wrong**.
- The doctrine applies to **constitutional amendments**, and the Supreme Court later extended its use to test ordinary laws too — but the classic exam answer is: it limits **Article 368**.
- **42nd Amendment (1976)** tried to kill this doctrine by saying "no amendment can be challenged in any court." The Supreme Court struck that down in **Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)**. Trap: exams swap the years/cases — Kesavananda = 1973, Minerva Mills = 1980.

---

## 🪄 The Unforgettable Trick

**"KESHAV built the house, MINERVA guarded it."**

- **KESHAV**ananda Bharati (1973) → doctrine **born**.
- **MINERVA** Mills (1980) → doctrine **protected** when the 42nd Amendment attacked it.

And for what counts as basic structure, remember **"JUDGES FEAR SED"**:
**JU**dicial review, **D**emocracy, **G**overnance by rule of law, **E**lections (free & fair), **S**ecularism, **F**ederalism, **E**quality, **A**… okay, just remember: **S**eparation of powers, **E**ssential features, **D**ignity of the individual. If a feature, when removed, makes the Constitution unrecognizable — it's basic structure.
