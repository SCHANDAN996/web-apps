# Advanced Indian Polity

## The Hook: A Constitution Written by Hand, Tested by Time

On 24 January 1950, in a hall in New Delhi, 284 members of the Constituent Assembly signed a document that had taken **2 years, 11 months and 18 days** to create. Every page of the original Constitution was handwritten by Prem Behari Narain Raizada — not a single word typed. He refused payment, asking only that his name appear on every page, with his grandfather's name on the last.

That document — 395 articles, 8 schedules, roughly 145,000 words — was the longest national constitution ever written. And here is the surprising part: it has survived over 70 years, 100+ amendments, an Emergency, and countless political storms, while many younger constitutions around the world collapsed.

Why? Because the framers built *flexibility into rigidity*. That tension — between what can change and what can never change — is exactly what examiners test you on. Let's master it.

## Part 1: The Basic Structure Doctrine — The Constitution's Immune System

### The Problem

Article 368 gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution. But can Parliament amend *anything* — even abolish democracy itself? In **Shankari Prasad (1951)** and **Sajjan Singh (1965)**, the Supreme Court said yes, amendments are not "law" under Article 13 and cannot be struck down.

Then came **Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)**: the Court reversed itself, holding that Fundamental Rights cannot be amended. Parliament responded with the **24th Amendment (1971)**, explicitly restoring its amending power.

### The Landmark: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

A 13-judge bench (the largest ever) ruled **7:6** that:

- Parliament **can** amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights (overruling Golaknath on this point).
- **BUT** it cannot destroy or damage the **"basic structure"** of the Constitution.

The Court did not define the basic structure exhaustively. Elements recognised across cases include: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, free and fair elections, and rule of law.

### The Doctrine Tested and Strengthened

| Case | Year | What Happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain** | 1975 | Struck down the 39th Amendment's clause shielding the PM's election from judicial review — first application of basic structure |
| **Minerva Mills v. Union of India** | 1980 | Struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment that gave Parliament unlimited amending power and gave DPSPs primacy over all FRs; declared **judicial review** and **harmony between FRs and DPSPs** part of basic structure |
| **Waman Rao** | 1981 | Doctrine applies to amendments made **after 24 April 1973** (date of Kesavananda judgment) |
| **I.R. Coelho** | 2007 | Laws placed in the **Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973** are open to challenge if they violate the basic structure |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. Which case *created* the basic structure doctrine, which case *first applied* it, and which case *extended it to the Ninth Schedule*? Say the three names aloud before reading on.

### 🪄 Mnemonic 1: "KIM-WI" for the doctrine's journey

**K**esavananda (1973 — born) → **I**ndra Gandhi case (1975 — first use) → **M**inerva Mills (1980 — strengthened) → **W**aman Rao (1981 — date fixed) → **I**.R. Coelho (2007 — Ninth Schedule).

## Part 2: Fundamental Rights vs DPSPs — The Great Balancing Act

### The Three Pillars

| Feature | Fundamental Rights (Part III) | DPSPs (Part IV) | Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source of inspiration | USA | Ireland | USSR |
| Justiciable? | Yes (Article 32) | No (Article 37) | No |
| Nature | Negative/positive obligations on State | Positive directions to State | Duties of citizens |
| Added by | Original Constitution | Original Constitution | **42nd Amendment, 1976** (Swaran Singh Committee) |

### The Conflict and Its Resolution

- **Champakam Dorairajan (1951):** FRs prevail over DPSPs.
- **25th Amendment (1971):** Article 31C — laws implementing Articles **39(b) and 39(c)** cannot be voided for violating Articles 14, 19, 31.
- **42nd Amendment (1976):** Expanded Article 31C to *all* DPSPs.
- **Minerva Mills (1980):** Struck down that expansion. The Court held that the **harmony and balance** between FRs and DPSPs is a basic feature — neither has absolute primacy.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which articles does Article 31C protect today — all DPSPs or only 39(b) and 39(c)? Which case decided this?

### Fundamental Duties — The Numbers Game

- Originally **10 duties** added by the 42nd Amendment (1976), on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation.
- The **11th duty** (parent/guardian to provide education opportunities to children aged 6–14) was added by the **86th Amendment, 2002** — the same amendment that made education a Fundamental Right under **Article 21A**.

### 🪄 Mnemonic 2: "42-86-11" chain

**42**nd Amendment → **10** duties; **86**th Amendment → **11**th duty + Article **21A**. Remember: *"42 gave ten, 86 made eleven."*

## Part 3: Amendment Procedure — Three Roads to Change

Article 368 (Part XX) provides three methods:

1. **Simple majority** (outside Article 368): e.g., creation/abolition of Legislative Councils, formation of new states, citizenship, quorum rules, salaries of MPs, Fifth/Sixth Schedule administration.
2. **Special majority** (Article 368): majority of total membership + 2/3 of members present and voting, in each House separately. Covers Fundamental Rights, DPSPs, and most other provisions.
3. **Special majority + ratification by half the states**: for "entrenched" provisions — election of the President, extent of Union/State executive power, Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers, the Seventh Schedule lists, representation of states in Parliament, and **Article 368 itself**.

Key rulings and facts:

- **No joint sitting** for a constitutional amendment bill (Article 108 does not apply). If the two Houses disagree, the bill fails.
- The President **must give assent** to a constitutional amendment bill — cannot withhold or return it (24th Amendment made this explicit).
- A constitutional amendment bill can be introduced in **either House**, by a minister **or a private member**, and does **not** require prior presidential recommendation.

🧠 **Active Recall:** List three provisions that need state ratification. Now ask: does a money bill need ratification? (No — it's not even a constitutional amendment.)

### 🪄 Mnemonic 3: "PEJ-SAR" for ratification-requiring subjects

**P**resident's election, **E**xecutive power (Union/State), **J**udiciary (SC/HCs), **S**eventh Schedule, **A**rticle 368 itself, **R**epresentation of states in Parliament.

## Part 4: Emergency Provisions — The Stress Test

### National Emergency (Article 352)

- Grounds: **war, external aggression, or armed rebellion**. (The 44th Amendment, 1978, replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion".)
- Proclaimed on the **written recommendation of the Cabinet** (not just the PM's advice — a 44th Amendment safeguard added after the 1975 misuse).
- Must be approved by both Houses within **one month** (originally two months; reduced by the 44th Amendment) by a **special majority**.
- Once approved, lasts 6 months at a time, extendable indefinitely with parliamentary approval every 6 months.
- **Effect on Fundamental Rights:** Article 358 automatically suspends Article 19; Article 359 allows the President to suspend *enforcement* of other FRs — **but Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended** (44th Amendment).

### President's Rule (Article 356) and Financial Emergency (Article 360)

- Article 356: failure of constitutional machinery in a state. Maximum **3 years** with parliamentary approval every 6 months; beyond **1 year**, only if a National Emergency is in operation (in whole of India or in that state) **or** the Election Commission certifies that elections cannot be held.
- **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994):** President's Rule is subject to **judicial review**; the proclamation can be struck down and the dismissed government restored. Secularism was affirmed as part of the basic structure here.
- Article 360 (Financial Emergency): approved within **two months** by simple majority; continues indefinitely once approved; **never proclaimed so far**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which two articles can never be suspended even during Emergency? Which amendment created this protection, and which case made Article 356 justiciable?

## Part 5: President vs Governor — The Mirror That Isn't

| Point | President | Governor |
|---|---|---|
| Veto over state bills | — | Can reserve a bill for the President (Article 200) |
| Pocket veto | Yes (no time limit to decide) | Yes, but only until reservation; President then has no time limit |
| Ordinance power | Article 123 | Article 213 |
| Pardoning power (Article 72) | Includes **death sentence** and **court-martial** cases | Article 161 — **cannot** pardon death sentence or court-martial |
| Discretion | Nominal (bound by CoM advice — 42nd/44th Amendments) | Real discretion exists (e.g., Article 356 report, hung assembly, reservation of bills) |
| Term/tenure | 5 years; impeachment under Article 61 | Holds office "during the pleasure of the President"; 5-year term but removable anytime |

**Key case:** *Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)* — the President and Governors are constitutional heads who normally act on ministerial advice, but Governors retain situational discretion.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Name two powers the President has that the Governor does not. (Hint: one involves death, one involves the military.)

## Part 6: The Judiciary — Guardians with Jurisdiction

- **Supreme Court:** Article 124 establishment; judges appointed by the President after consultation (the **Collegium system**, evolved through the Three Judges Cases — 1981, 1993, 1998). The **99th Amendment + NJAC Act (2014)** tried to replace the collegium; struck down in 2015 as violating the basic structure (independence of judiciary).
- **Jurisdictions of the SC:** Original (Art. 131), Writ (Art. 32), Appellate (Arts. 132–136), Advisory (Art. 143).
- **High Courts:** Art. 214; writ jurisdiction under **Article 226** — *wider* than Article 32 because it covers enforcement of FRs **and** "any other purpose" (ordinary legal rights).
- **Writs:** Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
  - **Prohibition** is issued by a higher court to a lower court/tribunal *before* the decision; **Certiorari** *after* the decision, to quash it. Examiners swap these constantly.

### 🪄 Mnemonic 4: Writs — "He Made People Cry Quietly"

**He**abeas Corpus (produce the body), **Ma**ndamus (command to a public official), **P**rohibition (stop before), **C**ertiorari (quash after), **Q**uo Warranto (by what authority?).

## Part 7: Federalism with a Unitary Tilt

- India is a **"Union of States"** (Article 1) — states have no right to secede.
- **Seventh Schedule:** Union List (100 subjects, originally 97), State List (61, originally 66), Concurrent List (52, originally 47). Five subjects moved from State to Concurrent by the **42nd Amendment (1976)**: education, forests, weights & measures, protection of wild animals and birds, administration of justice.
- **Residuary powers** lie with the **Union** (Article 248 + Entry 97, Union List) — unlike the USA, where they lie with the states.
- **Article 3:** Parliament can form new states, alter boundaries/names by **simple majority** — but the bill needs the President's prior recommendation and referral to the affected state legislature (whose opinion is **not binding**).
- **Article 249:** Rajya Sabha, by a 2/3 majority of members present and voting, can empower Parliament to legislate on a State List subject (valid 1 year, extendable).
- **Article 262:** Parliament can adjudicate inter-state river water disputes and bar court jurisdiction (basis of the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which list did "education" originally belong to, and which amendment moved it? Who holds residuary powers in India vs the USA?

## 🔗 Static-Dynamic Link

- **Article 370 (Jammu & Kashmir):** In August 2019, its special provisions were made inoperative and the state was reorganised into two Union Territories (J&K and Ladakh) under Article 3. In December 2023, the Supreme Court upheld the abrogation. Classic example of how a "temporary provision" (Part XXI) became a live exam topic.
- **106th Amendment (2023) — Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam:** reserves one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies; implementation linked to the next census and delimitation. Connects static provisions (Articles 330A, 332A) with current affairs.
- **Delimitation freeze:** The 84th Amendment (2001) froze Lok Sabha/Assembly seat allocation on the **1971 census** until the first census after 2026 — a static fact whose deadline is now approaching.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — "लगभग सही कथन" (Near-Correct Statement):**
*"Article 31C protects laws implementing all Directive Principles from challenge under Articles 14 and 19."*
Sounds 90% right — but after **Minerva Mills (1980)**, Article 31C protects only laws implementing **Articles 39(b) and 39(c)**, not all DPSPs. The 42nd Amendment's expansion was struck down. One word ("all") makes the whole statement false.

**Trap 2 — "कालानुक्रमिक भ्रम" (Chronological Confusion):**
Questions asking you to arrange *Golaknath (1967) → 24th Amendment (1971) → Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980)* in order. Examiners swap Golaknath and Kesavananda, or place the 42nd Amendment before Kesavananda. Fix the chain: **Golaknath said "no FR amendment" → Parliament hit back with the 24th → Kesavananda invented basic structure → 42nd tried to override it → Minerva Mills struck that down.**

**Trap 3 — "नकारात्मक वाक्यांश" (Hidden Negative):**
*"Which of the following is NOT a ground for National Emergency?"* Options: War / External aggression / Armed rebellion / **Internal disturbance**. Students who know the original text pick "armed rebellion" and lose the mark — the **44th Amendment (1978)** replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion". Always underline NOT/EXCEPT, and always ask: *as amended, or as originally framed?*
