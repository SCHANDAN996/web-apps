# Feynman Explains: The Limit — Calculus's Sneakiest Idea

## The Speedometer Puzzle

Imagine you're sitting in a car with your dad. He says, "We drove 120 kilometres in 2 hours, so our speed was 60 km/h."

You nod. Easy. Distance ÷ time = speed.

But then you glance at the speedometer. It says **73 km/h**. Wait — how can the needle show a speed *right now*? Speed needs distance and time. At a single frozen instant, the car travels **zero** distance in **zero** time. Zero divided by zero is... nothing. Meaningless. So how does the needle know?!

That puzzle stumped the smartest humans for 2,000 years. And the answer — the trick that cracked it — is the entire heart of calculus. It's called a **limit**.

## The Shrinking Stopwatch

Here's how a 12-year-old detective (that's you) solves it.

You can't measure speed at one instant. Fine. So cheat a little: measure over a **tiny** interval instead.

- From 2:00:00 to 2:00:10 — the car went 200 metres. Speed ≈ 72 km/h.
- From 2:00:00 to 2:00:01 — it went 20.05 metres. Speed ≈ 72.2 km/h.
- From 2:00:00 to 2:00:00.1 — speed ≈ 72.98 km/h.

See the pattern? As your stopwatch interval shrinks — 10 seconds, 1 second, a tenth of a second — the answer **homes in** on one number: 73. You never actually divide by zero. You just sneak closer and closer, and the answer settles down like a cricket ball landing in a fielder's hands.

That settling-down number is the **limit**. And "speed at one instant" — the derivative — is just a limit wearing a fancy hat.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "The Ghost of Zero"

This is the image to lock in your brain forever:

> **A limit is a ghost.** You never let the interval actually *become* zero — zero is a ghost you can chase but never touch. But you get so close that the answer stops running away and shows its face.

Whenever you see **lim (h → 0)**, just whisper: *"Chase the ghost, don't catch it."*

- **Derivative** = ghost-chasing to find *how fast* something changes (slope, speed).
- **Integral** = the reverse trick: chopping a weird shape into a million thin strips, adding them up, and letting the strips shrink to ghost-thinness to get the *exact* area.

Same ghost. Two directions. That's all of calculus.

## Why the Ghost Matters

Without limits, "instantaneous speed," "steepest point of a curve," and "area under a curve" would all be nonsense phrases. With limits, they become exact numbers — and suddenly we can build bridges, launch rockets, and price bank loans. Newton and Leibniz didn't invent new arithmetic; they just taught the world how to chase a ghost *correctly*.

## Your Turn — One Question

A ball drops from a tower. In the first second it falls 5 metres, in the next second 15 metres more. Your friend says, "At exactly t = 1 second, the ball's speed is 5 m/s, because that's how far it fell in the first second."

Using the ghost trick — is your friend right, wrong, or *almost* right? And how would you shrink the stopwatch to find the true answer?

*(Hint: the true speed at t = 1 is 10 m/s. Can you see why the shrinking intervals would land there?)*
