# The Feynman Explanation: Time & Distance

## The Hardest Concept: Relative Speed (Why Two Trains "Feel" Faster)

Ask any topper what trips students up in Time & Distance, and they'll say the same thing: **relative speed**. Not the formula — the *feeling* of it. Why do we add speeds when two trains come towards each other? Why subtract when they chase? Let's fix that forever.

---

## 🚂 The Story of Two Lazy Trains

Imagine you're 12 again, standing on a railway platform with your friend Meena. Two toy trains are on the same track, 100 metres apart.

- Train A crawls towards Train B at **2 metres every second**.
- Train B crawls towards Train A at **3 metres every second**.

Now here's the magic question: *how fast is the gap shrinking?*

Don't touch a formula. Just watch one second of the movie:

- In 1 second, Train A eats up 2 metres of the gap.
- In the same 1 second, Train B eats up 3 metres of the gap.
- Together, the gap shrank by **5 metres in 1 second**.

So the 100-metre gap disappears in 100 ÷ 5 = **20 seconds**. That's it. You just used relative speed without knowing its name!

**When two things move towards each other, both are working to destroy the gap — so their speeds ADD UP.** They're teammates.

---

## 🏃 Now Flip the Story: The Chase

Same platform. Now Meena's dog runs away from her at 3 m/s, and Meena chases it at 5 m/s. The dog has a 20-metre head start.

Watch one second again:

- Meena covers 5 metres.
- But the dog also runs 3 metres ahead — the thief keeps stealing back the gap!
- Net gain for Meena: only **2 metres per second**.

So a 20-metre gap closes in 20 ÷ 2 = **10 seconds**.

**When one thing chases another in the same direction, the front one keeps rebuilding the gap — so speeds SUBTRACT.** They're opponents.

---

## 🧠 The Unforgettable Trick: "FRIENDS ADD, THIEVES SUBTRACT"

> **Coming together = FRIENDS = ADD the speeds.**
> **Chasing = a THIEF running away = SUBTRACT the speeds.**

Say it out loud three times. Every train-crossing, boat-chase, or police-thief question in your exam is secretly just this playground story wearing a disguise.

And the "length of the train" part? That's just the size of the gap. A train crossing a pole has a gap equal to its own length (a pole is a point — it has no length to add). A train crossing a platform has a gap of *train length + platform length*, because the train's tail must clear the far end. Same story, bigger gap.

---

## ⚡ One More Feynman Secret: The Average Speed Trap

Here's where even graduates slip. If you travel to college at 30 km/h and return at 60 km/h, what's your average speed for the whole trip?

Your gut screams **45**. Your gut is wrong.

Why? Because you spend **more time** crawling at 30 than zooming at 60 — the slow speed gets more "voting time." Average speed is *total distance ÷ total time*, not the average of speeds.

Try it with a 60 km one-way trip: 2 hours going + 1 hour returning = 120 km in 3 hours = **40 km/h**. Always less than 45, always pulled towards the slower speed.

> **Trick: "Average speed loves the slow lane."** For equal distances, use 2ab/(a+b) — here, 2×30×60/90 = 40. ✔

## ❓ Your Turn (The Feynman Test)

Feynman said: *"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it."* So prove you understand:

**Two trains, 120 m and 180 m long, run towards each other at 40 km/h and 50 km/h. How long do they take to cross each other completely?**

Solve it using the story, not the formula: Are they friends or thieves? What's the total gap (both lengths)? Convert km/h to m/s (multiply by 5/18). If you can explain your answer to a 12-year-old — congratulations, the concept is yours forever.
