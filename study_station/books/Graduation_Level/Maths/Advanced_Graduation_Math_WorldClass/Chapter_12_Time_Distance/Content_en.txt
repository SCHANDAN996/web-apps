# Time & Distance

## Chapter Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Convert speeds between km/h and m/s in under 3 seconds.
2. Solve problems on trains, boats & streams, and races without writing long equations.
3. Handle relative speed, average speed, and circular track questions confidently.
4. Spot the traps examiners set in SSC CGL Tier-II, IBPS PO Mains, CAT, and CSAT papers.

---

## The Hook: A Train, A Pole, and 9 Seconds

A 180-metre-long train crosses a pole in 9 seconds. That's it — that's the whole question. And from this one line, the examiner expects you to find the train's speed in km/h.

Most students freeze. Not because the maths is hard, but because nobody told them the secret: **when a train crosses a pole, it travels a distance equal to its own length.** That's all. 180 metres in 9 seconds = 20 m/s = 72 km/h. Done in 10 seconds flat.

Time & Distance is not a chapter of formulas. It is a chapter of *one* formula wearing a hundred disguises. Once you see through the disguises, this becomes the highest-scoring topic in your quant paper.

---

## Why This Chapter Feels Hard (And Why It Isn't)

Let's be honest. You've probably memorised "Speed = Distance/Time" a dozen times, and still blanked out in the exam hall when the question said *"a man covers two-thirds of the journey at 4 km/h and the rest at 5 km/h..."*

The struggle is real, and here's why: school taught you the formula, but exams test the **relationships** — what happens to time when speed doubles, what "relative" really means when two objects move, why average speed is *not* the average of speeds. This chapter fixes exactly that.

---

## Chunk 1: The One Formula and Its Three Faces

Everything in this chapter comes from:

**Speed = Distance ÷ Time**

Rearranged: **Distance = Speed × Time** and **Time = Distance ÷ Speed**

But the real power lies in the proportions:

- **Speed constant** → Distance ∝ Time (double the time, double the distance)
- **Time constant** → Distance ∝ Speed
- **Distance constant** → Speed ∝ 1/Time (double the speed, *half* the time)

That last one is the examiner's favourite. If a man increases his speed in the ratio 5:6, his time ratio becomes 6:5. No calculation needed — just flip the ratio.

🧠 **Active Recall:** A car covers a fixed distance. If its speed becomes 3/4 of the original, what happens to the time taken? *(Answer: time becomes 4/3 of the original — flip the ratio.)*

---

## Chunk 2: Unit Conversion — The 5/18 Reflex

Examiners love mixing km/h and m/s. Make this conversion a reflex:

- **km/h → m/s: multiply by 5/18**
- **m/s → km/h: multiply by 18/5**

Why 5/18? Because 1 km = 1000 m and 1 hour = 3600 s, so 1 km/h = 1000/3600 = 5/18 m/s.

Quick anchors to memorise: 36 km/h = 10 m/s, 54 km/h = 15 m/s, 72 km/h = 20 m/s, 90 km/h = 25 m/s. Most train problems use exactly these numbers.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Convert 45 km/h to m/s without a pen. *(45 × 5/18 = 12.5 m/s.)*

---

## Chunk 3: Average Speed — The Trap Everyone Falls Into

**Average speed is NOT the average of the two speeds.** Repeat that aloud.

If a man travels equal distances at speeds *a* and *b*:

**Average Speed = 2ab / (a + b)**  (the harmonic mean)

Example: 60 km at 30 km/h, then 60 km at 60 km/h. Average = (2 × 30 × 60)/(30 + 60) = 3600/90 = **40 km/h** — not 45!

If he travels for equal *times* instead, then average speed is simply (a + b)/2. The examiner's trap: mixing up "equal distance" with "equal time." Read the question twice.

For three equal distances at speeds a, b, c: **Average Speed = 3abc / (ab + bc + ca)**

🧠 **Active Recall:** A cyclist goes to market at 10 km/h and returns the same way at 15 km/h. Average speed? *(2×10×15/25 = 12 km/h.)*

## Chunk 4: Trains — Length Is Everything

Train problems have one golden rule: **identify what distance the train actually covers.**

| Crossing object | Distance covered |
|---|---|
| A pole / a standing man | Train's own length (L) |
| A platform / bridge of length P | L + P |
| Another train (length M), opposite direction | L + M, at relative speed (sum) |
| Another train, same direction | L + M, at relative speed (difference) |

**Relative speed:** opposite directions → add speeds; same direction → subtract.

Example: Two trains of 120 m and 180 m move towards each other at 40 km/h and 50 km/h. Time to cross each other = (120 + 180) / [(40 + 50) × 5/18] = 300 / 25 = **12 seconds**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** A 250 m train crosses a 150 m platform in 20 s. Speed in km/h? *(Distance = 400 m; speed = 20 m/s = 72 km/h.)*

## Chunk 5: Boats & Streams — The River Helps or Fights

- **Downstream speed** (with the current) = boat speed + stream speed = **B + S**
- **Upstream speed** (against the current) = **B − S**

From these two, everything follows:

- **B = (Downstream + Upstream) / 2**
- **S = (Downstream − Upstream) / 2**

Example: A boat goes 30 km downstream in 2 h and the same distance upstream in 3 h. Downstream = 15 km/h, upstream = 10 km/h. So B = 12.5 km/h, S = 2.5 km/h.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If downstream speed is 18 km/h and the stream is 3 km/h, what is the upstream speed? *(B = 15, so upstream = 12 km/h.)*

## Chunk 6: Races & Circular Tracks

**Race language decoded:**

- "A beats B by 20 m in a 100 m race" → when A runs 100 m, B runs 80 m. Their speed ratio is 100:80 = 5:4.
- "A gives B a start of 10 m" → B starts 10 m ahead, so B runs only 90 m.

**Circular tracks:** Two runners on a circular track of length L:

- Same direction: they meet every **L / (difference of speeds)**
- Opposite directions: they meet every **L / (sum of speeds)**
- Time to meet at the **starting point** = LCM of their individual lap times

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a 500 m race, A beats B by 50 m. If A's speed is 10 m/s, what is B's speed? *(Ratio 500:450 = 10:9, so B = 9 m/s.)*

---

## 🎯 Mnemonic: "DST — Don't Stop Thinking"

Remember the formula triangle **D-S-T**: cover the one you want.

- Cover D → **D = S × T**
- Cover S → **S = D / T**
- Cover T → **T = D / S**

And for unit conversion, remember **"5/18 is small, 18/5 is tall"** — going from big units (km/h) to small (m/s), multiply by the small fraction 5/18; going the other way, multiply by the tall one, 18/5.
