# Geometry — The Chapter That Decides Your Rank

## Why Geometry? Why Now?

Here's a fact that should wake you up: in almost every major competitive exam — SSC CGL Tier-II, IBPS PO Mains, CAT, State PCS — Geometry is the section where the *gap* between a selected candidate and a rejected one is created. Not because it's the hardest topic, but because most aspirants *fear* it and skip it. The ones who master it collect marks that others voluntarily donate.

And here's the secret nobody tells you: **Geometry is not about memorising 200 formulas. It's about seeing 5–6 core ideas from different angles.** Once you see them, the "tough" questions collapse into 30-second problems.

## Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Use angle properties (parallel lines, triangles, circles) without hesitation.
2. Apply congruence and similarity rules to find unknown sides and angles.
3. Solve triangle problems using Pythagoras, median, and centroid properties.
4. Handle circle theorems — chords, tangents, secants, cyclic quadrilaterals.
5. Compute areas and volumes of 2D and 3D figures quickly.
6. Spot the examiner's traps (nearly-true statements, diagram illusions).

## "But I'm Bad at Geometry…"

Let's be honest. Most of us carry a school-time scar: a diagram full of lines, a teacher saying "it's obvious," and nothing being obvious at all. So we decided Geometry is "not our thing."

That belief is wrong. Geometry feels hard only when you try to *memorise* it. The moment you start *seeing* it — one small idea at a time — it becomes the most scoring topic in the paper. We'll go chunk by chunk. No chunk will be bigger than what you can digest in two minutes.

---

## Chunk 1: Lines and Angles — The Alphabet of Geometry

Everything in Geometry is built from lines and angles. Master these and 30% of the battle is won.

**Basic facts (never forget):**
- Angles on a straight line add to **180°**.
- Angles around a point add to **360°**.
- Vertically opposite angles are **equal**.
- Complementary angles sum to 90°; supplementary angles sum to 180°.

**Parallel lines cut by a transversal** give you three golden relations:
- **Corresponding angles** are equal (they sit in the same "corner" at each intersection).
- **Alternate interior angles** are equal (they form a "Z" shape).
- **Co-interior (same-side interior) angles** are supplementary (they form a "C" or "U" shape).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. A transversal cuts two parallel lines. One angle is 65°. What are the other seven angles? (Answer: four are 65°, four are 115°.)

**Mnemonic — "Z-F-C-U":** **Z** = alternate angles equal, **F** = corresponding angles equal, **C/U** = co-interior angles supplementary. Just trace the letter on the diagram!

## Chunk 2: Triangles — The King of Geometry

If Geometry were a kingdom, the triangle would be its king. More exam questions come from triangles than from any other figure.

**Foundation facts:**
- Sum of interior angles = **180°**.
- Exterior angle = sum of the two **opposite interior angles**.
- Sum of any two sides > third side; difference of any two sides < third side.

**Congruence — when are two triangles identical?**
Four tests: **SSS** (three sides), **SAS** (two sides + included angle), **ASA/AAS** (two angles + a side), **RHS** (right angle + hypotenuse + one side).

⚠️ **Trap alert:** **AAA is NOT a congruence test** — it only proves *similarity*. Examiners love this trap.

**Similarity — same shape, different size:**
- If two triangles are similar, the ratio of corresponding sides = ratio of heights = ratio of medians.
- **Ratio of areas = square of the ratio of sides.** (Sides in ratio 2:3 → areas in ratio 4:9. This one line solves dozens of exam questions.)
- **Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales):** A line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Two similar triangles have areas 36 cm² and 100 cm². What's the ratio of their corresponding sides? (Answer: √36 : √100 = 6 : 10 = 3 : 5.)

## Chunk 3: The Special Points Inside a Triangle

Four "centres" appear again and again:

| Centre | Formed by | Special property |
|---|---|---|
| **Centroid (G)** | Medians | Divides each median in **2 : 1** (vertex side is longer) |
| **Incentre (I)** | Angle bisectors | Centre of incircle; equidistant from all *sides* |
| **Circumcentre (O)** | Perpendicular bisectors of sides | Centre of circumcircle; equidistant from all *vertices* |
| **Orthocentre (H)** | Altitudes | In an obtuse triangle, it lies *outside* |

**Memory trick — "GIMO… wait, G-I-O-H":** Think **"Gandhi Is Our Hero"** — **G**andhi = Centroid (medians), **I**s = Incentre (angle bisectors), **O**ur = Orthocentre (altitudes), **H**ero = Circumcentre… hmm, that breaks. Better: **"C-I-O-O"** — **C**entroid-**M**edians, **I**ncentre-angle b**I**sectors, **O**rthocentre-altitudes, circumcentre-perpendicular bisectors. Or simply remember: *"Medians meet at the Centre of gravity"* — that's the centroid, 2:1.

**Pythagoras and its family:**
- In a right triangle: **a² + b² = c²**.
- Converse also holds — if a² + b² = c², the triangle is right-angled.
- Common triplets to memorise: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25), (20,21,29) — and their multiples.

**Apollonius Theorem (median formula):** If m is the median to side a, then **b² + c² = 2(m² + a²/4)**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a right-angled triangle, where does the circumcentre lie? (Answer: at the midpoint of the hypotenuse — the hypotenuse is the diameter of the circumcircle.)

## Chunk 4: Quadrilaterals and Polygons

**Quadrilateral facts:**
- Sum of interior angles = **360°**.
- **Parallelogram:** opposite sides equal and parallel; diagonals bisect each other.
- **Rhombus:** all sides equal; diagonals bisect each other at **90°**; area = ½ × d₁ × d₂.
- **Rectangle:** diagonals are **equal** and bisect each other.
- **Square:** all of the above combined.
- **Trapezium:** area = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height.

**Regular polygon formulas (n sides):**
- Each interior angle = **(n − 2) × 180° / n**
- Each exterior angle = **360° / n**
- Number of diagonals = **n(n − 3)/2**

🧠 **Active Recall:** A regular polygon has each exterior angle = 40°. How many sides? (Answer: 360/40 = 9 sides — a nonagon.)

## Chunk 5: Circles — The Examiner's Favourite

Circles give examiners the most elegant traps. Learn these theorems as *pictures*, not words:

1. **Angle at the centre = 2 × angle at the circumference** (standing on the same arc).
2. **Angle in a semicircle = 90°.**
3. Angles in the same segment are equal.
4. **Cyclic quadrilateral:** opposite angles are supplementary (sum = 180°).
5. **Tangent ⊥ radius** at the point of contact.
6. **Tangents from an external point are equal** in length.
7. **Alternate Segment Theorem:** angle between tangent and chord = angle in the alternate segment.
8. **Intersecting chords:** PA × PB = PC × PD. For a tangent from P: **PT² = PA × PB**.

**Useful formulas:**
- Length of chord = 2√(r² − d²), where d = perpendicular distance from centre.
- Common tangent (direct) between two circles = √(d² − (r₁ − r₂)²); transverse = √(d² − (r₁ + r₂)²).

🧠 **Active Recall:** O is the centre, and ∠AOB = 100° where A and B are on the circle. What is ∠ACB if C is on the major arc? (Answer: 50° — half the central angle.)

## Chunk 6: Mensuration — Where Geometry Meets Numbers

**2D areas:**
- Triangle: ½ × base × height; **Heron's formula:** √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)], s = (a+b+c)/2.
- Equilateral triangle: (√3/4)a²; its height = (√3/2)a.
- Circle: πr²; sector = (θ/360°) × πr²; arc length = (θ/360°) × 2πr.

**3D figures:**

| Solid | Curved/Lateral Surface | Total Surface | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cuboid (l,b,h) | 2h(l+b) | 2(lb+bh+hl) | lbh |
| Cube (a) | 4a² | 6a² | a³ |
| Cylinder (r,h) | 2πrh | 2πr(r+h) | πr²h |
| Cone (r, l, h) | πrl | πr(r+l) | (1/3)πr²h |
| Sphere (r) | 4πr² | 4πr² | (4/3)πr³ |
| Hemisphere (r) | 2πr² | 3πr² | (2/3)πr³ |

**Key relations:** Cone's slant height l = √(r² + h²). Cuboid's diagonal = √(l² + b² + h²); cube's diagonal = a√3.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If a sphere's radius doubles, by what factor does its volume grow? (Answer: 2³ = 8 times.)

## Chunk 7: The Examiner's Traps — Read This Twice

1. **Diagram illusion:** Figures in exams are often *not to scale*. Never assume an angle "looks like" 90°.
2. **AAA ≠ congruence.** It proves similarity only.
3. **Area ratio = square of side ratio** — students forget the square and lose easy marks.
4. **"Not drawn to scale" + nearly-true statements:** In statement-based questions, test each statement with the theorem, not with the picture.
5. **Units trap:** Area in cm², volume in cm³ — options often mix them.
6. **π = 22/7 vs 3.14:** Check what the question specifies before calculating.
