# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Number System (Advanced)

## How Exams Test This Chapter

Number System is one of the most reliable scoring areas across UPSC CSAT, SSC CGL Tier-II, IBPS PO Mains, CAT and State PCS papers. The questions rarely ask for plain definitions — they test whether you can **combine two or three concepts under time pressure**. The recurring patterns are:

- **Remainder problems** — especially "a number leaves remainder r₁ when divided by d₁ and r₂ when divided by d₂" (Chinese Remainder style), and cyclicity of unit digits.
- **Divisibility rules in disguise** — a large number with a missing digit, where you must apply the rule for 9, 11, 8 or 7.
- **Factors and multiples** — number of factors, sum of factors, number of ways to write N as a product of two factors, HCF–LCM word problems (bells tolling together, tiles, greatest length of rope).
- **Base systems and digit problems** — reversing digits, sum of digits, numbers in bases other than 10.
- **Fractions and recurring decimals** — converting p/q to decimal, identifying terminating vs non-terminating forms.
- **Algebraic identities applied to numbers** — aⁿ − bⁿ divisibility, (a+b)² manipulations, sum of first n naturals/squares/cubes.

## The Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

1. **The "almost right" remainder trap:** Options include the remainder you get if you forget to adjust for a negative remainder. E.g., remainder −2 written as an option alongside the correct +5 (when divisor is 7).
2. **HCF–LCM swap:** The question asks for the *greatest* number that divides several quantities (HCF), but one option is the LCM — planted for students who read in a hurry.
3. **"At least / smallest such number" wording:** LCM-type problems where you must *add* the common remainder after taking LCM; the trap option is the bare LCM.
4. **Unit-digit cyclicity with exponent 0 mod 4:** When the exponent is a multiple of 4, you must use the 4th power in the cycle, not the 0th. The "0th power = 1" trap is heavily planted.
5. **Number of factors of a perfect square:** Students forget that a perfect square has an *odd* number of factors; options include the even count from a wrong pairing argument.
6. **Negative statements:** "Which of the following is NOT divisible by…" — one careless reading flips the answer.
7. **Digit-reversal algebra:** "A two-digit number exceeds the number formed by reversing its digits by 18" — the trap is assuming the difference is 9 times the *sum* of digits instead of 9 times the *difference*.
8. **Terminating decimal confusion:** A fraction terminates only if the denominator (in lowest terms) has no prime factors other than 2 and 5. Options include fractions like 7/12 that "look" terminating.

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions)

**Q1.** What is the remainder when 7^84 is divided by 342?
(a) 0  (b) 1  (c) 49  (d) 341

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** 7³ = 343 = 342 + 1, so 7³ ≡ 1 (mod 342). Since 84 = 3 × 28, 7^84 = (7³)^28 ≡ 1^28 = 1 (mod 342).
**Hook:** The examiner counts on you not noticing 343 = 7³. Options (a) and (d) attract students who guess "big power means 0 or divisor − 1".
**Source:** PYQ-style (CAT/CSAT pattern)

**Q2.** A number when divided by 5 leaves remainder 3, and when divided by 7 leaves remainder 5. What is the smallest such number?
(a) 18  (b) 33  (c) 38  (d) 68

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Note 5 − 3 = 2 and 7 − 5 = 2; the shortfall is the same (2). So the number = LCM(5, 7) − 2 = 35 − 2 = 33. Check: 33 ÷ 5 → rem 3; 33 ÷ 7 → rem 5. ✓
**Hook:** Option (c) 38 is the trap for students who compute LCM + 3 instead of spotting the constant difference.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC/CSAT pattern)

**Q3.** The unit digit of (323)^2024 is:
(a) 1  (b) 3  (c) 7  (d) 9

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Only the unit digit 3 matters. Cycle of unit digits of powers of 3: 3, 9, 7, 1 (period 4). Since 2024 = 4 × 506, the exponent is an exact multiple of 4, so we take the 4th (last) position of the cycle: 3⁴ = 81 → unit digit 1. Hence the unit digit of (323)^2024 is 1.
**Hook:** The classic trap — when the exponent leaves remainder 0 on division by 4, students pick the "0th" power or the first term of the cycle (3). Remainder 0 always means the *last* position of the cycle.
**Source:** PYQ-style (CSAT pattern)

**Q4.** How many factors does 360 have?
(a) 20  (b) 22  (c) 24  (d) 28

**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹. Number of factors = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 × 3 × 2 = 24.
**Hook:** Option (b) 22 traps students who add exponents instead of adding 1 to each; (a) 20 comes from dropping one factor.
**Source:** NCERT Class 10 Maths (Real Numbers) / PYQ-style

**Q5.** The HCF and LCM of two numbers are 12 and 504 respectively. If one number is 72, the other is:
(a) 84  (b) 96  (c) 108  (d) 144

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Product of numbers = HCF × LCM = 12 × 504 = 6048. Other number = 6048 ÷ 72 = 84. Check: HCF(72, 84) = 12 ✓.
**Hook:** Option (b) 96 comes from division slips; students also sometimes divide the LCM by the given number and forget the HCF factor.
**Source:** NCERT Class 10 Maths / PYQ-style

**Q6.** The smallest number which when increased by 7 is exactly divisible by 24, 32 and 36 is:
(a) 281  (b) 288  (c) 295  (d) 869

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** LCM(24, 32, 36): 24 = 2³·3, 32 = 2⁵, 36 = 2²·3² → LCM = 2⁵ × 3² = 288. Required number = 288 − 7 = 281.
**Hook:** Option (b) 288 is the bare LCM — planted for those who forget to subtract 7.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC pattern)

**Q7.** A two-digit number is 4 times the sum of its digits. If the digits are interchanged, the new number is:
(a) 4 times the sum of digits  (b) 7 times the sum of digits  (c) 9 more than the original  (d) equal to the original

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Let digits be x (tens), y (units). 10x + y = 4(x + y) → 6x = 3y → y = 2x. Reversed number = 10y + x = 20x + x = 21x = 7(3x) = 7(x + y). So the reversed number is 7 times the sum of digits. (Example: 12 = 4 × 3, and 21 = 7 × 3.)
**Hook:** The trap is option (d) for those who assume symmetry, and (c) for those who test one example (12 → 21, difference 9) and generalise wrongly.
**Source:** PYQ-style (UPSC CSAT pattern)

**Q8.** Which of the following fractions gives a terminating decimal?
(a) 13/30  (b) 17/64  (c) 11/24  (d) 19/45

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** A fraction p/q (in lowest terms) terminates iff q has only primes 2 and 5. 64 = 2⁶ ✓. But 30 = 2·3·5, 24 = 2³·3, 45 = 3²·5 — all contain 3, so they recur.
**Hook:** All four denominators "look small"; the trap is 13/30 because 30 ends in 0 and feels terminating.
**Source:** NCERT Class 10 Maths (Real Numbers)

**Q9.** What is the remainder when 2^1000 is divided by 7?
(a) 1  (b) 2  (c) 4  (d) 6

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Powers of 2 mod 7 cycle: 2, 4, 1 (period 3). 1000 = 3 × 333 + 1 → position 1 in the cycle → 2^1000 ≡ 2 (mod 7).
**Hook:** Option (a) 1 is planted for students who compute 1000 mod 3 = 1 but then misread the cycle position; option (d) 6 attracts "divisor − 1" guessers.
**Source:** PYQ-style (CAT/PO Mains pattern)

**Q10.** The sum of all three-digit numbers divisible by 7 is:
(a) 70336  (b) 71344  (c) 69336  (d) 72344

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** First 3-digit multiple of 7 = 105, last = 994. Number of terms n = (994 − 105)/7 + 1 = 127 + 1 = 128. Sum = n/2 × (first + last) = 64 × 1099 = 70336.
**Hook:** The traps come from miscounting n (off-by-one errors) or taking 100 and 999 as endpoints. Always anchor to the actual first/last multiples.
**Source:** NCERT Class 10 Maths (AP) / PYQ-style

## Key Takeaways for the Exam Hall

- **Remainder + cyclicity + factors** together cover the bulk of questions from this chapter — master these three first.
- Always check whether the question says *greatest* (HCF) or *smallest* (LCM), and *increased by* or *leaves remainder* — one word flips the method.
- For unit digits, write the cycle of 4 first; never trust memory mid-exam. If the exponent is a multiple of 4, take the 4th position of the cycle.
- In "smallest number satisfying two remainder conditions", first check if the differences (divisor − remainder) are equal — that shortcut solves it in seconds.
- Verify factor-count answers by prime factorisation; never estimate.
