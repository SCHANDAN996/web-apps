# The Feynman Explanation: Standard Deviation — The "Average Distance from the Average"

## The Losing Match

Imagine two cricket players. Both score an **average of 40 runs** per match over 10 matches.

- **Player A** scores: 38, 42, 40, 39, 41, 40, 38, 42, 39, 41
- **Player B** scores: 5, 95, 10, 80, 40, 2, 98, 30, 60, 40

Same average — 40. But would you pick them equally for your team? Of course not. Player A is **consistent**; Player B is a gamble. The average alone hides this. You need a number that measures **how spread out** the scores are. That number is the **standard deviation**.

## The One-Line Idea

> Standard deviation = the *typical distance* of the data points from their own average.

- **Small** standard deviation → data huddles close to the mean (consistent, predictable).
- **Large** standard deviation → data is scattered far from the mean (inconsistent, risky).

## How to Build It (Step by Step)

Take a tiny data set: **2, 4, 6**

**Step 1 — Find the mean.**
Mean = (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = **4**

**Step 2 — Find each point's distance from the mean (deviation).**
2 − 4 = −2; 4 − 4 = 0; 6 − 4 = +2

**Step 3 — Square the deviations** (this removes minus signs and punishes big gaps more).
(−2)² = 4; 0² = 0; (+2)² = 4

**Step 4 — Average the squares.** This is the **variance**.
Variance = (4 + 0 + 4) ÷ 3 = 8/3 ≈ 2.67

**Step 5 — Take the square root** to return to the original units.
Standard deviation = √(8/3) ≈ **1.63**

So the "typical distance" of these numbers from their mean 4 is about 1.63.

## Why Square and Then Root?

If you simply averaged the deviations (−2, 0, +2), the positives and negatives would cancel out and you'd always get zero — useless. Squaring fixes the sign problem; the final square root brings the answer back into the same units as the data (runs, marks, rupees — not "square runs").

## Exam Memory Hook

**M-D-S-A-R**: **M**ean → **D**eviations → **S**quare them → **A**verage (variance) → **R**oot.

And remember: **variance = (standard deviation)²**. If a question gives variance 25, the standard deviation is 5 — not 625.

## Common Exam Traps

- **Adding a constant to every value** (e.g., every score +5) shifts the mean but leaves the standard deviation **unchanged** — the spread doesn't move.
- **Multiplying every value by k** multiplies the standard deviation by |k|.
- Standard deviation is **never negative**. If your answer is negative, you made an error.
- Don't confuse **variance** (σ²) with **standard deviation** (σ) — examiners love swapping them in options.
