# Algebra — The Language of Unknowns

## Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Master identities and factorisation without memorising blindly
- Solve linear, quadratic and simultaneous equations with speed
- Handle inequalities, modulus and surds confidently
- Tackle polynomials, remainder/factor theorems and progressions (AP/GP)
- Spot the traps examiners set in SSC CGL Tier-II, IBPS PO Mains, CAT and CSAT

---

## The Hook: The Number That Terrified a Civilisation

Around 500 BCE, a Greek mathematician named Hippasus made a discovery so disturbing that, legend says, his own school — the Pythagoreans — drowned him at sea for it. His crime? He proved that some numbers cannot be written as a fraction. The square root of 2 was "irrational", and the world of neat, tidy numbers cracked open forever.

Why start here? Because **Algebra is exactly this: the moment mathematics stops being about fixed numbers and starts being about the unknown.** The moment you write *x*, you are doing what terrified the ancients — admitting you don't know something, and then *systematically hunting it down*.

Every competitive exam you will face — SSC CGL, IBPS PO, CAT, CSAT — is secretly testing one skill: **can you convert a messy word problem into a clean equation, and solve it fast?** This chapter gives you that weapon.

## "But I Hate Algebra…"

Let's be honest. Most aspirants carry a school-time scar: pages full of *x* and *y*, a teacher saying "just transpose it", and no idea *why*. Here's the truth nobody told you:

**Algebra is not about letters. It is about balance.**

An equation is a weighing scale. Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. That's it. Every rule — transposition, cross-multiplication, elimination — is just a shortcut for keeping the scale balanced. Once you see this, the fear dissolves.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Before reading further, close your eyes and answer — why are we allowed to "move" a term from LHS to RHS by changing its sign? (Hint: what are we *actually* doing to both sides?)

## Chunk 1: The Golden Identities

Everything in advanced algebra stands on a handful of identities. Don't memorise them as symbols — memorise them as *patterns*.

- (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
- (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
- a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)
- (a + b)³ = a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b)
- (a − b)³ = a³ − b³ − 3ab(a − b)
- a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
- a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)
- a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = (a + b + c)(a² + b² + c² − ab − bc − ca)

**The exam favourite:** if a + b + c = 0, then **a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc**. This single result cracks many "impossible-looking" questions in SSC and CAT.

**The workhorse trick:** from (a + b)² and (a − b)², we get
**(a + b)² − (a − b)² = 4ab** and **(a + b)² + (a − b)² = 2(a² + b²)**.

So if x + 1/x = 5, then x² + 1/x² = 5² − 2 = 23, and x³ + 1/x³ = 5³ − 3×5 = 110. Notice the pattern: **square → subtract 2; cube → subtract 3 times the base.** This is one of the most repeated algebra patterns in SSC exams.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If x − 1/x = 4, what is x² + 1/x²? (Careful — the sign flips the adjustment. Answer: 4² + 2 = 18.)

## Chunk 2: Linear Equations — One Unknown, One Truth

A linear equation in one variable, ax + b = 0, has exactly one solution: x = −b/a. Simple. But exams rarely give you the equation — they give you a *story*: ages, work, money, mixtures.

**The 3-step translation method:**
1. **Name** the unknown (let the present age be x).
2. **Translate** each sentence into an equation piece by piece.
3. **Solve and verify** against the story, not just the algebra.

For two variables, you need two independent equations. Solve by:
- **Substitution** (express one variable, plug in)
- **Elimination** (match coefficients, add/subtract)
- **Cross-multiplication** for a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0

**Consistency check (a CSAT/CGL favourite):**
- a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ → unique solution
- a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ → infinitely many solutions
- a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ → no solution

🧠 **Active Recall:** Two equations represent two lines. What does "no solution" mean geometrically? (Parallel lines — they never meet.)

## Chunk 3: Quadratic Equations — Where the Real Game Begins

ax² + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0). Three tools:

1. **Factorisation** — fastest when roots are integers.
2. **Quadratic formula:** x = [−b ± √(b² − 4ac)] / 2a
3. **Discriminant analysis:** D = b² − 4ac
   - D > 0 → two distinct real roots
   - D = 0 → equal roots
   - D < 0 → no real roots
   - D a perfect square (with rational coefficients) → rational roots

**Vieta's relations (memorise like your name):**
- Sum of roots = −b/a
- Product of roots = c/a

These two lines solve many quadratic questions without ever finding the roots. "The sum of roots is 7 and product is 12" → roots are 3 and 4, equation is x² − 7x + 12 = 0. Done in seconds.

**Sign tricks:** If both roots are positive → sum positive, product positive. If roots have opposite signs → product negative (c/a < 0). Examiners love asking about the *nature* of roots precisely because students ignore these shortcuts.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without solving, what is the sum of roots of 2x² − 9x + 4 = 0? (Answer: 9/2.) And what does D = 81 − 32 = 49 tell you? (Perfect square → rational roots.)

## Chunk 4: Polynomials, Remainder & Factor Theorems

**Remainder Theorem:** When polynomial p(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is simply **p(a)**. No long division needed — just substitute.

**Factor Theorem:** (x − a) is a factor of p(x) **if and only if p(a) = 0**.

Example: Is (x − 2) a factor of x³ − 3x² + 4x − 4? Compute p(2) = 8 − 12 + 8 − 4 = 0. Yes, it is. Ten seconds, zero division.

These two theorems convert terrifying cubic problems into one-line substitutions — a classic IBPS PO Mains and CAT pattern.

🧠 **Active Recall:** What is the remainder when x²⁰²⁴ + 1 is divided by (x − 1)? (Just p(1) = 2.)

## Chunk 5: Inequalities and Modulus

Inequalities follow equation rules **with one deadly exception**: multiplying or dividing by a **negative number flips the sign**. −2x > 6 → x < −3 (not x > −3). This single flip is the examiner's favourite trap.

**Modulus basics:**
- |x| < a → −a < x < a (a sandwich)
- |x| > a → x < −a or x > a (two separate wings)

**Mnemonic to remember forever — "LESS is a Sandwich, MORE is a War (two sides)":**
Less-than squeezes x between two values; greater-than splits the answer into two opposite regions.

For quadratic inequalities like x² − 5x + 6 < 0, factorise: (x − 2)(x − 3) < 0. The product is negative *between* the roots → 2 < x < 3. **Rule: "< 0 lives between the roots, > 0 lives outside them"** (when the coefficient of x² is positive).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Solve |2x − 3| ≤ 5. (Sandwich: −5 ≤ 2x − 3 ≤ 5 → −1 ≤ x ≤ 4.)

## Chunk 6: Surds, Indices and Logarithms

**Laws of indices** (the non-negotiables):
- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
- a⁰ = 1 ; a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ ; a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ)

**Logarithms** are just indices wearing a mask: logₐb = c means a^c = b.
- log(MN) = log M + log N
- log(M/N) = log M − log N
- log Mⁿ = n log M
- logₐb = log b / log a (change of base)

**Mnemonic — "Logs turn shopping into addition":** multiplication becomes addition, division becomes subtraction, powers become multiples. Whenever you see a scary product of powers, take logs and watch it melt.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If log₁₀2 ≈ 0.301, what is log₁₀8? (8 = 2³ → 3 × 0.301 = 0.903.)

## Chunk 7: Progressions — AP and GP

**Arithmetic Progression (AP):** constant difference d.
- nth term: aₙ = a + (n − 1)d
- Sum of n terms: Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d] = n/2 (first + last)

**Geometric Progression (GP):** constant ratio r.
- nth term: aₙ = arⁿ⁻¹
- Sum: Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1)/(r − 1), r ≠ 1
- Infinite GP (|r| < 1): S∞ = a/(1 − r)

**Handy facts:** If a, b, c are in AP → 2b = a + c. If in GP → b² = ac. The arithmetic mean of two positive numbers is always ≥ their geometric mean (AM ≥ GM) — a result that powers an entire family of maxima-minima questions in CAT.

🧠 **Active Recall:** The 10th term of an AP with a = 3, d = 4? (3 + 9×4 = 39.) And the sum of the infinite GP 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + …? (1/(1 − 1/2) = 2.)

## The Examiner's Trap Box

1. **Sign flip in inequalities** — dividing by a negative and forgetting to flip.
2. **x + 1/x confusion** — squaring x − 1/x and subtracting 2 instead of adding.
3. **D < 0 misread** — "no real roots" does not mean "no answer"; it may be exactly what the question asks.
4. **|x| = 5 has two answers** — x = ±5. Never drop the negative case.
5. **a⁰ trap** — any non-zero number to the power 0 is 1, but 0⁰ is undefined; exams exploit the edge.
6. **AM ≥ GM equality** — holds only when all numbers are equal; questions hide maxima right there.
