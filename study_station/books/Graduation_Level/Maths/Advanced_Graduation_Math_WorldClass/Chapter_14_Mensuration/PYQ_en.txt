# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Mensuration (Advanced Level)

## How Exams Test This Chapter

Mensuration is one of the most reliable scoring areas in UPSC CSAT, SSC CGL Tier-II, IBPS PO Mains, CAT and State PCS papers. Examiners rarely ask you to merely "apply a formula." Instead, they wrap the formula inside a situation — a painted cube, a rolled-up sheet, a wire bent into two shapes, water flowing into a tank. The maths is usually one or two lines; the real test is whether you *see* the geometry hidden in the words.

### The recurring question patterns

**1. The "change in dimension" family.**
"If the radius is doubled, what happens to volume?" or "length increased by 20%, breadth decreased by 20% — net effect on area?" These appear constantly. The trap: students add percentages (20 − 20 = 0) instead of multiplying factors (1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, i.e., a 4% *decrease*). For 3D, a doubled radius makes volume 8 times, not 2 or 4.

**2. The "cost / painting / fencing" wrapper.**
Area and volume are disguised as money: cost of carpeting a room, painting the walls of a hall (walls only — exclude floor and ceiling!), fencing a field, tin needed for a closed box. The trap is almost always *which surfaces count*. A room's "walls" = lateral surface area of a cuboid = 2h(l + b). An open tank excludes the top.

**3. The "melt and recast" family.**
A metallic sphere is melted into small cones; a cylinder is recast into spheres. Volume is conserved — that single idea solves everything. The trap: students equate *surface areas* instead of volumes, or forget that the number of small solids = big volume ÷ small volume.

**4. The "surface area after cutting/joining" family.**
A cube cut into 64 identical smaller cubes — how many have exactly two painted faces? Or two cubes joined face-to-face — what is the new surface area? The trap: joining two cubes does NOT double the surface area; two faces get hidden (subtract 2a²).

**5. The "wire / rope reshaped" family.**
A wire bent from a square into a circle — compare areas. Perimeter/circumference stays constant. The trap: assuming area also stays constant. For a fixed perimeter, the circle always encloses the *maximum* area — a favourite CSAT fact.

**6. The "path around / inside a field" family.**
A path of uniform width runs around (or inside) a rectangular park. Outer area minus inner area. The trap: the path adds width on *both* sides, so each dimension changes by 2w, not w.

**7. The "cylinder from a sheet / cone from a sector" family.**
A rectangular sheet rolled into a cylinder; a sector of a circle folded into a cone. The trap: when a sheet is rolled, one side becomes the height and the other becomes the *circumference* (2πr), not the radius or diameter. For a sector, the arc length becomes the base circumference and the sector radius becomes the slant height.

**8. The "rainfall / water flow" family.**
Rain falling on a roof drains into a tank; water flows through a pipe into a cistern. Volume collected = base area × height of water. The trap: unit conversion — rainfall in cm over an area in m² must be converted before multiplying.

**9. The "ratio" family.**
Two cylinders have radii in ratio 2:3 and heights in ratio 5:4 — find the ratio of volumes. The trap: forgetting that radius is *squared* in the formula. Volume ratio = (2² × 5) : (3² × 4) = 20 : 36 = 5 : 9.

**10. The "diagonal" family.**
Diagonal of a square or cube given, area/volume asked. The trap: using the diagonal as if it were the side. Remember: side = diagonal/√2 (square), space diagonal of cube = a√3.

### The examiner's favourite traps (psychological hooks)

- **Almost-right statements:** "Doubling the radius doubles the volume" — sounds plausible, is false (volume becomes 8×). Examiners love statements that are *directionally* right but *magnitude* wrong.
- **Unit mismatch:** dimensions in metres, options in cm²; rainfall in cm, tank in m. The correct numerical value with the wrong unit is always one of the options.
- **Open vs closed:** "open box," "walls of a room," "hollow cylinder" — one word changes which surfaces count. The option set always contains the closed-figure answer as bait.
- **π = 22/7 vs 3.14:** when the radius is a multiple of 7, use 22/7 — the question is designed for it. Forcing 3.14 wastes time and invites arithmetic slips.
- **Perimeter–area confusion:** same perimeter does not mean same area. Options pair the perimeter answer with the area question.

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with solutions)

**Q1.** The length of a rectangle is increased by 25% and its breadth is decreased by 20%. What is the percentage change in its area?

(a) 5% increase (b) 0% (no change) (c) 5% decrease (d) 2.5% increase

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Net factor = 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00, so the area is unchanged.
**Trap:** Students compute 25 − 20 = 5% increase (option a). Percentage changes on *different* dimensions multiply; they never add or subtract.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q2.** A solid metallic sphere of radius 6 cm is melted and recast into identical small cones, each of radius 2 cm and height 3 cm. How many cones are formed?

(a) 24 (b) 36 (c) 48 (d) 72

**Answer: (d)**
**Solution:** Volume of sphere = (4/3)π(6)³ = 288π cm³. Volume of one cone = (1/3)π(2)²(3) = 4π cm³. Number = 288π ÷ 4π = 72.
**Trap:** Equating surface areas, or forgetting the 1/3 in the cone's volume (which gives 24 — option a, the bait).
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q3.** A cube is painted on all faces and cut into 64 identical smaller cubes. How many small cubes have exactly two painted faces?

(a) 8 (b) 12 (c) 24 (d) 32

**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** 64 = 4³, so each edge has 4 small cubes. Two-painted-face cubes lie on edges but not at corners: 12 edges × (4 − 2) = 24.
**Trap:** Option (a) is the corner count (3 painted faces); option (b) is just the number of edges. The formula is 12(n − 2).
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q4.** A wire is bent into a square of side 11 cm. The same wire is then bent into a circle. What is the area of the circle? (Take π = 22/7)

(a) 154 cm² (b) 144 cm² (c) 121 cm² (d) 176 cm²

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Wire length = 4 × 11 = 44 cm = circumference. So 2πr = 44 → r = 7 cm. Area = πr² = (22/7) × 49 = 154 cm².
**Trap:** Assuming the area stays 121 cm² (option c — the square's area). For a fixed perimeter, the circle encloses more area than any other shape.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q5.** A rectangular sheet of paper 44 cm × 18 cm is rolled along its length to form a cylinder. What is the volume of the cylinder? (Take π = 22/7)

(a) 2772 cm³ (b) 1386 cm³ (c) 5544 cm³ (d) 693 cm³

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Rolled along the length: circumference = 44 cm, height = 18 cm. Then 2πr = 44 → r = 7 cm. Volume = πr²h = (22/7) × 49 × 18 = 2772 cm³.
**Trap:** Taking 44 cm as the height instead of the circumference. Rolling the other way (circumference = 18 cm, height = 44 cm) gives r = 63/22 cm and volume ≈ 1134 cm³ — a different answer entirely, so the question's wording decides which side becomes the circumference.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q6.** A path 2 m wide runs around *inside* a rectangular park 40 m × 30 m. What is the area of the path?

(a) 264 m² (b) 280 m² (c) 256 m² (d) 300 m²

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Inner rectangle = (40 − 4) × (30 − 4) = 36 × 26 = 936 m². Path area = 1200 − 936 = 264 m².
**Trap:** Subtracting only 2 m from each dimension (giving 38 × 28) — the path eats 2 m from *both* sides, so subtract 4 m. Option (b) is that exact mistake.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q7.** The radii of two cylinders are in the ratio 2 : 3 and their heights in the ratio 5 : 4. What is the ratio of their volumes?

(a) 5 : 6 (b) 5 : 9 (c) 10 : 12 (d) 4 : 9

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** V = πr²h, so ratio = (2² × 5) : (3² × 4) = 20 : 36 = 5 : 9.
**Trap:** Using r instead of r² gives (2 × 5) : (3 × 4) = 10 : 12 = 5 : 6 — option (a), the classic bait.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q8.** A room is 8 m long, 6 m wide and 4 m high. What is the cost of painting its four walls and ceiling at ₹25 per m²?

(a) ₹4,000 (b) ₹4,800 (c) ₹5,200 (d) ₹3,700

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Walls = 2h(l + b) = 2 × 4 × (8 + 6) = 112 m². Ceiling = l × b = 48 m². Total = 160 m². Cost = 160 × 25 = ₹4,000.
**Trap:** Using total surface area 2(lb + bh + hl) = 208 m² (option c = 208 × 25) — that includes the floor, which is not painted.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q9.** The diagonal of a square is 10√2 cm. What is its area?

(a) 100 cm² (b) 200 cm² (c) 50 cm² (d) 100√2 cm²

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Side = diagonal/√2 = 10 cm. Area = 10² = 100 cm². (Shortcut: area = d²/2 = 200/2 = 100.)
**Trap:** Treating the diagonal as the side gives 200 cm² (option b). The d²/2 shortcut kills this trap permanently.
**Source: PYQ-style**

**Q10.** Water flows through a cylindrical pipe of internal diameter 7 cm at 5 m/s into a tank. How much water (in litres) flows into the tank in 10 minutes? (Take π = 22/7)

(a) 1,155 litres (b) 11,550 litres (c) 1,15,500 litres (d) 115.5 litres

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Radius = 3.5 cm = 0.035 m. Length of water column in 10 min = 5 × 600 = 3000 m. Volume = πr²h = (22/7) × (0.035)² × 3000 = (22/7) × 0.001225 × 3000 = 11.55 m³. Since 1 m³ = 1000 litres, volume = 11,550 litres.
**Trap:** The unit chain cm → m and m³ → litres is where most candidates slip; options (a) and (d) are the same digits with decimal shifts — pure unit-conversion bait.
**Source: PYQ-style**

## Strategy Notes

- **Read the last line first.** Knowing whether the question wants area, volume, cost or a ratio tells you which surfaces/dimensions matter before you start computing.
- **Hunt the hidden constant.** In melt-recast, wire-reshaping and water-flow questions, one quantity (volume, length, perimeter) never changes. Find it and the problem collapses to one equation.
- **Check units before touching π.** Convert everything to one unit system first; examiners place the "right digits, wrong unit" option deliberately.
- **Memorise the scaling laws:** length ×k → area ×k², volume ×k³. Half of all Mensuration PYQs reduce to this single idea.
- **30-second rule:** if a Mensuration problem needs more than three formula steps, you have probably missed a shortcut — re-read for the conserved quantity or a ratio approach.
