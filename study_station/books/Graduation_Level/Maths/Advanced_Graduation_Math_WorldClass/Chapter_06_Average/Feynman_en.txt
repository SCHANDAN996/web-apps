# The Seesaw Secret of Averages

## The Hardest Concept: Why does the average "move"?

Most students can compute an average. But the question that separates toppers from the rest is this: **when one number joins or leaves a group, why does the average shift — and by exactly how much?** Let's crack it like a 12-year-old would.

## The Story of the Seesaw and the Watermelons 🍉

Imagine a perfectly balanced seesaw in a park. On it sit 10 kids, and each kid is holding a watermelon weighing exactly **20 kg**. The seesaw balances at the "20 kg" mark — that's your average.

Now a new kid, Chintu, jumps on carrying a watermelon of **30 kg** — 10 kg *heavier* than everyone else.

What happens? Chintu doesn't keep his extra 10 kg to himself. He's generous — he **shares** his extra 10 kg equally among all 11 kids now on the seesaw. Each kid gets 10 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.91 kg extra. So the balance point slides from 20 to about **20.91 kg**.

That's it. That's the whole secret:

> **The average is a sharing point. Anyone above it donates; anyone below it receives. The seesaw always settles where donations exactly equal receipts.**

## The Reverse Trick (the one exams love)

Now suppose the teacher says: "After Chintu joined, the average became 21." Can you find Chintu's watermelon *without* knowing the old total?

Yes! Chintu must carry:
- his own "fair share" of the new average = 21 kg, **plus**
- the 1 kg raise he gifted to each of the 10 old kids = 10 kg

**Chintu's melon = 21 + 10 = 31 kg.** Done in 5 seconds, no algebra.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "TIP — The Immigrant Pays" 🧳

Whenever a new member **joins** a group:

> **New member's value = New average ± (Old count × Change in average)**
> - Average went **up**? The immigrant **paid** the raise → **add**.
> - Average went **down**? The immigrant was **poorer** → **subtract**.

If someone **leaves**, flip the logic: the leaver takes their share back.

Memory hook: **"TIP"** — just like a waiter gets a tip, the group gets a "tip" from the newcomer, and the whole group's average rises by that tip divided by the new headcount.

## Why this beats formulas

Formulas make you compute two totals and subtract. The seesaw makes you *see* it: a 30 kg melon among 20 kg melons is just "10 extra kg being distributed." Once you see averages as **redistribution**, questions about replacements, wrong entries, and included/excluded members all become the same story with different characters.
