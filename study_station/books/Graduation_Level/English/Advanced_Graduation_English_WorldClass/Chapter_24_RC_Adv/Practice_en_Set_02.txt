26. Read the sentence: "The committee has submitted its report to the ministry." What is the function of "its" in the sentence?
(a) A conjunction joining two clauses (b) A preposition showing direction (c) An adverb modifying "submitted" (d) A possessive pronoun referring to the committee
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Its" shows ownership — the report belongs to the committee, so it is a possessive pronoun. It is tempting to read it as a contraction of "it is", but that would be "it's" with an apostrophe. The answer is a possessive pronoun referring to the committee.
Source: NCERT Class 9 English Grammar

27. In a reading passage, the "main idea" is best described as:
(a) The central point the author wants to convey (b) The most interesting detail in the passage (c) The first sentence of the passage (d) The title of the passage
Answer: (a)
Solution: The main idea is the central message, not merely the opening line or a catchy detail. Many students pick the first sentence by habit, but authors often place the thesis in the middle or end. The answer is the central point the author wants to convey.

28. Choose the word closest in meaning to "candid" as used in: "The minister gave a candid assessment of the economy."
(a) Secretive (b) Lengthy (c) Optimistic (d) Frank and honest
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Candid" means truthful and straightforward. "Optimistic" is tempting because assessments often sound hopeful, but the word itself says nothing about hope — only about honesty. The answer is frank and honest.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English

29. A passage states: "Solar power capacity in India has grown rapidly, yet coal still supplies most of the country's electricity." The word "yet" signals:
(a) A contrast between two facts (b) A cause-and-effect relationship (c) A time sequence (d) An example
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Yet" is a contrast marker — it sets the growth of solar against the continued dominance of coal. Students often mistake it for a time word because "yet" also means "until now", but here it joins two opposing facts. The answer is a contrast between two facts.
Source: PYQ-style

30. What is the primary purpose of skimming a passage before reading the questions?
(a) To get a general sense of the topic and structure (b) To memorise every detail (c) To find the answers directly (d) To count the number of paragraphs
Answer: (a)
Solution: Skimming gives you the gist and the map of the passage so you know where to look later. Memorising details is impossible in one quick read, and answers come only after reading the questions. The answer is to get a general sense of the topic and structure.

31. In the sentence "Despite the heavy rain, the match continued," the word "despite" is followed by:
(a) A noun phrase (b) A full clause with a verb (c) An adverb (d) A conjunction
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Despite" is a preposition and must be followed by a noun or noun phrase ("the heavy rain"), never a full clause — for a clause you need "although". This is a classic examiner trap. The answer is a noun phrase.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English Grammar

32. The tone of a passage that says "The policy, though well-intentioned, has clearly failed the poorest households" is best described as:
(a) Celebratory (b) Critical but balanced (c) Sarcastic (d) Neutral
Answer: (b)
Solution: The writer acknowledges good intention before pointing out failure — that is criticism with fairness, not mockery or praise. "Sarcastic" is tempting because of the negative verdict, but there is no irony or ridicule. The answer is critical but balanced.

33. Choose the correct synonym of "scrutiny" in: "The report is under close scrutiny by the audit team."
(a) Neglect (b) Celebration (c) Careful examination (d) Delay
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Scrutiny" means careful, critical examination. "Neglect" is its opposite and traps students who guess from the negative feel of audits. The answer is careful examination.

34. Which of the following is an inference rather than a stated fact? Passage line: "Ramesh checked his phone for the fifth time in ten minutes and sighed."
(a) Ramesh checked his phone five times (b) Ramesh sighed (c) Ramesh was anxious or impatient (d) Ten minutes passed
Answer: (c)
Solution: An inference is a conclusion drawn from evidence, not directly written. The checking and sighing are stated; the anxiety is deduced. Options repeating the passage word-for-word are facts, not inferences. The answer is that Ramesh was anxious or impatient.
Source: PYQ-style

35. "The data ___ published last week." Choose the correct verb.
(a) was (b) were (c) has (d) is
Answer: (b)
Solution: In formal exam English, "data" is treated as plural (singular: datum), so it takes "were". "Was" sounds right in everyday speech — a classic sound-right trap — but formal usage prefers the plural. The answer is were.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English Grammar

36. A passage's title question asks you to choose the best title. The best title is the one that:
(a) Mentions the most striking detail (b) Repeats the first line of the passage (c) Is the longest option (d) Covers the whole passage without being too broad or too narrow
Answer: (d)
Solution: A good title fits the entire passage — not just one vivid detail, and not a theme so broad it could fit any passage. Examiners plant one too-narrow and one too-broad option as traps. The answer is the one that covers the whole passage without being too broad or too narrow.
Source: PYQ-style

37. Choose the word opposite in meaning to "scarce":
(a) Abundant (b) Rare (c) Costly (d) Fragile
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Scarce" means in short supply; its antonym is "abundant". "Rare" is a synonym and is planted to catch students who misread the question as asking for a synonym. The answer is abundant.

38. In "The rise of artificial intelligence has transformed industries," the subject of the sentence is:
(a) Artificial intelligence (b) Industries (c) Has transformed (d) The rise
Answer: (d)
Solution: The head noun of the subject phrase is "the rise"; "of artificial intelligence" is only an intervening phrase describing it. Picking "artificial intelligence" is the classic intervening-phrase trap. The answer is the rise.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English Grammar

39. When a question asks "According to the passage…", the answer must be:
(a) Your own opinion on the topic (b) Directly supported by the passage text (c) General knowledge about the topic (d) The most logical real-world option
Answer: (b)
Solution: "According to the passage" restricts you to what the text says, even if you know better from outside knowledge. Using real-world logic is the over-thinking trap. The answer is the option directly supported by the passage text.
Source: PYQ-style

40. Choose the correctly spelt word:
(a) Occurrence (b) Occurence (c) Ocurrence (d) Occurrance
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Occurrence" doubles both the c and the r (occur + ence, with r doubled after a stressed syllable). The single-r version looks plausible because "occur" has one r at first glance, but the derived form doubles it. The answer is occurrence.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English

41. Read the lines: "While urban India debates electric cars, nearly half of rural households still depend on firewood for cooking." The author's underlying concern is:
(a) The high price of electric cars (b) The health benefits of firewood (c) The unequal pace of development between urban and rural India (d) The growth of the automobile industry
Answer: (c)
Solution: The contrast word "while" pits urban luxury debates against rural basic needs — the concern is inequality, not cars or firewood themselves. The electric-car option is a detail trap. The answer is the unequal pace of development between urban and rural India.

42. A passage argues: "Remote work saves commuting time, but it blurs the boundary between office and home, often lengthening the working day." The author's attitude toward remote work is:
(a) Entirely positive (b) Mixed — acknowledging a benefit and a drawback (c) Entirely negative (d) Indifferent
Answer: (b)
Solution: The author names one gain (saved commute) and one loss (longer day), which is a balanced, mixed stance. Choosing "entirely negative" is the trap of noticing only the criticism after "but". The answer is mixed — acknowledging a benefit and a drawback.
Source: PYQ-style

43. Spot the error: "Neither of the two candidates (P) / have submitted (Q) / their documents on time. (R)"
(a) Part P (b) Part R (c) Part Q (d) No error
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Neither" is singular, so the verb must be "has submitted", not "have submitted". Part R looks suspicious because "their" follows a singular subject, but in modern usage "their" is acceptable; the firm error is the plural verb in Part Q. The answer is Part Q.
Source: NCERT Class 10 English Grammar

44. In an inference question, the safest answer is the one that:
(a) Uses strong words like "always" and "never" (b) Restates a sentence word for word (c) Introduces new information not in the passage (d) Is a moderate conclusion strongly hinted by the passage
Answer: (d)
Solution: Correct inferences are usually moderate and text-based; extreme words like "always" and "never" are classic trap markers. A word-for-word restatement is a fact, not an inference. The answer is the moderate conclusion strongly hinted by the passage.
Source: PYQ-style

45. Read: "The new dam will irrigate 40,000 hectares; however, it will submerge three villages." The word "however" tells the reader to expect:
(a) Additional supporting detail (b) A consequence that contrasts with the benefit (c) A summary of the passage (d) A definition
Answer: (b)
Solution: "However" is a contrast signal — a benefit is followed by a cost. Students who treat it as "in addition" fall for the supporting-detail trap. The answer is a consequence that contrasts with the benefit.
Source: PYQ-style

46. Choose the correct sentence:
(a) The news are very disturbing. (b) The news is very disturbing. (c) The news were very disturbing. (d) The news have been very disturbing.
Answer: (b)
Solution: "News" looks plural but is an uncountable singular noun and takes "is". The plural-looking ending is a sound-right trap that pushes students toward "are" or "were". The answer is "The news is very disturbing."

47. A passage on climate change ends: "Whether these measures will suffice depends not on technology, which exists, but on political will, which does not." The author implies that:
(a) Political commitment is the real obstacle to climate action (b) Technology for climate action is still undeveloped (c) Climate measures will certainly succeed (d) Technology is more important than politics
Answer: (a)
Solution: The parallel structure "which exists / which does not" says technology is ready but political will is missing — so the obstacle is political. The technology option reverses the passage, a classic near-correct trap. The answer is that political commitment is the real obstacle to climate action.

48. Spot the error: "One of my friends (P) / are coming (Q) / to the party tonight. (R)"
(a) Part P (b) Part R (c) Part Q (d) No error
Answer: (c)
Solution: The subject is "One", not "friends", so the verb must be "is coming". The plural noun "friends" sitting just before the verb is the intervening-phrase trap that makes "are" sound correct. The answer is Part Q.

49. In a fact-based question, the best first step is to:
(a) Re-read the entire passage slowly (b) Answer from general knowledge (c) Locate the keyword from the question in the passage and read the lines around it (d) Read the last paragraph only
Answer: (c)
Solution: Fact questions are answered by scanning for the keyword and reading its immediate context — this is the 15-second approach. Re-reading everything wastes time, and general knowledge is the over-thinking trap. The answer is to locate the keyword and read the lines around it.

50. Read: "Critics call the scheme a populist gimmick; supporters hail it as long-overdue reform. The truth, as usual, lies somewhere in between." The author's tone is:
(a) Biased toward the critics (b) Biased toward the supporters (c) Dismissive of the scheme (d) Cautiously neutral, weighing both sides
Answer: (d)
Solution: The author reports both camps and places the truth "in between", signalling deliberate neutrality. Picking either side is the trap of letting one quoted opinion colour the author's own voice. The answer is cautiously neutral, weighing both sides.
