# 📋 Important Grammar Rules & Tips — Error Spotting (Advanced)

## (a) 12 High-Yield Rules

| # | Rule | Example | Examiner's Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Subject-Verb Agreement with Intervening Phrases**: The verb agrees with the main subject, not the noun nearest to it. | The quality of the apples **was** (not *were*) good. | A plural noun just before the verb ("apples") lures you into a plural verb. |
| 2 | **Each / Every / Either / Neither + Singular Verb**: These distributives always take a singular verb. | Neither of the two boys **has** (not *have*) come. | "Of the two boys" sounds plural — the trap is the prepositional phrase. |
| 3 | **Collective Nouns**: Team, committee, jury, audience take a singular verb when acting as one unit; plural when members act separately. | The jury **has** given its verdict. / The jury **are** divided in their opinion. | Examiners mix both senses in one sentence to test whether you noticed the shift. |
| 4 | **One of the + Plural Noun + Singular Verb**: The noun after "one of the" is plural, but the verb stays singular. | One of my friends **has** (not *have*) gone abroad. | The plural "friends" next to the verb is the classic sound-right trap. |
| 5 | **Relative Pronoun Agreement**: In "one of the + noun + who/that/which", the verb agrees with the plural noun; but in "the only one of the…", it agrees with "one". | He is one of those students who **have** passed. / He is the only one of the students who **has** passed. | Adding "the only" flips the verb — a favourite advanced trap. |
| 6 | **Scarcely/Hardly…when, No sooner…than**: Fixed correlative pairs; never mix them. | No sooner had he arrived **than** (not *when*) it rained. | "No sooner…when" sounds acceptable in speech — it is wrong in exams. |
| 7 | **Inversion after Negative Adverbials**: Seldom, rarely, never, hardly, no sooner at the start → auxiliary before subject. | Seldom **did he** (not *he did*) come late. | The sentence "sounds right" without inversion in spoken English. |
| 8 | **Unfulfilled Wishes/Conditions**: "If I were", "I wish I were" — subjunctive *were* for all persons. | If I **were** (not *was*) a bird, I would fly. | "Was" is common in casual speech; exams demand "were". |
| 9 | **Parallel Structure**: Items joined by and/or/but/not only…but also must be in the same grammatical form. | He likes reading, swimming and **playing** (not *to play*) chess. | The third item in a long list quietly breaks the pattern — check the whole list. |
| 10 | **Comparisons must be Logical and Complete**: Compare like with like; use "that of / those of" where needed. | The climate of Delhi is hotter than **that of** (not *than*) Shimla. | Comparing "climate" with "Shimla" is illogical but easy to miss. |
| 11 | **Adjectives after Linking Verbs**: Verbs of sensation/state (look, seem, feel, taste, smell, sound) take adjectives, not adverbs. | The soup tastes **good** (not *well*). | "Well" feels grammatically "polished" — that is exactly the trap. |
| 12 | **Redundant Pairs**: Return back, repeat again, revert back, enter into (a room), free gift — one word is superfluous. | Please **return** (not *return back*) my book. | These are so common in Indian English that they pass unnoticed. |

## (b) Time Management — The 15/45-Second Rule

**Fact-based questions (target: 15 seconds)** — a single rule decides the answer; no context needed:
- Subject-verb agreement (Rules 1–5)
- Fixed correlative pairs and preposition pairs (Rule 6)
- Redundancy errors (Rule 12)
- Article and determiner errors
- Wrong tense form in a simple time marker ("since 2010", "yesterday")

**Inference-based questions (target: 45 seconds)** — you must read the whole sentence, sometimes twice:
- Parallel structure across long lists (Rule 9)
- Logical comparison errors (Rule 10)
- Modifier placement (dangling/misplaced modifiers)
- Pronoun reference and ambiguity
- Mixed errors where two parts look suspicious and you must pick the actual error

**Strategy:** Attempt all 15-second questions first in one sweep. Mark inference-based ones and return with fresh eyes. Never spend more than 60 seconds on any single error-spotting question — the cost-benefit ratio collapses after that.

## (c) Morphology — Root, Affix, Example

Knowing word structure helps you spot wrongly formed words (e.g., *irregardless*, *unloosen*) and guess meanings in tough sentences.

| Root / Affix | Meaning | Example Words |
|---|---|---|
| **-dict-** (Latin) | say, speak | predict, contradict, verdict, dictator |
| **-port-** (Latin) | carry | transport, export, portable, support |
| **-spect-** (Latin) | look, see | inspect, respect, spectator, perspective |
| **-rupt-** (Latin) | break | interrupt, bankrupt, erupt, corrupt |
| **-cred-** (Latin) | believe | credible, incredible, credit, credulous |
| **-bio-** (Greek) | life | biology, biography, antibiotic |
| **-chron-** (Greek) | time | chronology, synchronize, chronic |
| **-phon-** (Greek) | sound | telephone, symphony, phonetics |
| **mis-** (prefix) | wrongly | mislead, misinterpret, misplace |
| **-able / -ible** (suffix) | capable of | readable, visible, edible |
| **-tion / -sion** (suffix) | act/state (noun-forming) | action, decision, permission |
| **-ify** (suffix) | to make (verb-forming) | simplify, clarify, justify |

**Exam tip:** If an option contains a word that *looks* like a real word but violates formation patterns (e.g., "beautify" ✓ vs "beautifull" ✗, "irregardless" ✗), morphology is your fastest elimination tool.
