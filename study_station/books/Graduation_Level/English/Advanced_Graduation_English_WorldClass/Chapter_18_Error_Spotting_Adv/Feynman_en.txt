# The Feynman Technique — Learn by Teaching

## What is the Feynman Technique?

The Feynman Technique is a study method named after **Richard Feynman** (1918–1988), the American physicist and Nobel Prize winner (Physics, 1965), who was famous for explaining difficult ideas in simple words. The core idea is:

> **If you cannot explain a topic in simple language, you have not truly understood it.**

For SSC, Railway, Bank and State exam aspirants, this method is powerful because these exams test *clarity of basics* — not memorised paragraphs. If you can explain "Why does the monsoon arrive in Kerala first?" to a 10-year-old, you can answer any twisted MCQ on it.

---

## The Four Steps

### Step 1 — Choose a topic and study it
Pick one small topic (e.g., "Fundamental Rights", "Types of soil", "Simple Interest"). Read it from your NCERT/standard book as usual.

### Step 2 — Explain it to a child (or an imaginary one)
Take a blank sheet. Write or speak the explanation **as if teaching a 12-year-old**:
- Use simple everyday words.
- Avoid jargon. If you must use a term (e.g., "inflation"), define it in one easy line.
- Use examples from daily life (e.g., "Inflation means the ₹10 samosa of last year now costs ₹15").

### Step 3 — Find the gaps
Wherever you get stuck, hesitate, or use vague words like "something", "somehow" — **that is a gap in your understanding**. Go back to the book, re-read only that part, and fix it.

### Step 4 — Simplify and use analogies
Rewrite the explanation even more simply. Create an analogy:
- "The Constitution is like the rulebook of a cricket match — every player (citizen and government) must follow it."
- "Arteries are like water supply pipes going out from the pump (heart); veins are the return pipes."

If your explanation is now short, simple and example-based — the topic is yours for life.

---

## Why it works for competitive exams

1. **Kills false confidence** — Reading a chapter 3 times *feels* like learning. Explaining it exposes what you actually know.
2. **Builds statement-based MCQ skill** — When you truly understand a concept, you can spot "almost-correct" trap statements instantly.
3. **Improves memory** — Teaching forces active recall, which is far stronger than passive re-reading.
4. **Saves revision time** — A topic mastered this way needs only a quick glance before the exam.

---

## How to use it in your daily routine

| Situation | How to apply Feynman |
|---|---|
| After reading a chapter | Close the book. Explain the chapter aloud in 2 minutes. |
| A concept you keep forgetting | Write its explanation for a child; find the exact line where you get stuck. |
| Group study | Take turns teaching one sub-topic each. The "teacher" learns the most. |
| No study partner? | Teach an imaginary student, a wall, or record a voice note and listen for gaps. |
| Before the exam | Explain the hardest 5 topics of each subject in simple words — no book allowed. |

---

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Copying book language** — If your "explanation" is just the textbook sentence repeated, you skipped Step 2.
- **Skipping Step 3** — The gaps are the whole point. Note them down; they are your personal weak-spot list.
- **Using it only for theory** — It works for Maths and Reasoning too: explain *why* a formula works or *why* a shortcut is valid.
- **Doing it only in your head** — Writing or speaking aloud forces honesty; silent thinking lets you cheat yourself.

---

## Quick self-test

After finishing any topic today, ask yourself:
1. Can I explain it in under 2 minutes, in simple words?
2. Can I give one real-life example?
3. Can I answer "why?" at least two levels deep?

If any answer is "no" — that topic goes back to Step 3.

**Remember:** In the exam hall, nobody asks you to recite the book. They ask whether you *understood* it. The Feynman Technique is the fastest honest test of understanding you have.
