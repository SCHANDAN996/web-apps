# Important Rules & Tips

## (a) 12 High-Yield Rules

| # | Rule | Example | Examiner's Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find the **Mandatory Pairs** (fixed pairs) — two sentences that must come together because one continues the other's idea | "He was tired. **Therefore**, he went to sleep." — "therefore" forces the pair | A pair that *looks* linked but the pronoun in the second sentence has no noun in the first |
| 2 | The **opening sentence** never starts with a pronoun (he, it, they), a connector (but, so, however), or a reference word (this, that, such) | "Ramesh bought a book." can open; "**He** read it at once." cannot | An option placing a pronoun-first sentence at the start |
| 3 | **Pronoun–noun link**: a sentence with "he/she/it/they" must come *after* the sentence naming that person or thing | "The teacher entered the class. **She** began the lesson." | Two sentences with pronouns but no clear noun anywhere before them |
| 4 | **Article rule**: "a/an" introduces a noun for the first time; "the" refers to it later — so the "a" sentence comes before the "the" sentence | "I saw **a** dog. **The** dog was black." | Reversed order: "the dog" appearing before "a dog" |
| 5 | **Chronological/time order**: words like first, then, later, finally fix the sequence of events | "First he mixed the flour… **Finally**, he baked the cake." | A "finally" sentence placed in the middle of the sequence |
| 6 | **General → Specific**: a general statement opens; examples, details and explanations follow it | "Pollution is a serious problem. **For example**, Delhi's air…" | An example ("for instance…") placed before the general statement |
| 7 | **Connector logic**: but/however/yet need a contrasting idea *before* them; so/therefore/thus need a cause before them | "He worked hard. **However**, he failed." | "However" attached to a sentence that agrees with, not contrasts, the previous one |
| 8 | **Full name before short name**: the first mention uses the full name; later sentences use the surname or pronoun | "**Mahatma Gandhi** led the movement. **Gandhi** believed in truth." | The short name or "he" appearing before the full name |
| 9 | **Question–answer pair**: a sentence asking a question is immediately followed by the sentence answering it | "Why did he leave? **Because** he was ill." | The answer separated from its question by an unrelated sentence |
| 10 | **Closing sentence** often contains a conclusion, result, suggestion or summary word (thus, hence, in conclusion, overall) | "**Thus**, honesty is the best policy." | A concluding sentence placed in the middle of the paragraph |
| 11 | **Eliminate options**: once you fix one mandatory pair, reject every option that breaks it — usually 2–3 options die at once | If P→Q is fixed, delete all options where Q does not follow P | One option keeps the pair but breaks another link — check all links, not one |
| 12 | **Read the final arrangement once** to confirm the paragraph flows logically before marking the answer | Read the chosen sequence as a mini-story | An arrangement that is grammatically fine but logically absurd (effect before cause) |

## (b) Quick Strategy for the Exam

1. **Scan all sentences first** — do not start arranging from sentence one.
2. **Mark the opener** using Rules 2, 6 and 8.
3. **Lock 1–2 mandatory pairs** (Rules 1, 3, 4, 9).
4. **Eliminate options** (Rule 11) instead of building the full sequence from scratch.
5. **Verify by reading** the final order once (Rule 12).

## (c) Common Examiner Traps — Summary

- **Almost-correct order**: three sentences correct, one pair swapped — always check every link.
- **Pronoun without a noun**: a sentence starting with "it/they" placed before its noun appears.
- **Time confusion**: "finally/in the end" placed before the middle steps.
- **Similar names**: two people in the paragraph; the pronoun is attached to the wrong person.
- **Negative wording**: questions like "Which sentence does NOT follow logically?" — read the stem twice.
