# Word Roots & Morphology — Crack Any Word You've Never Seen

## 🎯 The Hook: One Root = Fifty Words

Imagine walking into the exam hall and seeing the word **"misanthrope"** in a synonym question. You've never memorised it. Panic? Not if you know two small pieces: *mis-* (hate) + *anthrop* (mankind) = one who hates mankind. Done. Five seconds. No dictionary, no guesswork.

That is the power of **morphology** — the science of how words are built. English has borrowed massively from Greek and Latin. Roughly 60% of English words (and up to 90% of scientific/academic vocabulary) carry Greek or Latin roots. Learn 50 roots and you unlock **thousands** of words. This chapter gives you the master keys.

## 🧱 Part 1: What Is Morphology?

A word is like a Lego model — built from blocks:

- **Root** — the core meaning (e.g., *spect* = to look)
- **Prefix** — added before the root; usually changes *direction/negation* (e.g., *in-*, *re-*, *anti-*)
- **Suffix** — added after the root; usually changes the *part of speech* (e.g., *-tion* makes a noun, *-able* makes an adjective)

**Example:** *unbelievable* = **un-** (not) + **believ(e)** (trust) + **-able** (capable of) → "not capable of being trusted."

> **Exam insight:** In vocabulary questions, the prefix usually tells you the *attitude* (positive/negative), and the root tells you the *topic*. Together they eliminate 2–3 wrong options instantly.

## 🧩 Part 3: Prefixes — The Attitude Switch

Prefixes are your fastest elimination tool. Learn them in families:

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|--------|---------|---------|
| anti- | against | antidote, antagonist |
| bene- / eu- | good | benefit, euphoria |
| mal- / mis- | bad / wrong | malnutrition, misfortune |
| hyper- | over, excess | hyperactive, hypertension |
| hypo- | under, less | hypothermia, hypothesis |
| inter- | between | international, intervene |
| intra- | within | intranet, intravenous |
| pre- / post- | before / after | preview, postpone |
| re- | again | rebuild, revise |
| un- / in- / dis- | not | unhappy, incorrect, disagree |

**Suffixes that change word class** (crucial for error-spotting):

| Suffix | Makes | Example |
|--------|-------|---------|
| -tion, -ment, -ness | Noun | action, movement, kindness |
| -ous, -ful, -ive, -able | Adjective | dangerous, hopeful, active, readable |
| -ify, -ize, -en | Verb | simplify, modernize, strengthen |
| -ly | Adverb | quickly, honestly |

## 📖 Part 4: Roots in Action — A Short RC Passage

Read this passage and notice how the golden roots appear in real exam-style English:

> *In an age of rapid technological change, the **chronology** of innovation has accelerated beyond anything historians could **predict**. Modern **anthropology** suggests that humans adapt not through strength but through **empathy** — our capacity to share the **pathos** of others. Yet critics warn of a growing **apathy** toward climate science, calling it a **malignant** indifference. **Portable** devices now **transport** information across continents in seconds, and a single **verdict** from a court can be **transcribed** and broadcast worldwide. Whether this **benevolent** connectivity will make us wiser, or merely more **credulous**, remains an open question.*

**Decode in context:**
- *chronology* (chron = time) → order of events in time
- *credulous* (cred = believe) → too ready to believe; gullible
- *malignant* (mal = bad) → harmful, evil in effect
- *apathy* (a- = without + path = feeling) → lack of feeling/interest

> **Detective Approach:** When a root-word appears in a passage, don't recall the dictionary — look at the *sentence around it*. "Critics warn of…" before *apathy* tells you it's something negative. Root + context = certainty.

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Sound-alike roots.** *Path* (feeling) vs *pathy* in "homeopathy" (disease-treatment); *phon* (sound) vs *phone* the device. Examiners pair **"sympathy"** with options like "understanding sound" to catch root-confusion. Always confirm the root's meaning, not the word's sound.

**Trap 2 — False friends / over-literal decoding.** *Invaluable* does NOT mean "not valuable" — it means "beyond value" (extremely valuable). *Infamous* means famous for something *bad*, not "not famous." When a prefix seems to flip meaning but the option feels odd, trust usage over mechanics.

**Trap 3 — Word-class confusion.** In error-spotting, examiners swap noun/adjective forms: *"He gave a **credible** speech"* ✓ vs *"He gave a **credulous** speech"* ✗ (credulous = gullible, describes a person who believes too easily). Same root, wrong suffix-family = wrong answer.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge (Hindi → English)

Hindi speakers often mis-handle derived word forms because Hindi builds words differently:

| ❌ Hindi-influenced English | ✅ Correct English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "He is very **credulousness** person." | "He is a very **credulous** person." | Adjective needed before a noun, not noun-form |
| "The **importance** of this is very high." (over-literal) | "This is very **important**." | Prefer the adjective form in simple sentences |
| "He **sympathy** with the poor." | "He **sympathises** with the poor." | *Sympathy* is a noun; the verb is *sympathise* |
| "What is the **chronolog** of events?" | "What is the **chronology** of events?" | Noun form ends in *-y*, not the bare root |

**Rule of thumb:** After "is/very/so" → adjective (*important, credulous*). After "the/his/their" → noun (*importance, credulity*).
