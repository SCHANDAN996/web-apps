# Sentence Structure — The Architecture of English

## Why This Chapter Decides Your Score

Every English question in SSC CGL, IBPS PO, or State PCS prelims is, at its core, a sentence-structure question. Spot-the-error tests whether you can see a broken subject–verb agreement hiding behind a long phrase. Sentence improvement tests whether you know which structure is grammatically legal. Para-jumbles test whether you can feel how sentences connect. Even cloze tests and comprehension reward the candidate who can break a long sentence into its skeleton in seconds.

Master the skeleton, and half the English paper becomes mechanical.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Identify the **subject, verb, object, and complement** of any sentence, however long.
2. Classify sentences as **simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex**.
3. Distinguish **phrases from clauses**, and independent clauses from dependent clauses.
4. Apply the **12 Golden Rules** that examiners test again and again.
5. Spot the classic traps: intervening phrases, inverted order, collective nouns, and "sound-right" errors.

---

## 1. The Skeleton: Subject + Verb

Every complete sentence needs at least one **independent clause** — a subject (who/what) and a finite verb (what happens/what is).

> **The committee / has submitted / its report.**
> (Subject) (Verb) (Object)

Everything else — adjectives, adverbs, prepositional phrases — is decoration. The examiner's favourite trick is to pile decoration between subject and verb so you lose track of the skeleton:

> **The committee, along with its advisors and consultants, has (not *have*) submitted its report.**

Strip the sentence to subject + verb before checking anything else.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In "The quality of the mangoes was good," what is the subject — *quality* or *mangoes*? Why does the verb stay singular?

## 2. The Four Sentence Types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause | She passed the exam. |
| **Compound** | Two+ independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon | She passed the exam, **and** her brother cleared the interview. |
| **Complex** | One independent clause + one or more dependent (subordinate) clauses | **Although** she was ill, she passed the exam. |
| **Compound-Complex** | Two+ independent clauses + at least one dependent clause | Although she was ill, she passed the exam, and her brother cleared the interview. |

A **dependent clause** begins with a subordinating conjunction (*because, although, since, when, if, unless, while*) or a relative pronoun (*who, which, that*). It cannot stand alone. Writing one as a full sentence creates a **fragment** — a classic error option.

🧠 **Active Recall:** "Because the train was late." — Is this a sentence? What is missing?

## 3. Phrase vs Clause — The Distinction That Wins Marks

- **Phrase:** a group of words **without** a subject–verb pair. (*in the morning, running fast, to win the prize*)
- **Clause:** a group of words **with** a subject–verb pair.

Three clause types examiners love:

1. **Noun clause** — acts as subject/object: *What he said* surprised everyone.
2. **Adjective (relative) clause** — modifies a noun: The book **that you lent me** is fascinating.
3. **Adverb clause** — modifies a verb: **When the bell rang**, the students left.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In "The man who came yesterday is my uncle," identify the clause type and the word it modifies.

## 4. The 12 Golden Rules of Sentence Structure

1. **Subject–verb agreement survives distance.** Phrases like *along with, as well as, together with, in addition to* do NOT make the subject plural. *The captain, along with his men, **was** rescued.*
2. **"And" joins, "or/nor" nears.** Two subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb; joined by *or/nor/either…or*, the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject. *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** present.*
3. **Each, every, everyone, everybody, someone, nobody** take singular verbs and singular pronouns. *Everyone **has** done **his or her** work.*
4. **Collective nouns** (team, jury, committee, family) are singular when acting as one unit, plural when members act separately. *The jury **has** given its verdict. / The jury **are** divided in their opinion.*
5. **Uncountable-looking plurals:** *news, physics, mathematics, measles* are singular. *Scissors, trousers, spectacles, wages* are plural.
6. **"A number of" = plural; "The number of" = singular.** *A number of students **were** absent. The number of students **was** forty.*
7. **Inversion after negative openers:** *Hardly, Scarcely, No sooner, Not only, Never, Rarely, Seldom* at the start invert subject and auxiliary. *No sooner **had** he arrived **than** the meeting began.*
8. **Fixed pairs:** *No sooner…than, Hardly/Scarcely…when, Not only…but also, Either…or, Neither…nor.* Mixing them (*No sooner…when*) is a guaranteed trap.
9. **Parallel structure:** items in a list or comparison must share grammatical form. *He likes reading, swimming, and **to cycle*** ❌ → *…and **cycling*** ✔
10. **Dangling modifiers:** the introductory phrase must describe the subject that follows. *Walking down the road, a snake bit him* ❌ (the snake wasn't walking) → *Walking down the road, **he** was bitten by a snake* ✔
11. **Relative pronoun agreement:** the verb inside a relative clause agrees with the antecedent. *This is one of the books that **were** stolen* (books → were).
12. **A sentence needs a finite verb.** Participles and infinitives alone cannot carry a sentence. *He being late* is a phrase, not a sentence.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back, recite the fixed pair for "No sooner" and the rule for "a number of" vs "the number of."

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Intervening Phrase:** *The behaviour of the children **were** excellent.* The plural word *children* sits next to the verb to seduce you; the real subject is *behaviour* (singular). **Always strip prepositional phrases before agreeing the verb.**

**Trap 2 — The Sound-Right Error:** *Neither of the boys **have** done it* sounds acceptable in speech, but *neither of / either of / each of* is formally singular → **has**. Exams test written grammar, not spoken habit.

**Trap 3 — The Inverted Sentence:** *Among the guests **was** a famous singer.* When a sentence begins with a prepositional phrase or *there*, find the real subject **after** the verb.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi से English

| हिंदी में हम कहते हैं | गलत English | सही English |
|---|---|---|
| मैंने उससे कहा… | I told him that why he was late. | I asked him why he was late. |
| तुम क्या कर रहे हो? | What you are doing? | What **are you** doing? (question = inversion) |
| उसने मुझसे कहा कि वह थका है | He said that he is tired. | He said that he **was** tired. (reported speech backshift) |
| मैं दिल्ली जा रहा हूँ कल | I am going to Delhi tomorrow, isn't it? | I am going to Delhi tomorrow, **aren't I**? |
| हालांकि वह बीमार था, लेकिन… | Although he was ill, **but** he came. | Although he was ill, he came. (although और but साथ नहीं) |

**Rule of thumb:** Hindi allows *although…but* together and keeps statement order in questions; English forbids both.

## 🧷 Mnemonic — "FANBOYS & WABBITS"

- **FANBOYS** (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) = coordinating conjunctions → join **independent** clauses (compound sentence).
- **WABBITS** (When, Although, Because, Before, If, Though, Since) = subordinating conjunctions → create **dependent** clauses (complex sentence).

If a clause starts with a WABBIT, it cannot stand alone — it is "waiting" for its main clause.

## Summary Table

| Concept | Key Test | Quick Check |
|---|---|---|
| Independent clause | Can stand alone | Has subject + finite verb, complete meaning |
| Dependent clause | Cannot stand alone | Starts with subordinator/relative pronoun |
| Simple sentence | 1 independent clause | One finite verb core |
| Compound | 2+ independent clauses | FANBOYS or semicolon |
| Complex | 1 independent + dependent | WABBITS present |
| Agreement | Subject–verb match | Strip intervening phrases |
| Inversion | Auxiliary before subject | Negative openers, questions |
| Parallelism | Same grammatical form | Check lists and comparisons |
