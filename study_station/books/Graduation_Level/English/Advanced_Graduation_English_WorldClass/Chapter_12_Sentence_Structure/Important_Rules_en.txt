# 📋 Important Grammar Rules & Tips — Sentence Structure

## (a) 12 High-Yield Rules

| # | Rule | Example | Examiner's Trap |
|---|------|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | Every sentence needs a **subject + finite verb**. A phrase without a finite verb is a fragment. | ❌ "Running down the street." ✅ "He was running down the street." | Fragments disguised as sentences in Error Spotting — check for a finite verb first. |
| 2 | **Subject–Verb Agreement**: the verb agrees with the subject, not with the noun nearest to it. | "The quality of the mangoes **was** good." | Intervening phrase trap: "The box of chocolates **is** (not *are*) on the table." |
| 3 | **Inversion** after negative adverbs (Hardly, Scarcely, No sooner, Never, Rarely, Seldom, Not only). | "No sooner **had he** entered than it rained." | "No sooner he had entered…" — missing inversion is the classic trap. |
| 4 | **Hardly/Scarcely → when; No sooner → than.** Never mix the pairs. | "Hardly had I slept **when** the phone rang." | "No sooner… when" is always wrong. |
| 5 | A **clause** has subject + verb; a **phrase** does not. Identify clause type (noun/adjective/adverb) by the job it does. | "I know **that he is honest**." (noun clause, object of *know*) | "Where he lives" can be a noun clause or adverb clause — check its function, not its words. |
| 6 | **Subordinating conjunctions** (because, although, since, if, unless) make a clause dependent; it cannot stand alone. | ❌ "Although he tried hard. He failed." ✅ "Although he tried hard, he failed." | Two subordinated clauses joined without a main clause = fragment. |
| 7 | **Correlative conjunctions** come in fixed pairs: either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, both…and. | "Neither Ram **nor** Shyam came." | "Neither… or" and "Not only… and" are wrong pairings. |
| 8 | With **neither…nor / either…or / not only…but also**, the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject. | "Neither the teacher nor the students **were** present." | Agreement with the first subject is the trap. |
| 9 | **Parallel structure**: items joined by and/or/but/not only…but also must be in the same grammatical form. | "She likes **reading, writing and swimming**." | "She likes reading, writing and **to swim**" — broken parallelism. |
| 10 | A sentence has only **one main finite verb**; other verbs become non-finite (to-infinitive, -ing, past participle). | "He went there **to meet** her." | Double finite verbs: "He went there and **meets** her" (tense break). |
| 11 | **Question word order**: auxiliary before subject. In embedded/indirect questions, use statement order. | Direct: "What **are you** doing?" Indirect: "Tell me what **you are** doing." | "Tell me what are you doing" — the most frequent Hindi-speaker error tested. |
| 12 | **Complex vs Compound**: Compound = two independent clauses (and, but, or, so); Complex = one main + one or more subordinate clauses. | Compound: "He tried, **but** he failed." Complex: "**Although** he tried, he failed." | Exams ask you to "identify the sentence type" — count the independent clauses. |

## (b) Time Management — The 15/45-Second Rule

| Type | Question Nature | Time | Examples |
|------|----------------|------|----------|
| **Fact-based (15 sec)** | Direct recall of a fixed rule; answer visible at first reading. Decide fast, don't re-read. | ≤ 15 sec | Hardly…when / No sooner…than pairing; neither…nor agreement; identify phrase vs clause; fragment spotting; correlative pairs. |
| **Inference-based (45 sec)** | Needs analysis of the whole sentence — function of a clause, parallelism across a long sentence, sentence-type identification, subtle tense/agreement in long intervening phrases. | ≤ 45 sec | "Identify the type of clause in bold"; error hidden after a long intervening phrase; choosing the best-combined sentence in Sentence Improvement. |

**Strategy:** If a question is Fact-based and you don't recall the rule in 15 seconds, mark it and move on — re-reading won't create the rule in your memory. Save the 45-second budget for clause-analysis and long-sentence errors.

## (c) Morphology — Roots & Affixes for Structure Words

| Root / Affix | Meaning | Example Words |
|--------------|---------|---------------|
| **struct** (Latin, *struere*) | to build | structure, construct, instruct, destruction |
| **junct / join** (Latin, *jungere*) | to join | conjunction, junction, adjunct, subjoin |
| **sent / sens** (Latin, *sentire*) | to feel | sentence, sentiment, consent, sense |
| **frag** (Latin, *frangere*) | to break | fragment, fragile, fraction, fracture |
| **sub-** (prefix) | under, below | subordinate, subject, subclause |
| **co- / con-** (prefix) | together | coordinate, conjunction, connect |
| **-tion / -sion** (suffix) | act/state (noun-forming) | construction, inversion, completion |
| **-ive** (suffix) | having the quality of (adjective-forming) | subjunctive, declarative, imperative |
| **gram / graph** (Greek) | to write | grammar, diagram, paragraph |
| **syn-** (Greek prefix) | together | syntax, synthesis, synonym |

**Tip:** Knowing *sub-* (under) + *ordinate* (order) instantly tells you a "subordinate clause" is a clause of lower rank — it cannot stand alone. Morphology turns grammar terms into self-explaining words.
