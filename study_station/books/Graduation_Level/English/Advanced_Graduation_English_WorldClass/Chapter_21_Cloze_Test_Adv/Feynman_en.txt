🧒 Feynman Technique — Advanced Cloze Test

The Hardest Concept: "How do I fill a blank when two options look equally correct?"

Imagine you are 12 years old and you are playing a jigsaw puzzle. You have a gap in the middle of the picture. Two puzzle pieces look almost the same — same colour, same size. How do you pick the right one?

You don't stare at the pieces. You look at the picture AROUND the gap. The piece must match what is already there — the sky above, the road below, the tree on the left.

A cloze test is exactly this puzzle. The blank is the gap. The sentences before and after the blank are the picture around it. The "right-looking" wrong option is the piece that matches the colour but not the shape.

A Daily-Life Example

Read this: "Ravi was very tired, so he ___ to bed early."

Options: (a) went (b) goes (c) going (d) gone

All four are forms of "go" — like four puzzle pieces of the same colour. Now look at the picture around the gap: "was" and "so" tell you the story is in the past, and the sentence needs one complete action. Only "went" fits the shape. The other three match the colour (the verb "go") but not the shape (past tense, finite verb).

The Unforgettable Trick: "Read the Ring, Not the Blank"

Most students stare at the blank and ask, "Which word sounds good here?" Wrong question. The right question is: "What do the words around the blank demand?"

The Ring = one sentence before + the sentence of the blank + one sentence after. The Ring always answers three questions:

1. TIME — Is the passage in past, present, or future? (Fixes the tense.)
2. MOOD — Is the writer positive, negative, or neutral? (Fixes words like "however" vs. "moreover", "fortunately" vs. "unfortunately".)
3. GRAMMAR SHAPE — Does the gap need a noun, verb, adjective, or connector? (A preposition before the blank usually demands a gerund or noun; "the ___" demands a noun.)

If an option fails even ONE of the three Ring questions, throw it out — no matter how good it "sounds".

Why "Sound-Right" Fails You

Your ear is trained on spoken, informal English. The examiner knows this. So he plants an option that sounds natural in speech but breaks a written-English rule — like "discuss about" or "despite of". The Ring method protects you because you check grammar shape, not sound.
