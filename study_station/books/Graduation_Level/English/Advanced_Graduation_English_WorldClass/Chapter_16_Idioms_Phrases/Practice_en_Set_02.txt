# Idioms & Phrases — Practice Set 2 (Questions 26–50)
# Difficulty: Q26–40 Easy (recall) | Q41–50 Medium (application)

26. The idiom "to bite the dust" means:
(a) to eat in a hurry (b) to clean the floor (c) to suffer defeat or failure (d) to work in a mine
Answer: (c)
Solution: "To bite the dust" means to be defeated, fail, or come to an end. It has nothing to do with literally eating or cleaning dust.
Source: PYQ-style

27. "A blessing in disguise" refers to:
(a) a hidden curse (b) a good thing that seemed bad at first (c) a religious ceremony (d) a person in costume
Answer: (b)
Solution: The idiom describes something that appears unfortunate initially but turns out to be beneficial. "Disguise" here means the benefit is hidden, not that someone wears a costume.
Source: PYQ-style

28. Choose the correct meaning of "to let the cat out of the bag":
(a) to free a pet (b) to buy something expensive (c) to create chaos (d) to reveal a secret
Answer: (d)
Solution: The idiom means to disclose a secret, usually accidentally. It comes from an old market trick where a cat was substituted for a piglet in a bag.
Source: PYQ-style

29. "Once in a blue moon" means:
(a) every night (b) during a festival (c) very rarely (d) in difficult times
Answer: (c)
Solution: A "blue moon" (a second full moon in one calendar month) is uncommon, so the idiom means something that happens very rarely.
Source: PYQ-style

30. The phrase "to hit the nail on the head" means:
(a) to say exactly the right thing (b) to do carpentry work (c) to hurt someone (d) to miss the point
Answer: (a)
Solution: It means to describe a situation or problem with perfect accuracy. Option (d) is the exact opposite — a classic trap.
Source: PYQ-style

31. "To burn the midnight oil" means:
(a) to work or study late into the night (b) to waste electricity (c) to light lamps at a festival (d) to destroy documents
Answer: (a)
Solution: The idiom refers to working or studying late at night, from the days when oil lamps were used for light after dark.
Source: PYQ-style

32. "A red herring" is:
(a) a rare fish (b) a warning signal (c) an important clue (d) a misleading distraction
Answer: (d)
Solution: A red herring is something that diverts attention from the real issue — common in debates and detective fiction. Option (c) is the trap: it is a false clue, not an important one.
Source: PYQ-style

33. "To sit on the fence" means:
(a) to avoid taking sides (b) to relax outdoors (c) to guard property (d) to take a firm decision
Answer: (a)
Solution: The idiom describes someone who remains neutral and refuses to commit to either side of an argument. Option (d) is the opposite meaning.
Source: PYQ-style

34. "To break the ice" means:
(a) to start a quarrel (b) to end a friendship (c) to initiate a conversation in a social setting (d) to destroy evidence
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Breaking the ice" means easing initial social awkwardness and starting communication, especially among strangers.
Source: PYQ-style

35. The idiom "to cost an arm and a leg" means:
(a) to be worthless (b) to cause physical injury (c) to be very expensive (d) to require hard labour
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means something costs a great deal of money. The body parts are figurative — the idiom is about price, not injury.
Source: PYQ-style

36. "To be on cloud nine" means:
(a) to be extremely happy (b) to be confused (c) to be in danger (d) to be daydreaming
Answer: (a)
Solution: The idiom expresses a state of extreme happiness or elation. Option (d) is a mild trap — daydreaming is idle thought, not joy.
Source: PYQ-style

37. "To spill the beans" means:
(a) to reveal secret information (b) to make a mess (c) to waste food (d) to cook carelessly
Answer: (a)
Solution: Like "let the cat out of the bag," this idiom means to disclose a secret, often prematurely or accidentally.
Source: PYQ-style

38. "A bolt from the blue" refers to:
(a) a thunderstorm (b) a sudden, unexpected event (c) a planned surprise party (d) a fast runner
Answer: (b)
Solution: It means a sudden, shocking, unexpected occurrence — like lightning from a clear sky. Option (a) is literal; the idiom is figurative.
Source: PYQ-style

39. "To take something with a grain of salt" means:
(a) to season food lightly (b) to doubt the truth of something (c) to accept something completely (d) to preserve something
Answer: (b)
Solution: The idiom means to view a claim with scepticism rather than believing it fully. Option (c) is the opposite — a frequent examiner trap.
Source: PYQ-style

40. "To kill two birds with one stone" means:
(a) to be cruel to animals (b) to hunt efficiently (c) to waste resources (d) to achieve two aims with a single action
Answer: (d)
Solution: The idiom means accomplishing two objectives through one effort. It is about efficiency, not hunting.
Source: PYQ-style

41. Despite the initial losses, the investors decided to ______, hoping the market would recover.
(a) throw in the towel (b) hang in there (c) call it a day (d) jump the gun
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Hang in there" means to persevere and not give up, which fits "hoping the market would recover." "Throw in the towel" means to quit — the opposite of what the sentence requires.
Source: PYQ-style

42. The manager's sarcastic remark during the meeting really ______ with the junior staff.
(a) walked on thin ice (b) broke the ice (c) cut no ice (d) put on ice
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Cut no ice" means to fail to impress or have no effect — fitting a sarcastic remark that fell flat. "Broke the ice" (starting friendly conversation) is the trap; sarcasm does the opposite.
Source: PYQ-style

43. When the scam was exposed, the minister tried to ______ by blaming his secretary.
(a) face the music (b) hold his horses (c) bell the cat (d) pass the buck
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Pass the buck" means to shift responsibility or blame onto someone else — exactly what blaming the secretary shows. "Face the music" means accepting consequences, the opposite behaviour.
Source: PYQ-style

44. She speaks ______; you never have to guess what she really thinks.
(a) around the bush (b) her mind (c) a blue streak (d) through her hat
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Speak one's mind" means to say exactly what one thinks, matching "you never have to guess." "Talk through one's hat" means to talk nonsense — a trap for the unwary.
Source: PYQ-style

45. The new policy was ______; it pleased neither the employees nor the management.
(a) a feather in the cap (b) a shot in the arm (c) a dead duck (d) a bone of contention
Answer: (c)
Solution: "A dead duck" means a complete failure with no chance of success — the policy pleased nobody. "A bone of contention" means a cause of dispute, but the sentence stresses total failure, not argument.
Source: PYQ-style

46. He promised to support the project, but at the last moment he ______ and left us stranded.
(a) burnt his bridges (b) wore his heart on his sleeve (c) turned over a new leaf (d) got cold feet
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Get cold feet" means to lose nerve and withdraw at the last moment — matching "left us stranded." "Burnt his bridges" means cutting off all retreat permanently, which is stronger than mere withdrawal.
Source: PYQ-style

47. The two brothers finally decided to ______ and divide the ancestral property equally.
(a) bury the hatchet (b) hit below the belt (c) wash dirty linen in public (d) add fuel to the fire
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Bury the hatchet" means to end a quarrel and make peace, which fits the amicable division of property. "Wash dirty linen in public" means exposing private disputes publicly — the opposite tone.
Source: PYQ-style

48. ⚠️ Trap: "The project is not viable; we are just flogging a dead horse by investing more." Here the idiom means:
(a) working hard for future profit (b) wasting effort on something that cannot succeed (c) punishing someone severely (d) reviving an old plan successfully
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Flog a dead horse" means continuing to put effort into something already decided or dead — pure waste. Option (d) is the trap: a dead horse cannot be revived, so the effort is futile, not successful.
Source: PYQ-style

49. ⚠️ Trap: Choose the sentence where the idiom is used CORRECTLY:
(a) Since she had no preparation, she cleared the interview by the skin of her teeth. (b) He was so tired that he decided to burn the candle at both ends and sleep early. (c) The detective found a red herring which solved the case immediately. (d) To celebrate, they let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party.
Answer: (a)
Solution: "By the skin of one's teeth" means barely succeeding — consistent with no preparation. In the other sentences the idioms contradict the context: burning the candle at both ends means overworking (not sleeping early), a red herring misleads (not solves), and letting the cat out of the bag would ruin a surprise, not celebrate it.
Source: PYQ-style

50. The inquiry committee's report ______ the corruption charges, leaving no room for doubt.
(a) blew hot and cold on (b) gave a clean chit to (c) nailed the lie of (d) put the lid on
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Put the lid on" here means to suppress or settle the matter conclusively, fitting "leaving no room for doubt." "Gave a clean chit" means declaring innocent, which contradicts confirming the charges.
Source: PYQ-style
