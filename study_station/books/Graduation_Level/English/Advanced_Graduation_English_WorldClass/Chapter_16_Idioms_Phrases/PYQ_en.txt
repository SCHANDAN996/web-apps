# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Idioms & Phrases

## How Exams Test This Chapter (Pattern Analysis)

Idioms & Phrases is one of the most **predictable** vocabulary areas across SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS, GD), Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI), Railways (NTPC, Group D) and State PCS prelims. The question format barely changes:

**Format 1 — Direct Meaning (most common):** An idiom is given; pick the correct meaning from four options.
**Format 2 — Reverse Match:** A meaning is given; pick the idiom that expresses it.
**Format 3 — Sentence Usage:** Choose the sentence in which the idiom is used correctly (common in IBPS/SBI PO).
**Format 4 — Fill in the Blank:** Complete the sentence with the appropriate idiom.

## Which Sub-Topics Repeat Most Often

Based on the pattern of questions that recur across exams, examiners show a clear preference for:

- **Body-part idioms** — *by hook or by crook, to bite the dust, to wash one's dirty linen in public, a bone of contention, to burn one's fingers.*
- **Colour idioms** — *once in a blue moon, a red herring, black sheep, white elephant, green with envy.*
- **Animal idioms** — *a wild goose chase, to let the cat out of the bag, to kill two birds with one stone, a dark horse, to rain cats and dogs.*
- **Death/difficulty idioms** — *to kick the bucket, to face the music, to be in hot water, a hard nut to crack.*
- **Classical/Biblical-origin idioms** — *Achilles' heel, an apple of discord, to carry coal to Newcastle, a Herculean task* (favoured in CGL Tier-II and PO-level papers).

## The Examiner's Traps (How Wrong Options Are Built)

| Trap Type | How It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal Meaning Trap** | One option gives the word-for-word literal sense | *Kick the bucket* → "to strike a pail" ❌ (means *to die*) |
| **Sound-Right Trap** | An option that "feels" plausible but is wrong | *A red herring* → "an important clue" ❌ (means *a misleading distraction*) |
| **Near-Synonym Confusion** | Two options are close; only one matches the exact nuance | *To burn the midnight oil* = study/work late, not merely "to stay awake" |
| **Over-thinking Trap** | The simple, direct meaning is correct; students reject it as "too easy" | *At sixes and sevens* simply = in confusion/disorder |
| **Similar-Idiom Swap** | Options mix two similar idioms | *By fits and starts* (irregularly) vs *in fits* (sudden bursts of emotion) |

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions)

**Q1.** "To let the cat out of the bag" means:
(a) To free a trapped animal (b) To reveal a secret (c) To create chaos (d) To buy a pet
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to disclose a secret, usually unintentionally. Option (a) is the **Literal Meaning Trap**. *Source: PYQ-style (recurs across SSC exams)*
**Trap Type:** Literal Meaning

**Q2.** "A red herring" refers to:
(a) A rare delicacy (b) An important discovery (c) Something that diverts attention from the real issue (d) A warning signal
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** A red herring is a misleading clue or distraction. Option (b) is the **Sound-Right Trap** — it feels positive and plausible. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Sound-Right

**Q3.** "To burn the midnight oil" means:
(a) To waste resources (b) To work or study late into the night (c) To stay awake worrying (d) To celebrate all night
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom specifically implies productive late-night work/study, not mere wakefulness — option (c) is the **Near-Synonym Confusion** trap. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Near-Synonym Confusion

**Q4.** "Achilles' heel" means:
(a) A source of great strength (b) A fatal weakness in someone otherwise strong (c) A painful injury (d) An ancient Greek custom
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** From the Greek myth of Achilles, vulnerable only at his heel. Option (a) is the exact-opposite trap. *Source: PYQ-style (favoured in CGL Tier-II)*
**Trap Type:** Sound-Right

**Q5.** "Once in a blue moon" means:
(a) Very frequently (b) On a rare occasion (c) During a lunar eclipse (d) At midnight
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** A blue moon is rare, so the idiom means "very rarely." Option (a) is the antonym trap; (c) is the literal trap. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Literal Meaning

**Q6.** "To wash one's dirty linen in public" means:
(a) To do household chores openly (b) To discuss private quarrels before others (c) To admit one's mistakes (d) To live an honest life
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means exposing private/family disputes publicly. Option (c) is the **Sound-Right Trap** — admitting mistakes sounds noble but is not the meaning. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Sound-Right

**Q7.** "At sixes and sevens" means:
(a) In perfect order (b) In a state of confusion (c) In large numbers (d) At gambling odds
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** It simply means disorder/confusion. Students often **over-think** and pick (d) because of the numbers. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Over-thinking

**Q8.** "A wild goose chase" means:
(a) An adventurous hunt (b) A foolish and fruitless search (c) A long journey (d) A competitive race
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means a hopeless, pointless pursuit. Option (a) is the literal trap. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Literal Meaning

**Q9.** "To carry coals to Newcastle" means:
(a) To work hard for a living (b) To supply something where it is already abundant (c) To travel for trade (d) To waste fuel
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Newcastle was a coal-mining town; taking coal there is superfluous. Option (d) is the **Sound-Right Trap**. *Source: PYQ-style (PO-level)*
**Trap Type:** Sound-Right

**Q10.** "To bite the dust" means:
(a) To eat in a hurry (b) To suffer defeat or failure (c) To clean the floor (d) To behave humbly
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** It means to fail, fall, or be defeated. Option (d) is the near-synonym trap — humility is not defeat. *Source: PYQ-style*
**Trap Type:** Near-Synonym Confusion

## Applying the 15/45-Second Rule

**Q1 above (15-second, Fact-based):** "To let the cat out of the bag" is a direct recall question. You either know it or you don't. **Strategy:** If the meaning is in your memory bank, answer in under 15 seconds and move on. Never re-read the options twice — the literal option (a) is designed to waste your time.

**Q9 above (45-second, Inference-based):** "To carry coals to Newcastle" is less common and may be unfamiliar. **Strategy:** Spend up to 45 seconds decoding it logically — Newcastle is famous for coal, so carrying coal *there* is pointless → "supplying what is already abundant." Use the idiom's internal imagery to infer, then eliminate. If no logic emerges in 45 seconds, mark and skip — never gamble blindly on classical idioms.

## Key Takeaways

- Direct-meaning questions dominate; master the 150–200 most repeated idioms first.
- The literal option is almost always wrong — treat it as a decoy.
- Classical-origin idioms (Achilles' heel, apple of discord) appear more at CGL Tier-II / PO level.
- Body-part and colour idioms are the highest-yield clusters across all exams.
