# Idioms & Phrases

## 🎯 Why This Chapter Matters

SSC, Banking and State exams regularly include questions directly from idioms and phrases. The cruel part? These questions take only **15 seconds** if you know the idiom — and are **impossible to guess** if you don't. There is no grammar rule to derive "to bite the dust." You either know it or you don't. That makes this chapter the purest test of preparation — and one of the fastest scoring areas in the English paper.

This chapter gives you a system: learn idioms through **stories, roots and themes**, not random lists.

---

## 📌 Learning Objectives

After this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Recall the meaning of the most exam-relevant idioms instantly.
2. Eliminate the "literal meaning" trap option that examiners always plant.
3. Use Greek/Latin roots to decode unfamiliar words in the exam hall.
4. Distinguish between idioms, phrasal verbs, proverbs and one-word substitutes.
5. Apply the 15-second rule to idiom questions without overthinking.

---

## 1. What Exactly Is an Idiom?

An **idiom** is a fixed group of words whose meaning is **different from the literal meaning** of the individual words. "To kick the bucket" has nothing to do with buckets — it means *to die*.

Three properties define an idiom:

- **Fixed form** — you cannot change the words. "Kick a bucket" or "kick the buckets" is wrong.
- **Figurative meaning** — the real meaning is metaphorical.
- **Cultural origin** — most idioms come from history, sports, the Bible, Shakespeare, sailing or farming.

A **phrase**, in exam terms, is any fixed expression (e.g., *at large*, *in the long run*) whose meaning you must know as a unit. A **proverb** is a complete sentence giving advice (*A stitch in time saves nine*). Exams test all three under the heading "Idioms & Phrases."

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why can't you say "kick a bucket"? What two things separate an idiom from a proverb?

## 2. The Four Families of Exam Idioms

Instead of memorising alphabetically, group idioms by **theme** — the brain remembers stories, not lists.

**Family 1 — Body idioms:** *to wash one's dirty linen in public* (discuss private matters openly), *to have a bee in one's bonnet* (an obsession), *by the skin of one's teeth* (narrowly), *to turn a blind eye* (ignore deliberately).

**Family 2 — Animal idioms:** *a red herring* (a misleading distraction), *to let the cat out of the bag* (reveal a secret), *a wild goose chase* (a useless search), *to kill two birds with one stone*.

**Family 3 — Colour idioms:** *once in a blue moon* (very rarely), *a white elephant* (a costly but useless possession), *red tape* (official delay/bureaucracy), *in black and white* (in writing).

**Family 4 — War & sport idioms:** *to bite the dust* (to fail/be defeated), *to hit below the belt* (to act unfairly), *to throw in the towel* (to give up), *to win hands down* (to win easily).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which family does "red tape" belong to, and what does it mean? Give one body idiom meaning "narrowly."

## 3. The Literal-Meaning Trap — How Examiners Fool You

Every idiom question has four options. One is correct. One is the **literal meaning** (planted for weak students). Two are **plausible distractors** (related to the theme but wrong).

Example: *To have an axe to grind*
- (a) to sharpen a tool ← literal trap
- (b) to have a selfish motive ✅
- (c) to work very hard ← plausible distractor
- (d) to take revenge ← plausible distractor

**Strategy:** The moment you see an option describing the literal action, eliminate it. Then choose between the remaining figurative meanings.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In the example above, why is (d) "to take revenge" a tempting but wrong answer?

## 4. 🔑 Mnemonic: The "CINEMA" Hook for High-Frequency Idioms

Remember six exam favourites with one word — **CINEMA**:

- **C** — *Crocodile tears* = false sorrow
- **I** — *In the same boat* = in the same difficulty
- **N** — *Null and void* = having no legal force
- **E** — *Elephant in the room* = an obvious problem nobody discusses
- **M** — *Make both ends meet* = live within one's income
- **A** — *Apple of one's eye* = most beloved person

## 5. Greek & Latin Roots — Your Decoding Machine

Many formal words in exams are built on classical roots. Learn the root, unlock ten words.

| Root | Meaning | Derived Words |
|---|---|---|
| **dict** (L) | say, speak | dictate, predict, verdict, contradict |
| **port** (L) | carry | transport, export, portable, deport |
| **cred** (L) | believe | credible, incredible, credit, credulous |
| **spec/spic** (L) | see, look | inspect, spectator, conspicuous, perspective |
| **rupt** (L) | break | interrupt, erupt, bankrupt, corrupt |
| **mal** (L) | bad | malice, malnutrition, malfunction, malevolent |
| **bene** (L) | good | benefit, benevolent, benediction |
| **chron** (G) | time | chronic, chronology, synchronize, anachronism |
| **path** (G) | feeling/suffering | sympathy, apathy, empathy, pathetic |
| **phon** (G) | sound | telephone, phonetics, symphony, cacophony |

### 📖 Roots in Context — Read This Passage

*When the minister gave his **verdict** on the scheme, critics called his claims **incredible**. The project, meant to **transport** grain to drought-hit areas, had been **bankrupt** from the start. A **chronic** lack of funds and the **malice** of local contractors had **corrupted** every tender. Yet the minister's **benevolent** speech won public **sympathy**, and reporters who tried to **inspect** the records were **deported** from the district.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Find eight root-words in the passage and name their roots. What does "anachronism" mean, using the root *chron*?

## 6. ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Literal Option:** As shown above, the literal meaning is always present and always wrong. *To read between the lines* ≠ to read carefully; it = to find the hidden meaning.

**Trap 2 — The Near-Miss Idiom:** Exams confuse similar idioms: *at sixes and sevens* (in confusion) vs. *at the eleventh hour* (at the last moment); *a bed of roses* (easy situation) vs. *a bed of thorns* (painful situation). Learn confusing pairs **together**.

**Trap 3 — The Overthinking Trap:** Idiom questions are 15-second questions. If you know it, mark and move. Students who "reason out" an unknown idiom usually talk themselves into the literal trap. Unknown idiom → make your best guess in 15 seconds and skip ahead.

## 7. 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi Speaker's Corner

| In Hindi we think… | Wrong English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| "उसने बिल्ली को बैग से बाहर निकाला" (translating word-by-word) | He took the cat out from the bag. | He **let the cat out of the bag**. (fixed form) |
| "मैं बाल-बाल बचा" | I escaped by my teeth's skin. | I escaped **by the skin of my teeth**. |
| "वह मेरी आँखों का तारा है" | He is the star of my eyes. | He is the **apple of my eye**. |

**Rule:** Never translate a Hindi muhavara word-for-word into English. English has its own fixed idioms — learn the English form as a single unit.
