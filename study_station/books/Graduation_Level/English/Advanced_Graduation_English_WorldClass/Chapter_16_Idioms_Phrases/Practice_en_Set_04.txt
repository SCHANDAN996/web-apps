Practice Set 4 — Idioms & Phrases (Questions 76–100)
Difficulty: 20 Medium, 5 Hard | 2 Examiner-Trap questions included

76. To "burn the midnight oil" means:
(a) To waste fuel carelessly (b) To work or study late into the night (c) To stay awake due to anxiety (d) To celebrate till late night
Answer: (b)
Solution: The idiom refers to working or studying late at night, from the days of oil lamps. Option (c) is a tempting trap — staying awake is not the same as working.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

77. "A red herring" is:
(a) A misleading clue that diverts attention (b) A rare and valuable discovery (c) An obvious warning sign (d) A profitable business deal
Answer: (a)
Solution: A red herring is something that distracts from the real issue — common in debates and detective fiction. It has nothing to do with value or profit.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

78. To "let the cat out of the bag" means:
(a) To free a trapped animal (b) To start a quarrel openly (c) To create unnecessary chaos (d) To reveal a secret, usually by mistake
Answer: (d)
Solution: The idiom means to disclose a secret, often unintentionally. Do not confuse it with "to bell the cat," which means to take a risky step for everyone's good.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

79. "To sit on the fence" means:
(a) To avoid taking sides in a dispute (b) To guard one's property (c) To watch others work (d) To take a comfortable position
Answer: (a)
Solution: A fence-sitter remains neutral and refuses to commit to either side. The literal image of guarding property is the examiner's trap.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

80. "To hit the nail on the head" means:
(a) To hurt someone with harsh words (b) To say or do exactly the right thing (c) To finish a task forcefully (d) To repeat a mistake
Answer: (b)
Solution: It means to describe a situation with perfect accuracy. Option (a) is a sound-right trap based on the word "hit."
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

81. "A blessing in disguise" is:
(a) A gift given secretly (b) A person who pretends to help (c) A prayer answered late (d) A misfortune that turns out to be beneficial
Answer: (d)
Solution: It refers to something that seems bad at first but produces a good result. The word "disguise" tempts students toward option (b), which is wrong.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

82. To "beat about the bush" means:
(a) To search thoroughly (b) To work hard without result (c) To destroy evidence (d) To talk around a topic without coming to the point
Answer: (d)
Solution: The idiom describes indirect, evasive speech. Option (b) is a trap — wasted effort is not the core meaning; avoiding the main point is.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

83. "To wash one's dirty linen in public" means:
(a) To admit one's faults honestly (b) To discuss private quarrels openly (c) To clean one's reputation (d) To do menial work for others
Answer: (b)
Solution: It means to expose private or family disputes before outsiders. Option (a) is the trap — honest confession is different from publicising private scandals.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

84. "To have an axe to grind" means:
(a) To hold an old grudge forever (b) To be prepared for a fight (c) To possess sharp skills (d) To have a selfish motive behind an action
Answer: (d)
Solution: It means having a personal, selfish interest in a matter. Option (a) is close but wrong — a grudge is about revenge, while this idiom is about hidden self-interest.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

85. "To read between the lines" means:
(a) To read very quickly (b) To understand the hidden or implied meaning (c) To skip unimportant parts (d) To memorise a text
Answer: (b)
Solution: It means grasping what is suggested but not stated directly — a key inference skill. Option (c) is a sound-right trap.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

86. "A wild goose chase" is:
(a) A foolish and hopeless search (b) An adventurous journey (c) A competitive race (d) A hunting expedition
Answer: (a)
Solution: It describes a pointless pursuit of something unattainable. The word "adventurous" in option (b) is the examiner's trap — the idiom is negative, not exciting.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

87. "To take with a grain of salt" means:
(a) To accept something completely (b) To make food tastier (c) To doubt the truth of a statement (d) To add value to an offer
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means to view something with scepticism, not fully believing it. Option (a) is the exact opposite — a classic trap.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

88. "To burn one's boats" means:
(a) To destroy enemy property (b) To cut off all means of retreat and commit fully (c) To act in anger (d) To waste one's resources
Answer: (b)
Solution: From ancient warfare — burning boats after landing meant no going back. It signifies an irreversible commitment, not mere destruction or anger.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

89. "A bolt from the blue" is:
(a) A lucky escape (b) A flash of lightning (c) A brilliant idea (d) A sudden and unexpected event
Answer: (d)
Solution: It means a sudden, shocking event, like lightning from a clear sky. Option (c) is the trap — the idiom usually carries a negative or shocking tone, not inspiration.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

90. "To grease the palm" means:
(a) To flatter someone (b) To help someone in need (c) To bribe someone (d) To shake hands warmly
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means to give a bribe. Option (a) is the trap — flattery is verbal, while greasing the palm involves money or favours.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

91. "To be at sixes and sevens" means:
(a) To be in a state of confusion (b) To gamble recklessly (c) To be perfectly arranged (d) To quarrel over numbers
Answer: (a)
Solution: It describes disorder and confusion. Option (c) is the opposite — a deliberate examiner trap.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

92. "To kill two birds with one stone" means:
(a) To be cruel by nature (b) To take unnecessary risks (c) To achieve two aims with a single effort (d) To waste energy on trivial tasks
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means accomplishing two objectives through one action. The literal violent image is a distractor; the idiom is about efficiency.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

93. "To wear one's heart on one's sleeve" means:
(a) To dress fashionably (b) To pretend to be emotional (c) To display one's emotions openly (d) To be deeply romantic
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means showing feelings openly rather than hiding them. Option (d) is the trap — it applies to all emotions, not only love.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

94. "A white elephant" is:
(a) A costly possession that is useless and burdensome (b) A rare and precious possession (c) A symbol of good luck (d) A royal gift
Answer: (a)
Solution: A white elephant is expensive to maintain but of little use. Option (b) is the classic trap — rare does not mean desirable here.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

95. "To pour oil on troubled waters" means:
(a) To make a bad situation worse (b) To escape from difficulty (c) To waste money on problems (d) To calm a quarrel or disturbance
Answer: (d)
Solution: It means to pacify an angry or tense situation. Option (a) is the trap — "adding fuel to fire" means worsening; this idiom means the opposite.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

96. "To draw the line" means:
(a) To distinguish between two things (b) To sketch a plan (c) To set a limit beyond which one will not go (d) To end a friendship
Answer: (c)
Solution: It means fixing a firm boundary of acceptable behaviour. Option (a) is the trap — "to draw a distinction" is different from setting a limit.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

97. "To rest on one's laurels" means:
(a) To retire early with honours (b) To receive awards gracefully (c) To be satisfied with past success and stop trying (d) To depend on others' achievements
Answer: (c)
Solution: It carries a negative tone — complacency after earlier success. Option (a) is the trap; the idiom criticises stopping effort, not honourable retirement.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

98. "To hold water" (of an argument) means:
(a) To be valid and sound when examined (b) To be lengthy and detailed (c) To be emotional and moving (d) To be full of loopholes
Answer: (a)
Solution: An argument that "holds water" stands up to scrutiny. Option (d) is the exact opposite — a deliberate examiner trap.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

99. "To eat humble pie" means:
(a) To apologise and admit one's error humbly (b) To live in poverty (c) To accept a small share (d) To pretend modesty
Answer: (a)
Solution: It means making a humble apology after being proved wrong. Option (b) is a sound-right trap based on the word "humble."
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)

100. "To cross the Rubicon" means:
(a) To travel to a foreign land (b) To take an irreversible step from which there is no return (c) To face a dangerous enemy (d) To achieve an impossible victory
Answer: (b)
Solution: From Caesar crossing the river Rubicon in 49 BC, committing himself to civil war. It means passing a point of no return — not merely facing danger or travelling abroad.
Source: Standard idiom dictionaries (Oxford)
