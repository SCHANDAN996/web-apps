🧒 Feynman Technique — Voice, Explained Like You're 12

The Hardest Concept: "Why does the sentence change shape at all?"

Imagine your house. Two things can happen with the same event — Mom cooked dinner.

Version 1: "Mom cooked dinner." — You're looking at MOM. She is the star of the sentence. She does the action. This is ACTIVE voice.

Version 2: "Dinner was cooked by Mom." — Now you're looking at the DINNER. The dinner didn't do anything — it just received the action. This is PASSIVE voice.

Same event. Same people. The only thing that changed is WHERE THE CAMERA IS POINTING. Active = camera on the doer. Passive = camera on the receiver.

The Unforgettable Trick: The "Football Pass" 🏈

Think of the verb as a football.

- ACTIVE: The subject THROWS the ball. → "Ravi kicked the ball." (Ravi = thrower)
- PASSIVE: The subject CATCHES the ball. → "The ball was kicked by Ravi." (Ball = catcher)

So whenever you're confused, ask one question: "Is the subject throwing the action or catching it?"
- Throwing → Active.
- Catching → Passive. And every catcher sentence in English MUST wear its uniform: be-verb (is/was/been…) + V3 (the third form of the verb). No uniform, no passive. "The ball kicked by Ravi" ❌ — the ball is missing its "was" jersey!

Now the part that tricks even grown-ups: the TENSE never changes — only the be-verb carries it.

Watch the be-verb do all the work while "cooked" stays frozen:

- Mom cooks dinner → Dinner is cooked (present)
- Mom cooked dinner → Dinner was cooked (past)
- Mom will cook dinner → Dinner will be cooked (future)
- Mom is cooking dinner → Dinner is being cooked (continuous)

See? "Cooked" never moves. Only the little be-verb shapeshifts (is / was / will be / being). That's the whole secret of passive voice in one line: freeze the V3, let "be" carry the tense.

One more thing a 12-year-old would ask: "Why would anyone use passive at all?"

Because sometimes the doer doesn't matter! "My phone was stolen." — Do you know who stole it? No! So the phone (the receiver) becomes the star. Newspapers love this: "Three people were injured." Exams love it too.

⚠️ The trap examiners set for you: They hide the real subject. "The letter along with two parcels was delivered." Your eyes see "parcels" and scream "were!" — but the real subject is "the letter" (singular), so "was" is correct. In passive questions, always find WHO received the action first, then match the be-verb to it.
