🧒 FEYNMAN TECHNIQUE — PRONOUN

The Hardest Concept: Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement (and the "Intervening Phrase" trap)

Imagine you are 12 years old and playing cricket with your friends. You shout, "Rohan is batting!" Now, if you keep saying "Rohan, Rohan, Rohan" again and again, it sounds boring. So you say "he" instead. Simple — "he" is a pronoun, and "Rohan" is its antecedent (the noun it stands for).

Now here is the tricky part. Suppose you say: "Rohan, along with his two brothers, is playing." Notice — only ONE boy is batting (Rohan). The brothers are just standing with him. So you must say "he is playing," not "they are playing."

The examiner's favourite trick is to hide the real subject behind a crowd of extra words:

❌ "The quality of the mangoes were good."
✅ "The quality of the mangoes was good."

Who is good? Not the mangoes — the QUALITY. "Of the mangoes" is just noise, like the brothers standing next to Rohan. Cross it out mentally, and the sentence becomes: "The quality was good." Easy!

The Unforgettable Trick — "The Scissors Rule" ✂️

Whenever you see a long sentence, take a pair of mental scissors and CUT OUT everything between the subject and the verb (phrases like "along with," "as well as," "of the boys," "together with," "in addition to"). Whatever remains must agree — pronoun with antecedent, verb with subject.

Try it: "Each of the students has submitted his assignment."
Cut "of the students" → "Each has submitted his assignment." ✔️
("Each" is always singular — it points to one person at a time, like a teacher calling roll one by one.)

One More Daily-Life Picture

Think of a pronoun as a substitute player in football. A substitute can only replace ONE specific player — he must match that player's position. Similarly, a pronoun must match its antecedent in number (singular/plural), gender, and person. If the substitute runs onto the field replacing the wrong player, the referee blows the whistle — and in the exam, the examiner marks it as the error!
