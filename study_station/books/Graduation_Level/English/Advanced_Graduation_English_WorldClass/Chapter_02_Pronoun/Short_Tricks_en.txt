# 🪄 Short Tricks + Skip Strategy — Pronoun

## Memory Boxes (Mnemonics)

**📦 Box 1 — "FANBOYS take the same case"**
Pronouns joined by *and, or, but* must be in the **same case**.
✅ "You and **I** (not *me*) are friends." / "Between you and **me** (not *I*)."

**📦 Box 2 — "LET the object go!"**
After **let**, always use the **objective case**: *Let him and me go.* (Never "Let he and I go.")

**📦 Box 3 — "THAN/AS love the subject"**
In comparisons, mentally complete the verb: "He is taller than **I** (am)." If the hidden verb acts on the pronoun, use objective: "I love you more than **her** (= than I love her)."

**📦 Box 4 — "Each, Every, Either, Neither = SINGULAR forever"**
Mnemonic: **"E-E-E-N walks alone."**
✅ "Each of the boys has **his** book." (Never *their* in formal exam English.)

**📦 Box 5 — "One → One's → Oneself"**
If a sentence starts with **one**, every later pronoun must be **one/one's/oneself** — never *he/his*.
✅ "One should do **one's** duty."

**📦 Box 6 — "AVO → 231"**
Order of personal pronouns:
- **Good** things → **2-3-1** (you, he, I): "You, he and I did the work."
- **Bad** things/confession → **1-2-3** (I, you, he): "I, you and he are guilty."
Mnemonic: **"Praise others first; blame yourself first."**

**📦 Box 7 — "Who knocks, Whom receives"**
**Who** = subject (does the action); **Whom** = object (receives it).
Quick test: answer with *he* → **who**; answer with *him* → **whom**.
"____ did you invite?" → "I invited **him**" → **Whom** did you invite?

**📦 Box 8 — "It is I, not me"**
After the verb **to be** (is/am/was/were), use the **nominative case**: "It is **I** who called." (Formal exam rule, even though spoken English says "It's me.")

**📦 Box 9 — "Preposition + me"**
After a preposition (to, for, between, with, except), always objective case:
✅ "Between you and **me**" ❌ "Between you and I" — the examiner's favourite trap!

**📦 Box 10 — "Gerund wants a possessive"**
Before a gerund (V-ing used as noun), use **possessive** pronoun:
✅ "I object to **his** coming late." (Not *him coming*.)
Mnemonic: **"ING is mine/yours/his."**

**📦 Box 11 — "Relative pronoun trio: Who–Which–That"**
- **Who** → persons, **Which** → things/animals, **That** → both (restrictive clauses).
- After *all, only, superlatives* → prefer **that**: "This is the best book **that** I have."
- (After *same* and *such*, use **as** — see Box 14.)

**📦 Box 12 — "Reflexives never stand alone as subject"**
❌ "Myself went to Delhi." ✅ "**I** went to Delhi."
Reflexives (myself, himself) only reflect back: "He hurt **himself**."
Mnemonic: **"Self needs a mirror, not a throne."**

**📦 Box 13 — "Collective noun: united = it, divided = they"**
"The jury **has** given **its** verdict." (one body) vs "The jury **are** divided in **their** opinion." (members acting separately.)

**📦 Box 14 — "Same…as / Such…as"**
After **same** and **such**, the relative pronoun is **as**, not *who/which/that*:
✅ "This is the same book **as** I lost."

**📦 Box 15 — "None but takes singular; None of takes plural verb option"**
"None but the brave **deserves** the fair." — but "None of the students **were/was** present." When in doubt in exams, check what follows *none of*.

## ⏭️ Skip Strategy

**Tip 1 — Skip the "Sound-Right" trap in 15 seconds.**
If a sentence *sounds* correct in spoken English ("Between you and I", "It's me", "Him and me went"), treat that as a red flag, not a green one. Spoken comfort = exam trap. Apply the rule mechanically; if you can't recall the rule in 15 seconds, mark and move on.

**Tip 2 — Intervening-phrase questions deserve 45 seconds, not guessing.**
In "The captain, along with his men, ___ (was/were)…", the phrase between commas is a decoy. Strike it out mentally, match the pronoun/verb to the **real subject**, and answer. If two intervening phrases appear, this is a 45-second question — budget for it, don't rush it.

**Tip 3 — Skip "over-thinking" on case after than/as.**
Don't rewrite the whole sentence in your head. Just add the hidden verb once ("than I *am*"). If the sentence still feels ambiguous after one check, choose the nominative (I/he/we) — examiners test the nominative far more often — and move on. Never spend more than 45 seconds on a single pronoun question; pronoun questions are won by rule recall, not by deliberation.
