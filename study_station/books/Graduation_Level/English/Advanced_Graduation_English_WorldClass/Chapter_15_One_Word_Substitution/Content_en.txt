# One-Word Substitution (एक शब्द प्रतिस्थापन)

## Chapter Introduction

A one-word substitution is by far the most common vocabulary question type in SSC, Banking, Railway and State-level exams. The examiner gives you a phrase, a definition, or a description — and asks you to pick the single word that replaces it. The skill being tested is not guesswork; it is whether you have a ready stock of precise English words.

**The 15/45-second rule for this chapter:**

- **Fact-based questions (15 seconds):** You either know the word or you don't. If the definition matches a word you have memorised, mark it and move on in 15 seconds. Do not re-read the options hoping for inspiration.
- **Inference-based questions (45 seconds):** Some questions describe an unfamiliar situation. Here you earn your 45 seconds: break the description into parts, recall word roots (*-phobia* = fear, *-cide* = killing, *auto-* = self), and eliminate options whose roots do not fit.

**Skip strategy:** If after 45 seconds no root or meaning connects, skip the question and return later. Never spend two minutes on a one-mark vocabulary item.

## 1. How to Build One-Word Substitutions: Word Roots

Most one-word substitutions come from Latin and Greek roots. Learning 20 common roots unlocks hundreds of words.

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-cide* (Latin) | killing | homicide, suicide, regicide, infanticide |
| *-phobia* (Greek) | fear | claustrophobia, hydrophobia, xenophobia |
| *-phile / -philia* | love of | bibliophile, Anglophile |
| *auto-* | self | autobiography, autocracy, autograph |
| *bio-* | life | biography, biology, biodegradable |
| *chrono-* | time | chronology, chronic, anachronism |
| *-cracy* (Greek) | rule | democracy, autocracy, bureaucracy, theocracy |
| *-gamy* | marriage | monogamy, bigamy, polygamy |
| *-logy* | study of | zoology, etymology, anthropology |
| *omni-* | all | omnipotent, omniscient, omnivorous |
| *pan-* | all | panorama, pandemic |
| *-vorous* (Latin) | eating | carnivorous, herbivorous, omnivorous |

## 3. Common Errors of Hindi Speakers

A small box of "In Hindi we say this, but in English we say that" — direct translation traps:

| ❌ Wrong (word-for-word from Hindi) | ✅ Correct English |
|---|---|
| What you are doing? | What are you doing? |
| He is my cousin brother. | He is my cousin. |
| I am having a car. | I have a car. |
| He gave me a good compliment (when meaning "gift"). | He gave me a nice present. |
| My all friends came. | All my friends came. |
| She is more taller than me. | She is taller than me. |

**Why this matters for one-word substitution:** examiners often build options around literal translations. For example, the phrase "one who eats human flesh" tempts Hindi speakers toward a made-up word; the correct answer is *cannibal*. Trust the standard English word, not the translated instinct.

## 4. PYQ Pattern Analysis (What Exams Actually Ask)

Across SSC (CGL, CHSL, GD, MTS), Banking (IBPS, SBI) and Railway exams, one-word substitution questions follow predictable patterns:

1. **Definition-to-word format dominates.** "A person who speaks many languages — (a) Polyglot (b) Bilingual (c) Linguist (d) Multilateral." The trap is the *almost-correct* option: *linguist* (a language scholar) is close to *polyglot* but not the same.
2. **Root families repeat.** Questions cluster around *-cide*, *-phobia*, *-cracy*, *-gamy* and *omni-* words. Mastering the tables above covers the bulk of what recurs.
3. **Examiner's traps:**
   - **Near-synonym trap:** two options that look alike (*epitaph* vs *epigram*; *biography* vs *autobiography*). Read the definition twice — "written by oneself" is the deciding phrase.
   - **Negative-wording trap:** "That which *cannot* be avoided" — students who skim miss the *cannot* and pick the opposite.
   - **Similar-name trap:** *somnambulist* (walks in sleep) vs *somniloquist* (talks in sleep) — one root differs.
4. **Fact vs inference split:** roughly, direct definitions of common words are 15-second fact questions; unusual descriptions (e.g., "a government in which religious leaders rule") are 45-second inference questions where you decode the root (*theo-* = god/religion → *theocracy*).

## 5. Two Examiner's-Trap Examples

**Trap 1 — the near-synonym:**
"A notice of a person's death, usually with a short biography, published in a newspaper."
(a) Epitaph (b) Obituary (c) Elegy (d) Epilogue
**Answer: (b)** *Obituary* is a newspaper death notice. *Epitaph* is carved on a tombstone — the classic confusion pair.

**Trap 2 — the negative wording:**
"That which cannot be believed."
(a) Incredible (b) Credulous (c) Incredulous (d) Credible
**Answer: (a)** *Incredible* = cannot be believed (describes the thing). *Incredulous* = unwilling to believe (describes the person) — the trap option.

## 6. Revision Strategy

1. Learn the root table first (Section 1) — it converts memorisation into decoding.
2. Revise Sections 2A–2F in chunks of 10 words; test yourself by covering the right column.
3. In the exam, apply the 15/45-second rule strictly: known word → 15 seconds; root-decoding → 45 seconds; no connection → skip and return.
4. Revisit the Hindi-speaker error box (Section 3) before the exam — these exact confusions appear in error-spotting and substitution questions alike.
