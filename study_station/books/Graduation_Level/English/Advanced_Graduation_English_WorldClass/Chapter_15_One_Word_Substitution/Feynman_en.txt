# 🧒 Feynman Technique — One-word Substitution, Explained to a 12-Year-Old

## The Big Idea: English Loves Shortcuts

Imagine your school bag. Inside it you have a pencil, an eraser, a sharpener, a ruler, and a pen. Now imagine that instead of saying "my pencil, my eraser, my sharpener, my ruler and my pen" every single time, you just say **"my stationery."** One word. Five things. Done!

That is exactly what **One-word Substitution** is. English is a lazy-smart language. Instead of speaking a long sentence like *"a person who loves books and reads all the time,"* English just says **"a bibliophile."** One word replaces the whole sentence.

In your exam, the question gives you the long sentence, and four one-word options. Your job: pick the shortcut word.

## A Daily-Life Example You Will Never Forget

Think of your family WhatsApp group:

- Uncle ji who **talks too much** = **Garrulous / Loquacious**
- Aunty ji who **knows everything about everyone** (or thinks she does!) = **Omniscient** (one who knows all)
- Your cousin who **can write with both hands** = **Ambidextrous**
- Dadi ji who **lives to be 100 years old** = **Centenarian**
- That one relative who **eats everything — veg, non-veg, anything** = **Omnivorous**

See the pattern? You already *know* these people. The exam is only asking you to learn their English "nicknames."

## The Unforgettable Trick: Break the Word Like a Biscuit 🍪

Most one-word substitutions are built from small Greek/Latin Lego blocks. Break the word, and the meaning falls out:

| Block | Means | Word built from it |
|---|---|---|
| **OMNI** | all | **Omni**potent (all-powerful), **Omni**scient (all-knowing), **Omni**vorous (eats all) |
| **BIBLIO** | book | **Biblio**phile (book-lover) |
| **PHILE** | lover | Biblio**phile**, Philanthropist's cousin — Anglo**phile** (lover of England) |
| **AMBI** | both | **Ambi**dextrous (both hands), **Ambi**valent (both feelings) |
| **CENT** | hundred | **Cent**enarian (100 years), **Cent**ury |

**Memory hook:** *"OMNI is the ALL-mighty block."* In your home, God is omnipotent, omniscient — and at a wedding buffet, your uncle is omnivorous! 😄

So even if you see a new word in the exam, don't panic. Break it like a biscuit: **Auto + biography** = self + life-writing = the story of your own life. You just solved a question you never studied!

## The One Hard Part (and How to Beat It)

The tricky bit is that some words *sound* like they mean something else:

- **Fatalist** is NOT "one who causes death" — it is one who *believes everything is decided by fate*.
- **Invaluable** does NOT mean "without value" — it means *so valuable you cannot measure it* (priceless).
- **Notorious** does NOT mean "famous" — it means *famous for something bad*.

**Trick:** Whenever a word "sounds obvious," suspect a trap. Ask: *"Is the exam trying to fool me with the sound?"* That one question saves you 2–3 marks.

## 📝 Blurting Sheet Instruction

Close this book. Take a blank page. In **90 seconds**, blurt out (write fast, without thinking):

1. The 5 root blocks from the biscuit table above, with one word each.
2. The 5 WhatsApp-family words (garrulous, omniscient, ambidextrous, centenarian, omnivorous).
3. The 3 sound-trap words (fatalist, invaluable, notorious).

Now open the book and check. Whatever you missed — that is your real weakness, not what you *felt* you forgot. Rewrite only the missed ones three times.

**Question for you:** If **OMNI** means "all" and **POTENT** means "powerful" — can you guess what *omnipotent* means before looking it up? If yes, congratulations — you are no longer memorising words, you are *building* them. That is the whole game.
