Card 1
Front: What is the "Forgetting Curve" and who described it?
Back: Hermann Ebbinghaus showed that without review, we forget most new information within days — roughly half within a day and the majority within a week. Spaced revision flattens this curve.

Card 2
Front: What is Active Recall?
Back: Deliberately retrieving information from memory (closing the book and answering questions) instead of passively re-reading. Retrieval itself strengthens the memory trace.

Card 3
Front: What is Spaced Repetition?
Back: Reviewing material at increasing intervals — e.g., after 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days — so each review happens just before you would forget.

Card 4
Front: Mnemonic: What does "1-3-7-21" stand for in revision planning?
Back: The classic spaced-revision ladder: revise a topic 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, and 21 days after first learning it. Remember: "One-Three-Seven-Twenty-One keeps the forgetting on the run."

Card 5
Front: What is the difference between recognition and recall?
Back: Recognition = identifying the right answer among options (easy, passive). Recall = producing the answer from memory with no cues (hard, powerful). Exams need recall-level strength built through self-testing.

Card 6
Front: What is a "Revision Tracker"?
Back: A simple log (table, diary, or app) recording topic, date studied, dates revised, and confidence score — so no topic silently slips past its revision window.

Card 7
Front: Examiner's Trap: Why is re-reading your notes five times a weak strategy?
Back: It creates an "illusion of competence" — familiarity feels like mastery, but in the exam hall you must retrieve, not recognize. Trap type: Sound-Right (it feels productive but isn't).

Card 8
Front: What is the Feynman Technique in one line?
Back: Explain a concept in simple words as if teaching a child; wherever your explanation breaks down is exactly what you haven't understood — go back and fix that gap.

Card 9
Front: What is a "Blurting Sheet"?
Back: A blank page on which you write everything you remember about a topic from memory, then compare with your notes. The gaps you missed become your next revision targets.

Card 10
Front: What is the Testing Effect?
Back: The well-established finding that taking a test on material improves long-term retention more than spending the same time studying it again.

Card 11
Front: Mnemonic: What does "RACE" stand for when a revision session starts?
Back: Recall first → Assess gaps → Correct errors → Enter the next revision date in your tracker. "RACE through revision, don't crawl through re-reading."

Card 12
Front: How should you mark confidence in a revision tracker?
Back: Use a simple 3-level code — Green (confident, revise after long gap), Yellow (shaky, revise soon), Red (forgot, relearn today). Colour-coding directs effort where it's needed most.

Card 13
Front: Examiner's Trap: "I scored well in this mock, so I can skip revising these topics." What's wrong?
Back: One good score measures that day's performance, not durable memory. Without scheduled revision, the forgetting curve still applies. Trap type: Over-thinking/Overconfidence.

Card 14
Front: What is Interleaving?
Back: Mixing different topics or question types within one study session (e.g., grammar + vocabulary + comprehension) instead of blocking one topic for hours. It feels harder but improves discrimination and retention.

Card 15
Front: What is the ideal ratio of new learning to revision as the exam approaches?
Back: Early phase: mostly new learning with daily short revision. Final month: revision dominates — roughly 70–80% revision, 20–30% new practice. Never learn brand-new topics in the last week.

Card 16
Front: Why should you revise errors from mock tests before attempting a new mock?
Back: Unreviewed errors repeat. An error log converts every mistake into a targeted revision item; a new mock without review just re-confirms old weaknesses.

Card 17
Front: What is "chunking" in revision?
Back: Breaking a large syllabus into small, independently revisable units (e.g., "Tenses → Conditional sentences → Type 3") so each tracker entry is finishable in one short session.

Card 18
Front: How long should a single revision session ideally last?
Back: Short, focused bursts of 25–45 minutes with a brief break work better than marathon sessions; attention and encoding quality drop sharply after sustained fatigue.

Card 19
Front: What should you do if a topic turns "Red" (forgotten) twice in your tracker?
Back: Don't just re-read — change the method: use the Feynman explanation, make a flashcard, or solve 10 targeted MCQs. Repeated failure means the encoding method, not effort, is the problem.

Card 20
Front: What is the "2-Day Review" rule used in this book?
Back: After finishing any chapter, schedule the first revision within 2 days — this catches memory while it's still fresh and makes every later revision cheaper and faster.
