# ADJECTIVE — The Word That Paints the Noun

## 🎯 Why This Chapter Can Win or Lose Your Exam

Adjective errors are among the most frequently tested grammar points in SSC CGL, IBPS PO, and State PCS prelims papers. The cruel part? The sentence *sounds* correct. "He is more superior to me" feels fine to the ear — and that is exactly the trap. Adjectives are the examiner's favourite hiding place because Indian bilingual speakers carry Hindi patterns ("वह मुझसे ज़्यादा होशियार है" → wrongly translated as "more cleverer") straight into English. Master this chapter and you convert the examiner's favourite trap into your fastest marks.

## 📌 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Identify all **8 types of adjectives** and their correct position in a sentence.
2. Apply the **rules of comparison** (positive, comparative, superlative) without falling for double-comparison traps.
3. Use **adjectives as nouns** ("the rich", "the blind") and **nouns as adjectives** correctly.
4. Spot the **12 Golden Rules** violations in error-spotting questions within 15 seconds.
5. Distinguish confusing adjective pairs: *fewer/less, elder/older, latter/later, some/any, each/every*.

## 1. What Is an Adjective?

An **adjective** is a word that describes, qualifies, or limits a noun or pronoun. It answers: *What kind? How many? Which one? How much?*

- She bought a **red** saree. *(what kind?)*
- He has **five** pens. *(how many?)*
- **This** book is mine. *(which one?)*
- There is **little** water left. *(how much?)*

**Position matters.** Adjectives appear in two positions:

- **Attributive** — before the noun: *a **tall** building*
- **Predicative** — after a linking verb: *The building is **tall**.*

⚠️ Some adjectives are **predicative only**: *afraid, asleep, awake, alive, alone, ill, glad*. You cannot say "an asleep child" — you must say "a sleeping child" or "the child is asleep."

## 2. The 8 Types of Adjectives

| Type | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Proper** | From proper nouns | Indian, Shakespearean, Buddhist |
| **Descriptive (Quality)** | Kind/quality | honest, brave, beautiful |
| **Quantitative** | How much (uncountable) | some, much, little, whole |
| **Numeral** | How many / order | five, first, several, all |
| **Demonstrative** | Pointing out | this, that, these, those, such |
| **Distributive** | Each one of a group | each, every, either, neither |
| **Interrogative** | Asking (with noun) | what, which, whose |
| **Possessive** | Ownership | my, our, his, her, its, their |

**Key distinction the examiner tests:** An interrogative/demonstrative word is an **adjective** only when followed by a noun; otherwise it is a **pronoun**.

- **Which** book do you want? → adjective
- **Which** do you want? → pronoun
- **That** boy won. → adjective | **That** is true. → pronoun

1. "Each student was given a certificate." — what type of adjective is *each*?
2. In "Whose is this?", is *whose* an adjective or pronoun?

## 3. Degrees of Comparison — The Heart of the Chapter

**Positive** (no comparison): *Ram is tall.*
**Comparative** (two things): *Ram is taller than Shyam.*
**Superlative** (one among all): *Ram is the tallest boy in the class.*

### Regular formation
- Short adjectives: add **-er / -est** → tall, taller, tallest
- Adjectives ending in -y: **-ier / -iest** → happy, happier, happiest
- Long adjectives: **more / most** → beautiful, more beautiful, most beautiful

### Irregular forms (memorise — asked directly!)
| Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| good/well | better | best |
| bad/ill | worse | worst |
| many/much | more | most |
| little | less | least |
| far | farther/further | farthest/furthest |
| old | older/elder | oldest/eldest |
| late | later/latter | latest/last |

### 🔑 The Latin Comparatives — No "than", No "more"!
These adjectives are **already comparative** and take **"to"**, not "than":

> **superior, inferior, senior, junior, prior, posterior, anterior**

The verb **prefer** follows the same pattern — it takes **"to"**, not "than":

- ✅ He is senior **to** me. ❌ He is senior **than** me.
- ✅ I prefer tea **to** coffee. ❌ I prefer tea **more than** coffee.
- ❌ He is **more superior** to me. (double comparison — never!)

**Mnemonic 🧠: "SIS-PPP take TO"** — **S**uperior, **I**nferior, **S**enior, **P**rior, **P**osterior, **P**refer (+ junior, anterior) — all take **TO**, never *than*, never *more*.

### Absolute adjectives — no degrees at all
Some adjectives express a complete state and **cannot be compared**: *unique, perfect, universal, supreme, extreme, complete, empty, full, round, dead, chief*.

- ❌ This is the **most unique** painting. → ✅ This is a **unique** painting.
- ❌ His plan is **more perfect**. → ✅ His plan is **perfect** (or *nearly perfect*).

## 4. Elder vs Older, Latter vs Later, Fewer vs Less — The Killer Pairs

**Elder / Eldest** — only for **family members**, never followed by *than*:
- ✅ He is my **elder** brother. ✅ He is **older than** me.
- ❌ He is elder than me.

**Latter / Later** — *latter* = the second of two things (opposite of *former*); *later* = time:
- ✅ Of the two plans, I prefer the **latter**. ✅ I will come **later**.

**Fewer / Less** — *fewer* for countable, *less* for uncountable:
- ✅ **Fewer** students, **less** water. ❌ Less students.

**Farther / Further** — *farther* = physical distance; *further* = additional:
- ✅ Delhi is **farther** than Noida. ✅ For **further** details, call me.

### 🧠 Active Recall
1. "My elder sister is older than yours" — correct or not? Why?
2. Fill in: "There is ___ milk and ___ bottles." (less/fewer)

## 5. Adjectives Used as Nouns & Nouns Used as Adjectives

**Adjective + "the" = a whole class of people** (plural verb!):
- **The rich** should help **the poor**. ❌ The rich *is*…
- **The blind** deserve our sympathy.

**Nouns as adjectives stay singular** (no plural, no possessive):
- ✅ a **ten-rupee** note, a **five-year** plan, a **three-hour** journey
- ❌ a ten-rupees note, a five-years plan

**Order of adjectives** (when several come together) — remember **OSASCOMP**:
**O**pinion – **S**ize – **A**ge – **S**hape – **C**olour – **O**rigin – **M**aterial – **P**urpose
- ✅ a **beautiful small old round red Indian wooden** table
- ❌ a wooden red Indian old table

## ⚠️ EXAMINER'S TRAP BOX

**Trap 1 — Double comparison:** "more better", "most fastest", "more superior". The moment you see *more/most* + *-er/-est*, mark it wrong. Examiners bury it mid-sentence: *"She is more taller than her sister."*

**Trap 2 — "Than" after Latin comparatives:** *senior than, prefer…than, superior than* — always wrong. These take **to**.

**Trap 3 — Sound-right adjective/adverb swap:** "He did the work **good**" sounds fine in casual speech but is wrong — after the verb *did* you need the adverb **well**. But after linking verbs (*is, seems, looks, feels, smells, tastes*), the **adjective** is correct: "The soup tastes **good**" ✅ (not *well*).

---

## 🏆 The 12 Golden Rules of Adjectives

1. Latin comparatives (*superior, senior, prefer…*) take **to**, never *than*, never *more*.
2. Absolute adjectives (*unique, perfect, universal*) have **no degrees**.
3. **Elder/eldest** only for family; never with *than*.
4. **Fewer** + countable; **less** + uncountable.
5. "The + adjective" (the poor, the rich) = plural class → **plural verb**.
6. Noun-as-adjective stays **singular**: a ten-rupee note.
7. After linking verbs (*seem, look, feel, taste, smell, sound*), use an **adjective**, not an adverb.
8. Comparative degree compares **two**; superlative needs **the** + compares **three or more**.
9. "One of the + **superlative** + **plural noun**": *One of the best players*.
10. **Each/every** + singular noun; **all** + plural.
11. **Little** = almost none (negative); **a little** = some (positive); **the little** = whatever exists. Same for *few/a few/the few*.
12. Adjective order follows **OSASCOMP** — opinion before size before age before colour.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi से English

| Hindi speaker says ❌ | Correct English ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| He is more taller than me. | He is taller than me. | Hindi "और ज़्यादा लंबा" tempts "more"; English -er already means "more". |
| He is senior than me. | He is senior **to** me. | "मुझसे सीनियर" → "से" feels like "than", but *senior* takes *to*. |
| I have less friends here. | I have **fewer** friends here. | "कम" covers both; English splits countable/uncountable. |
| My all friends came. | **All** my friends came. / All **of** my friends came. | "मेरे सभी दोस्त" — *all* comes before the possessive. |
| He is my older brother. (for family) | He is my **elder** brother. | "बड़े भाई" = elder, not older. |
| The poors need help. | **The poor** need help. | "The + adjective" never takes -s. |
