# Fill in Blanks (Advanced)

## 1. Hook
Fill in the Blanks is not just a test of vocabulary. It is a test of your overall English — grammar, word choice, and how words naturally combine. Every blank you fill correctly shows that you understand the sentence as a whole, not just one word.

## 2. Learning Objectives
By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
- Choose the right word for the blank.
- Use grammar rules to fill blanks.
- Use root words to guess meanings.
- Avoid examiner traps.

Active Recall (🧠): What are collocations? Why is "make a crime" wrong? Pause and recall.

## 3. Chunked Learning

### 3.1 What the Blank Really Tests
A fill-in-the-blank question tests three things at once:
1. **Grammar** — the word must fit the sentence structure (tense, number, part of speech).
2. **Vocabulary** — you must know the meaning of each option.
3. **Collocations** — words that naturally go together in English.

### 3.2 Collocations: Words That Work Together
Collocations are natural word partnerships. Native usage decides them, not logic:
- **commit** a crime (not "make a crime" or "do a crime")
- **make** a mistake (not "do a mistake")
- **take** a decision / **make** a decision
- **heavy** rain (not "strong rain")
- **strong** tea (not "powerful tea")

If two options seem grammatically correct, the right answer is usually the one that forms a natural collocation.

Active Recall (🧠): Name three common collocations. Pause and recall.

### 3.3 Using Grammar Clues
Read the words around the blank before looking at the options:
- After "has/have" → past participle (gone, taken, written).
- After an article (a/an/the) → a noun is needed.
- After a preposition (in, on, of, for) → a noun or gerund (-ing form).
- Before a noun → an adjective is usually needed.

Example: "She has ______ the letter." Options: (a) write (b) wrote (c) written (d) writing. "Has" demands the past participle → **written**.

### 3.4 Using Root Words to Guess Meanings
When you meet an unknown word, break it into parts:
- **bene-** = good (benefit, benevolent)
- **mal-** = bad (malfunction, malicious)
- **pre-** = before (predict, preview)
- **-less** = without (hopeless, careless)

Even a rough guess at the meaning often eliminates two wrong options.

### 3.5 Examiner Traps
- **Near-correct words**: options that differ by one letter or one shade of meaning (e.g., "affect" vs "effect").
- **Grammar fit, meaning misfit**: a word that fits grammatically but makes the sentence illogical.
- **Two-part blanks**: both words must be correct — check the second word even if the first looks right.
- **Negative sentences**: words like "hardly", "seldom", "rarely" already carry a negative sense; adding "not" makes the sentence wrong.

Active Recall (🧠): Why can a grammatically correct option still be the wrong answer? Pause and recall.

## 4. Quick Strategy
1. Read the full sentence first — understand its tone (positive/negative) and logic.
2. Predict your own word for the blank before seeing the options.
3. Eliminate options that break grammar rules.
4. Between the remaining options, choose the natural collocation.
5. Re-read the complete sentence with your answer to confirm it sounds right.

## 5. Summary
- Fill in the Blanks tests grammar, vocabulary, and collocations together.
- Use the words around the blank as grammar clues.
- Learn common collocations — they decide many questions.
- Break unknown words into roots to guess meanings.
- Beware of near-correct options and double negatives.
