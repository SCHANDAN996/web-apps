# Conjunction — The Glue of English Sentences

## 1. What Is a Conjunction?

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words, phrases, or clauses. It does not add meaning of its own; it shows the **relationship** between the parts it joins.

- Ram **and** Shyam are friends. *(joins two words)*
- She is poor **but** honest. *(joins two qualities — contrast)*
- I waited **because** the train was late. *(joins two clauses — reason)*

Think of a conjunction as a **railway coupling**: coaches (clauses) are useless separately on a journey; the coupling makes them one train.

## 2. Type 1 — Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS)

Coordinating conjunctions join **equals** — word with word, phrase with phrase, independent clause with independent clause. There are exactly seven, remembered by the mnemonic **FANBOYS**:

| Letter | Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| F | **for** | reason | He stayed, for he was tired. |
| A | **and** | addition | Tea and coffee are served. |
| N | **nor** | negative addition | He neither called, nor wrote. |
| B | **but** | contrast | Rich but unhappy. |
| O | **or** | choice | Tea or coffee? |
| Y | **yet** | contrast (surprise) | Old, yet energetic. |
| S | **so** | result | It rained, so we stayed home. |

**Key exam point:** When two independent clauses are joined by a coordinating conjunction, a **comma** usually comes before it: *"The bell rang, and the students left."*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Recite FANBOYS. Which FANBOYS conjunction shows (a) result, (b) reason, (c) surprising contrast?

## 3. Type 2 — Subordinating Conjunctions

A subordinating conjunction joins a **dependent (subordinate) clause** to an **independent (main) clause**. The dependent clause cannot stand alone.

Common subordinating conjunctions, grouped by meaning:

| Relationship | Conjunctions |
|---|---|
| Time | when, while, after, before, until, since, as soon as, no sooner…than, hardly…when |
| Reason | because, since, as, now that |
| Condition | if, unless, provided (that), in case, as long as |
| Contrast/Concession | although, though, even though, whereas, while |
| Purpose | so that, in order that, lest |
| Comparison | than, as…as |

**Golden structural rule:** If the subordinate clause comes **first**, use a comma after it: *"Although he is rich, he is humble."* If it comes **second**, no comma: *"He is humble although he is rich."*

**The "because–so" trap:** Hindi logic allows "क्योंकि…इसलिए" together, but English **never** uses *because* and *so* (or *although* and *but*) in the same sentence:
- ❌ Because he was ill, so he did not come.
- ✅ Because he was ill, he did not come. / He was ill, so he did not come.
- ❌ Although he is rich, but he is not happy.
- ✅ Although he is rich, he is not happy.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why is "Although… but" wrong in English? Write one correct sentence using *unless*.

## 4. Type 3 — Correlative Conjunctions (The Paired Twins)

These work in **pairs** — and examiners attack the pairing:

| Pair | Usage | Example |
|---|---|---|
| either…or | choice | Either you work or you fail. |
| neither…nor | negative choice | Neither Ram nor Shyam came. |
| not only…but also | addition (emphasis) | She is not only wise but also kind. |
| both…and | addition | Both Delhi and Agra were visited. |
| whether…or | alternatives | Whether he comes or stays, I shall go. |
| no sooner…than | immediate sequence | No sooner did she arrive than it rained. |
| hardly/scarcely…when | immediate sequence | Hardly had he slept when the phone rang. |
| rather…than | preference | I would rather die than beg. |
| such…as / such…that | example / result | Such was his speed that none could catch him. |

**The Proximity Rule (verb agreement):** With *either…or* and *neither…nor*, the verb agrees with the subject **nearer** to it:
- Neither the teacher **nor the students were** present.
- Neither the students **nor the teacher was** present.

**The Parallelism Rule:** Both halves of a correlative pair must join the **same grammatical structure**:
- ❌ He not only plays cricket but also football. *(plays only cricket? ambiguous)*
- ✅ He plays not only cricket but also football.

🧠 **Active Recall:** (a) Which word pairs with *no sooner* — *when* or *than*? (b) Correct this: "Neither he nor his friends was there."

## 5. Conjunction vs Preposition vs Adverb — The Look-Alikes

Some words change job depending on use — a favourite advanced-level trap:

| Word | As Conjunction | As Preposition |
|---|---|---|
| since | Since he left, I am alone. *(+ clause)* | since Monday *(+ noun)* |
| until | Wait until I return. *(+ clause)* | until Friday *(+ noun)* |
| for | He stayed, for he was ill. *(+ clause)* | for you *(+ noun)* |
| after | After he came, we ate. *(+ clause)* | after lunch *(+ noun)* |

**Test:** If the word is followed by a **subject + verb**, it is a conjunction; if followed by a **noun/pronoun only**, it is a preposition.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic — "FANBOYS on the BUS"

- **FANBOYS** = the 7 coordinating conjunctions (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So).
- **BUS** = the three big subordinating families: **B**ecause (reason), **U**nless (condition), **S**ince/So that (time & purpose).
- For correlative traps, remember: **"No sooner THAN, Hardly WHEN"** — *sooner* has a **T**, so it takes **T**han; *hardly* has no T, so it takes **when**.

---

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Wrong Partner:** "No sooner had he entered **when** the bell rang." ❌ The correct partner is **than**. Examiners swap *than/when* and *nor/or* constantly.

**Trap 2 — Double Conjunction:** "Because he was late, **so** he missed the train." ❌ One conjunction per relationship. Delete *because* or *so*.

**Trap 3 — Broken Parallelism:** "He came not only to see me **but also for meeting** my father." ❌ Structures must match: *"…not only to see me but also to meet my father."*

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi सोच, English बोल

| Hindi-speaker's common error | Why it happens | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| "Because I was tired, so I slept early." | Hindi: क्योंकि…इसलिए दोनों साथ | Because I was tired, I slept early. |
| "Although he worked hard, but he failed." | Hindi: यद्यपि…फिर भी | Although he worked hard, he failed. |
| "Neither he came nor he called not." | Hindi double negative: न…न | Neither did he come nor did he call. |
| "Unless you do not work hard, you will fail." | Hindi: जब तक नहीं | Unless you work hard, you will fail. *(unless already = if…not)* |
| "He as well as I are going." | Hindi treats both as subject | He as well as I **is** going. *(the verb agrees with the first subject, "He"; "as well as I" is only an addition)* |
