# The Feynman Technique — Learn by Teaching

## What Is It?

The Feynman Technique is a study method named after Richard Feynman, the Nobel Prize–winning physicist famous for explaining difficult ideas in simple words. The core idea: **if you cannot explain a topic in simple language, you have not truly understood it.**

For government-exam aspirants (SSC, Railway, Bank, State exams), this method turns passive reading into active learning — exactly what MCQ-based exams demand.

## The Four Steps

### Step 1: Choose a Topic
Pick one small, specific topic from your syllabus — for example, "Fundamental Rights", "Types of Rocks", or "Simple Interest". Write the topic name at the top of a blank page.

### Step 2: Explain It Like You're Teaching a Child
Write (or speak aloud) an explanation of the topic as if teaching a Class 6 student:
- Use simple, everyday words.
- Avoid jargon; if a technical term is necessary (e.g., "photosynthesis"), define it in one line.
- Use examples from daily life.

### Step 3: Find the Gaps
Wherever you get stuck, hesitate, or use vague words like "something like this" — that is a **gap in your understanding**. Go back to your NCERT book or notes, re-study only that part, and fill the gap.

### Step 4: Simplify and Repeat
Rewrite your explanation more simply and clearly. Use an analogy or a short story if possible. Repeat until your explanation flows without looking at the book.

## Why It Works for Competitive Exams

- **Active recall**: Explaining from memory strengthens retention far more than re-reading.
- **Concept clarity**: Exams like SSC CGL and IBPS PO ask statement-based questions that trap students who memorised without understanding.
- **Speed**: A well-understood concept is recalled in seconds during the exam.
- **Revision tool**: Your one-page simple explanations become excellent last-minute revision notes.

## How to Use It in Your Daily Routine

1. After studying a chapter, close the book and explain the main ideas aloud or on paper.
2. Teach a friend, a family member, or even an imaginary student.
3. Keep a "gap notebook" — list every point where you got stuck, and revise those weekly.
4. Before the exam, try explaining each chapter's key topics in under two minutes each.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Copying from the book**: That is transcription, not teaching. The book must stay closed.
- **Skipping Step 3**: The gaps are the whole point — they show you exactly what to re-study.
- **Using complicated language to sound smart**: Simple language is the test of real understanding.

## Quick Example

**Topic: Why does the Earth have seasons?**

Weak explanation (jargon-heavy): "Seasons occur due to the axial tilt and revolution parameters."

Feynman-style explanation: "The Earth is tilted like a spinning top leaning to one side. As it travels around the Sun in a year, sometimes the northern half leans towards the Sun (summer in India) and sometimes away from it (winter). That leaning — not distance from the Sun — is what gives us seasons."

The second version shows real understanding — and it is the kind of clarity that helps you eliminate wrong options in the exam hall.
