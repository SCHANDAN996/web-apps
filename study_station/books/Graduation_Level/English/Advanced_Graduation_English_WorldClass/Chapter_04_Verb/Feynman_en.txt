🧒 FEYNMAN TECHNIQUE — The Verb, Explained Like You're 12

Imagine a sentence is a football match. The noun is the player. But a player standing still is boring — nothing happens. The VERB is the kick. No kick, no match. No verb, no sentence. That's why every complete sentence in English must have at least one verb — it is the engine.

Now, the hardest part of verbs for exam students is not "what is a verb" — it's Subject-Verb Agreement when the subject tries to hide. Let me show you with a daily-life example.

🍛 The Plate of Biryani Trick

Suppose your mother says: "The plate of biryanis IS on the table."

Wait — biryanis (plural!) is sitting right next to the verb. Shouldn't it be "are"? NO. Because the real subject is "plate" (one plate). The biryanis are just passengers sitting inside the phrase "of biryanis". The verb only listens to the driver — "plate" — not the passengers.

This is exactly what examiners do. They stuff a plural noun between the subject and the verb to fool your ear:

❌ "The quality of these mangoes ARE good." (Sounds right — mangoes are!)
✅ "The quality of these mangoes IS good." (Subject = quality, singular.)

🎯 The Unforgettable Trick: "CHOP THE PHRASE"

Whenever you see a long sentence, take an imaginary knife and CHOP OUT everything between the subject and the verb (phrases starting with of, with, along with, together with, as well as, in addition to, besides, accompanied by). What remains must agree:

"The captain, along with his ten teammates, [CHOP] … was present."
Captain (singular) → was. The teammates are passengers. They don't drive the verb.

⚠️ One warning: "and" is NOT a passenger-phrase. "The captain and his teammates WERE present" — because "and" adds a second driver. Two drivers = plural verb.

🧠 Quick Recall: In "A bouquet of roses ___ (is/are) lovely" — who is the driver? (Answer: bouquet → is.)
