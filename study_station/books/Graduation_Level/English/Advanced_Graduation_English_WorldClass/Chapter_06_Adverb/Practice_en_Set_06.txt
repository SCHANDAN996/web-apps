126. Choose the sentence in which the adverb is placed correctly.
(a) He always drives carefully on highways. (b) He drives always carefully on highways. (c) Always he drives carefully on highways he. (d) He drives carefully always on highways always.
Answer: (a)
Solution: Adverbs of frequency (always, often, never) come before the main verb but after the verb "to be". "He always drives" is the standard mid-position placement.

127. Spot the error: "She only told me yesterday (P) / that she had finished (Q) / the project two days earlier. (R) / No error (S)"
(a) No error (b) Part Q (c) Part R (d) Part P
Answer: (d)
Solution: Part P has a misplaced "only". "Only" must sit next to the word it limits. If she told only me, write "She told only me yesterday"; if she only told (didn't show), write "She only told me". As written, the meaning is ambiguous — a classic examiner trap.

128. Choose the correctly framed sentence.
(a) He is enough tall to reach the shelf. (b) He is tall enough to reach the shelf. (c) Enough he is tall to reach the shelf. (d) He enough is tall to reach the shelf.
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Enough" as an adverb comes AFTER the adjective or adverb it modifies: "tall enough", "fast enough". Before a noun it comes first: "enough money".

129. Spot the error: "The patient is (P) / much better today (Q) / and can walk fastly. (R) / No error (S)"
(a) Part R (b) Part Q (c) Part P (d) No error
Answer: (a)
Solution: Part R is wrong. "Fast" is both an adjective and an adverb; "fastly" does not exist in standard English. Correct: "can walk fast".

130. Choose the sentence with the correct use of "hardly".
(a) Hardly he had reached the station when the train left. (b) Hardly he reached the station when the train had left. (c) Hardly had he reached the station when the train left. (d) He hardly had reached the station when the train left.
Answer: (c)
Solution: When a negative adverb (hardly, scarcely, rarely, never) begins a sentence, inversion is required: "Hardly had he reached… when…". Note "hardly…when", not "hardly…than".

131. Choose the correct option to fill the blank: "Scarcely had she closed her eyes ______ the phone rang."
(a) than (b) that (c) then (d) when
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Scarcely/Hardly" pairs with "when"; "No sooner" pairs with "than". Mixing them is a favourite examiner trap.

132. Spot the error: "No sooner did the bell ring (P) / when the students (Q) / rushed out of the class. (R) / No error (S)"
(a) Part P (b) No error (c) Part R (d) Part Q
Answer: (d)
Solution: Part Q should begin with "than", not "when". The correct pair is "No sooner…than". "When" belongs with hardly/scarcely.

133. Choose the sentence that uses "quite" correctly to mean "completely".
(a) He is quite a tall boy in the class. (b) The task is quite impossible for one man. (c) She quite sings well in the morning. (d) Quite he is tired after work.
Answer: (b)
Solution: With "absolute" adjectives (impossible, dead, perfect, empty), "quite" means "completely". With gradable adjectives it means "fairly". "Quite impossible" = completely impossible — correct usage.

134. Choose the correctly punctuated and placed adverb sentence.
(a) He speaks very fluently English. (b) He speaks English very fluently. (c) Very fluently he speaks English fluently. (d) He very speaks fluently English.
Answer: (b)
Solution: An adverb of manner should not separate a verb from its object. "Speaks English very fluently" keeps verb + object together — the standard rule examiners test.

135. Spot the error: "He is too good (P) / a player to be (Q) / dropped from the team. (R) / No error (S)"
(a) No error (b) Part Q (c) Part R (d) Part P
Answer: (a)
Solution: The sentence is correct. "Too + adjective + a/an + noun" is a valid structure: "too good a player". Do not confuse with "too…to" negative sense; here "to be dropped" fits the pattern.

136. Choose the correct sentence.
(a) He is too weak that he cannot walk. (b) He is too weak to walk. (c) He is so weak to walk. (d) He is much too weak that he walks.
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Too…to" expresses an excessive degree with a negative result: "too weak to walk" = so weak that he cannot walk. "Too…that" is ungrammatical.
Source: NCERT-based grammar

137. Choose the correct transformation: "She is so tired that she cannot work."
(a) She is too tired to work. (b) She is too tired that she works. (c) She is very tired to work. (d) She is enough tired to work.
Answer: (a)
Solution: "So…that…cannot" converts to "too…to". "So tired that she cannot work" = "too tired to work".

139. Choose the sentence where "rather" is used correctly.
(a) He rather is a lazy boy. (b) It is rather a cold day today. (c) She sings rather she dances. (d) Rather he came late yesterday night.

Solution: "Rather" can precede "a/an + adjective + noun": "rather a cold day" (also "a rather cold day"). It is a degree adverb meaning "fairly/to some extent".

Solution: Part Q should be "each other" — used for two persons; "one another" is traditionally used for more than two. This distinction is a repeated exam favourite.

142. Choose the sentence with the correct adverb form.
(a) He behaved cowardly in the crisis. (b) He behaved cowardlily in the crisis. (c) He behaved in a cowardly manner in the crisis. (d) He behaved coward in the crisis.
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Cowardly" is an adjective, not an adverb (like friendly, costly, lively). To express manner, use "in a cowardly manner/way". "Cowardlily" is not standard.

(a) Only she then (b) Then only she (c) Only then (d) She only then
Answer: (c)
Solution: When "only then/only after/only when" begins a sentence, inversion follows: "Only then did she realise…". This negative/limiting adverbial inversion is a hard-level trap.

144. Spot the error: "He is working (P) / hardly these days (Q) / to pass the examination. (R) / No error (S)"

145. Choose the sentence where "yet" is used correctly.

Answer: (a)
Solution: "Yet" meaning "up to now" is used in negatives and questions with the present perfect, usually at the end: "Have you finished yet?" / "I haven't finished yet."

Solution: The pattern is "so + adjective + that-clause". "Too…that" is wrong; "very" and "much" do not take a result clause with "that".
