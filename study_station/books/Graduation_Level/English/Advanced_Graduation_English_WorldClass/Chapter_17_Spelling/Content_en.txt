# Spelling Check — Mastering the Art of Correct Spelling

## Why This Chapter Matters

In SSC CGL, IBPS PO, and State PCS prelims, spelling questions look deceptively simple — four words, one wrongly spelt, pick it. Yet even strong candidates lose marks here. Why? Because English spelling is not logical; it is **historical**. Words came from Greek, Latin, French, and Old English, and each family brought its own spelling habits. The examiner knows exactly which habits confuse Indian aspirants — silent letters, doubled consonants, -able vs -ible, -ance vs -ence — and builds traps around them.

The good news: spelling is the **most rule-able** part of vocabulary. Most tested words follow a small set of recurring patterns. Master those patterns, and this chapter becomes quick marks in under 15 seconds per question.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Apply the core spelling rules (i-before-e, doubling, silent-e, -able/-ible, etc.) to unfamiliar words.
2. Recognise the 12 high-frequency trap families examiners recycle every year.
3. Use Greek and Latin roots to *predict* the spelling of words you have never seen.
4. Distinguish commonly confused pairs (principal/principle, stationary/stationery).
5. Answer fact-based spelling questions in 15 seconds with confidence.

## Part 1: Why English Spelling Is Irregular — and Why That Helps You

English absorbed vocabulary from many languages. Greek gave us *psychology, pneumonia, rhythm* (silent p, silent h, odd y). Latin gave us *separate, definite, privilege*. French gave us *bureaucracy, entrepreneur, liaison*. Old English gave us *knight, wrong, answer* (silent k, w, w).

**The strategic insight:** examiners do not test random dictionary words. They test words that sit at the *collision point* of these systems — where an Indian learner's phonetic instinct ("spell it as it sounds") fails. If you know the collision points, you know the question bank.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why is English spelling irregular? Name two language families that contributed silent letters to English.

## Part 2: The Core Spelling Rules

### Rule 1: "I before E, except after C" — with its famous exceptions

- **i before e:** believe, achieve, relief, niece
- **except after c:** receive, deceive, perceive, ceiling
- **or when it sounds like /ay/:** neighbour, weigh, vein, reign
- **Exceptions (memorise!):** seize, weird, height, foreign, leisure, their

### Rule 2: Doubling the final consonant (the 1-1-1 rule)

If a word has **1 syllable, 1 vowel, 1 final consonant**, double the consonant before -ing/-ed/-er:

- run → running | stop → stopped | big → bigger
- For longer words, double only if the **stress is on the last syllable**: begin → beginning, occur → occurred, refer → referred — BUT open → opened, visit → visited (stress on first syllable, no doubling).

### Rule 3: Silent-e rules

- Drop the silent **e** before a vowel suffix: make → making, hope → hoping, argue → arguing.
- Keep the **e** before a consonant suffix: hope → hopeful, sure → surely, move → movement.
- **Exceptions:** argue → argument, true → truly, whole → wholly, nine → ninth.

### Rule 4: -able vs -ible

- **-able** usually attaches to complete words: read → readable, comfort → comfortable.
- **-ible** usually attaches to Latin roots that are not standalone words: visible, terrible, possible, audible.
- When in doubt, -able is far more common — but the exam favourites are -ible words: *permissible, accessible, irresistible, negligible*.

### Rule 5: -ance/-ence and -ant/-ent

- **-ance/-ant:** relevant, abundant, attendance, resistance.
- **-ence/-ent:** independent, existence, occurrence, persistent.
- No perfect rule exists — these must be memorised in families. Note the trap: *independent* ends in -ent, never -ant.

### Rule 6: -ceed, -cede, -sede

Only **three** words end in -ceed: **exceed, proceed, succeed**. Only **one** word ends in -sede: **supersede**. Everything else takes -cede: precede, recede, concede, intercede.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Spell the past tense of "occur" and "open" — why does one double the consonant and the other not? How many English words end in -sede?

## Part 4: Greek & Latin Roots — Predict Spellings You Have Never Seen

Roots tell you both the **meaning** and the **spelling skeleton** of a word. If you know *chron* = time, you will never write "cronology".

| Root | Meaning | Derived words |
|---|---|---|
| **chron** (Gk) | time | chronology, chronic, synchronize, anachronism |
| **graph/gram** (Gk) | write | biography, autograph, telegram, grammar |
| **dict** (L) | say | dictate, predict, verdict, dictionary |
| **scrib/script** (L) | write | describe, manuscript, prescription, inscription |
| **port** (L) | carry | transport, portable, import, portfolio |
| **spect** (L) | look | inspect, spectator, perspective, retrospect |
| **phon** (Gk) | sound | telephone, symphony, phonetics, microphone |
| **vid/vis** (L) | see | video, visible, vision, revise, supervise |

### Roots in Context — Read and Spot the Words

> *In retrospect, the inspector's report reads like a chronicle of predictable failures. The manuscript he submitted described how portable recording devices had captured every phone conversation in the office. His verdict was blunt: the transport division's supervisor had revised the official chronology of events, and the inscription on the gate — visible in the video evidence — proved the biography of the project had been rewritten. What spectators once viewed as a symphony of coordination was, in fact, an anachronism: a system out of step with its own time.*

Every bold-root word from the table appears above. This is how you lock spelling into memory — **see the word working in a sentence**, not floating in a list.

## Part 5: Commonly Confused Pairs (Homophone Traps)

| Word A | Word B | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| **principal** (head/main) | **principle** (rule) | The princi**pal** is your **pal**; a ru**le** = princip**le** |
| **stationary** (not moving) | **stationery** (pens, paper) | st**a**tionary = **a**t rest; station**e**ry = **e**nvelopes |
| **complement** (completes) | **compliment** (praise) | compl**e**ment = compl**e**te; compl**i**ment = **I** like praise |
| **advice** (noun) | **advise** (verb) | adv**ice** = noun (like ice); adv**ise** = verb (like "ise" = action) |
| **affect** (verb) | **effect** (noun) | **A**ffect = **A**ction; **E**ffect = **E**nd result |
| **lose** (misplace) | **loose** (not tight) | **Lose** lost an 'o' |
| **quite / quiet** | — | qu**ie**t has "ie" like "lie still" |

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The "looks right" wrong spelling.** Options like *occurence, neccessary, embarassment, maintainance* all *look* plausible. The correct forms — **occurrence** (double c, double r), **necessary** (one c, double s — "one **C**ollar, two **S**leeves"), **embarrassment** (double r, double s), **maintenance** (e after t) — must be memorised as a "Most Wanted" list.

**Trap 2 — The all-correct twist.** Some questions ask "which word is correctly spelt?" with three wrong options. Candidates trained only to *find errors* freeze. Read the instruction twice before answering.

**Trap 3 — British vs American spelling.** Indian exams follow **British** spelling: colour, honour, centre, programme, travelled, defence. An option like "color" or "center" is *wrong* for your exam even though it is valid American English.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi Speaker's Spelling Habits

| Hindi-speaker tendency | Why it happens | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| Writing "recieve" | Hindi is phonetic — we write what we hear | **receive** (i after c → ei) |
| Writing "enviroment" | The 'n' is barely pronounced | **environment** |
| Writing "goverment" | Same silent-n problem | **government** |
| Writing "seperate" | We hear "sep-a-rate" | **separate** — "there's **a rat** in sep**a rat**e" |
| Writing "definately" | Pronunciation blurs the i | **definitely** — think "finite" inside it |
| Writing "wich / wich one" | Dropping the h of wh- words | **which** |

Rule of thumb: **English is not written as it is spoken.** Never trust your ear alone in a spelling question — trust the rule or the root.
