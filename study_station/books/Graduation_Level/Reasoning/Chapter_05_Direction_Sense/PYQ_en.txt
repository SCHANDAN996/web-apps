PYQ ANALYSIS — DIRECTION SENSE (CODED/DISTANCE)

HOW EXAMINERS TEST THIS CHAPTER
Direction Sense questions appear regularly in SSC (CGL, CHSL, GD, MTS), RRB (NTPC, Group D), Bank (IBPS, SBI) and State exams. They are almost always scoring questions — there is no formula to memorise, only careful step-by-step tracking. Examiners test this chapter in a few repeating patterns:

1. Pure direction tracking: A person walks X km in one direction, turns left/right, walks again, and so on. You must find the final direction faced or the straight-line distance from the starting point.

2. Coded directions: Directions are disguised with symbols or words — e.g., "A @ B means A is to the north of B", or "P % Q means P is 5 m east of Q". You must decode the language first, then solve like a normal direction problem.

3. Shadow-based questions: "In the morning, Ram's shadow falls to his left…" These test whether you know that shadows fall towards the west in the morning and towards the east in the evening.

4. Clock-based direction questions: "If south-east becomes north, what does west become?" The whole compass rotates by a fixed angle; apply the same rotation to the asked direction.

5. Distance + direction combined: The answer needs both — e.g., "5 km north-east of the starting point". These usually involve a right-angled triangle, so the Pythagoras theorem (3-4-5, 6-8-10, 5-12-13 triples) is used to find the shortest distance.

6. Meeting-point questions: Two people start from the same or different points, walk along given paths, and you must find where they meet or how far apart they end up.

WHICH SUB-TOPICS REPEAT MOST OFTEN
- Left/right turn chains with a final "which direction is he facing?" question — the single most common pattern.
- Shortest distance from the starting point using Pythagoras.
- Coded direction language (symbol-based) — increasingly common in SSC CGL and Bank exams.
- Shadow questions (morning/evening) — short, one-line questions, easy marks if the rule is known.
- Compass rotation questions ("north becomes east…").

EXAMINER'S TRAPS (परीक्षक के जाल)
- Left/right confusion after a turn: Once the person turns, "left" and "right" are relative to the NEW facing direction, not the original one. Most wrong answers come from applying turns to the old direction.
- "Towards" vs "from": "He walks towards the north" and "he comes from the north" are opposite movements. Read the verb carefully.
- Shadow reversal: Morning shadow → west; evening shadow → east. Options usually include the reversed pair to catch those who mix them up.
- Clockwise vs anticlockwise rotation: In "south-east becomes north" type questions, first fix the angle and direction of rotation, then apply it. Rotating the wrong way gives a plausible-looking wrong option.
- Distance trap: The question may ask for the distance from the STARTING point, but the options include the total distance walked. Straight-line (displacement) and total path length are different.
- Coded language misread: In symbol-based questions, one wrong decoding at the start ruins the whole chain. Decode every symbol on paper before moving.
- Negative wording: "Which direction is he NOT facing?" — rare but present; underline the NOT.

TIME STRATEGY
- 15-second rule: If the question is a simple turn-chain or shadow question, solve it mentally or with a tiny sketch within 15–30 seconds.
- 45-second rule: If it involves coded symbols, two people, or a Pythagoras calculation, draw a proper diagram and take up to 45–60 seconds — these are still worth the time because accuracy is high.
- Skip signal: If after one reading you cannot fix the starting direction (some questions hide it in a shadow or clock clue), mark it and return later instead of guessing.

HOW TO ATTEMPT (RECOMMENDED METHOD)
1. Fix north on your page (upwards) before reading further.
2. Track each move with a short arrow; label distances.
3. After every turn, re-mark what left/right now means.
4. For shortest distance, complete the right triangle and check for standard triples before calculating.
5. For coded questions, write the decoded meaning of every symbol first, then solve.
