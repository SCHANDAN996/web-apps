# Analogy — The Art of Seeing Relationships

## 1. Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Identify the **exact relationship** between a given pair of words — not just "some connection".
- Classify analogies into standard types (synonym, antonym, part-whole, tool-function, cause-effect, degree, etc.).
- Eliminate wrong options using the **sentence test** and **relationship-priority rule**.
- Spot the examiner's favourite traps: multiple plausible relations, double relations, and difficult vocabulary.
- Solve a standard analogy in **under 30 seconds**.

---

## 2. The Hook: Why Examiners Love Analogy

Here is a secret: an analogy question is the **cheapest question to set and the most expensive to get wrong**. The examiner needs only four words — "Doctor : Patient :: Lawyer : ?" — and yet this tiny question tests your vocabulary, your logic, and your ability to think under pressure, all at once.

That is why analogy appears in **every** major exam — SSC CGL, CHSL, Banking (IBPS/SBI), RRB NTPC, and state PCS prelims. And here is the good news: unlike current affairs, analogy has **no syllabus that changes**. The relationships are finite. Master the ~15 relationship types in this chapter, and no analogy question can surprise you.

---

## 3. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Most students lose marks in analogy not because they don't know the words, but because of one habit: **they pick the first option that "feels related."**

Example: **Bird : Nest :: Lion : ?**
(a) Den  (b) Forest  (c) Roar  (d) Cub

Many students pick (b) Forest — "lions live in forests, right?" But the relationship is *creature : its home/shelter*. A bird's home is a nest; a lion's home is a **den**. Forest is where a lion *may* live, but it is not its *made/identified shelter*. The examiner designed option (b) exactly for students who stop thinking too early.

The fix is one disciplined habit: **never match words — match relationships.**

---

## 4. Chunk 1 — What an Analogy Really Is

An analogy question gives you a pair of words with a definite relationship (A : B), and asks you to find the word that has the **same relationship** with a third word (C : ?).

The golden tool is the **Sentence Test**: make one precise sentence connecting A and B, then apply the *same sentence* to C.

- **Doctor : Hospital** → "A doctor *works in* a hospital." → Teacher : **School** ("A teacher works in a school.")
- If your sentence is vague ("Doctor and hospital are connected"), every option will look correct. Precision is everything.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what is the Sentence Test? Try it on: *Pen : Write :: Knife : ?*

## 5. Chunk 2 — The Master List of Relationship Types

Learn these types so well that you can name the relationship *before* looking at the options.

| # | Type | Example | Sentence |
|---|------|---------|----------|
| 1 | Synonym | Begin : Commence | B means the same as A |
| 2 | Antonym | Brave : Cowardly | B is the opposite of A |
| 3 | Part : Whole | Chapter : Book | A is a part of B |
| 4 | Whole : Part | Car : Engine | B is a part of A (order matters!) |
| 5 | Tool : Function | Scissors : Cut | A is used to do B |
| 6 | Worker : Workplace | Chef : Kitchen | A works in B |
| 7 | Worker : Product | Carpenter : Furniture | A makes B |
| 8 | Cause : Effect | Virus : Disease | A causes B |
| 9 | Degree / Intensity | Warm : Hot | B is a stronger form of A |
| 10 | Animal : Young one | Dog : Puppy | B is the young of A |
| 11 | Animal : Sound | Bee : Buzz | B is the sound of A |
| 12 | Animal : Dwelling | Horse : Stable | B is the home of A |
| 13 | Person : Quality | Hero : Courage | B is the defining quality of A |
| 14 | Country : Currency | Japan : Yen | B is the currency of A |
| 15 | Study / Science | Cardiology : Heart | A is the study of B |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Cover the table. Name the relationship type for: *Thermometer : Temperature* and *Novel : Chapter*. (Hint: one is tool-function, one is whole-part — which is which?)

## 6. Chunk 3 — The Order Rule (Where Most Marks Are Lost)

Analogy is **directional**. *Chapter : Book* (part : whole) is NOT the same as *Book : Chapter* (whole : part).

**Question:** Tree : Branch :: River : ?
(a) Water  (b) Tributary  (c) Sea  (d) Dam

Tree : Branch is whole : part. So we need river : its part → **Tributary** (b). Students who ignore order pick "Sea" (where a river ends) — a related word, wrong relationship.

**Rule:** Your answer must match the given pair in (i) relationship, (ii) order, and (iii) grammatical form (noun ↔ noun, verb ↔ verb).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why is "Nest : Bird" a wrong match for "Den : Lion" even though both involve animals and homes?

---

## 7. Chunk 4 — A Mnemonic to Remember the Types

Remember **"SPA-WTC-DAP"** — say it as *"Spa with tea, DAP!"*

- **S** — Synonym / Antonym
- **P** — Part–Whole
- **A** — Animal relations (young, sound, dwelling)
- **W** — Worker (workplace, product)
- **T** — Tool–Function
- **C** — Cause–Effect
- **D** — Degree
- **A** — Attribute (person–quality)
- **P** — Place-based (country–currency, country–capital)

When stuck, run through SPA-WTC-DAP mentally — one of these nine buckets covers almost every exam analogy.

---

## 8. Chunk 5 — Handling Tough Vocabulary

Advanced exams (CGL, IBPS PO) use words like *laconic, garrulous, ephemeral*. Strategy:

1. If you know 3 of the 4 words in the options, use **elimination** — the odd relationships fall away.
2. Guess the word's **positive/negative tone** from context; antonym questions often collapse to tone.
3. Never leave it blank if negative marking allows an educated guess — eliminate at least one option first.

🧠 **Active Recall:** *Garrulous : Talkative* — is this synonym, antonym, or degree? What would *Garrulous : Taciturn* be?

## 9. Summary Table

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Read the given pair; make a **precise sentence** linking them |
| 2 | Name the relationship **type** (use SPA-WTC-DAP) |
| 3 | Check the **order** (part→whole or whole→part?) |
| 4 | Apply the same sentence to the third word |
| 5 | Eliminate options that are "related but wrong relationship" |
| 6 | Verify grammatical form matches (noun/verb/adjective) |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Multiple plausible relationships.**
The examiner fills options with words that are *all* connected to the third word in some way. Only one matches the *given pair's* relationship. In "Bird : Nest :: Lion : ?", Forest, Roar, and Cub are all lion-related — but only **Den** matches *dwelling*. Always match the relationship, not the topic.

**Trap 2 — Difficult vocabulary to create panic.**
Words like *ephemeral, obdurate, pusillanimous* are chosen to make you feel the question is unsolvable. It usually isn't — the relationship is often a simple synonym/antonym. Use tone (positive/negative) and elimination. Panic, not vocabulary, is the real trap.

**Trap 3 — Double-relation questions.**
Some pairs carry **two** relationships, and the correct option must satisfy both. Example: *Doctor : Patient* — (i) professional : person served, (ii) the professional *charges* the person. "Lawyer : Client" fits both; "Teacher : Student" fits only the first (teaching is usually not per-service charged in the same sense). When two options look right, look for the **second, hidden relationship** — the examiner put it there on purpose.
