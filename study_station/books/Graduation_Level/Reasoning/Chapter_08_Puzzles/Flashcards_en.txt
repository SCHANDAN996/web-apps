Card 1
Front: What is the very first thing you should do when a complex puzzle question appears on screen?
Back: Read ALL the clues once without writing anything, then identify the variables (persons, days, floors, colours) and fix the most definite clue first. Never start drawing before reading everything.

Card 2
Front: In a floor-based puzzle, which clue should you fix first — "A lives on an even floor" or "C lives on the 3rd floor"?
Back: Always fix the definite clue first: "C lives on the 3rd floor." Definite (fixed-position) clues anchor the diagram; conditional clues are placed around them later.

Card 3
Front: What is the "two-case method" and when do you use it?
Back: When a clue allows two possible positions (e.g., "B is either immediately above or below D"), draw both cases in parallel and continue. The case that contradicts a later clue is discarded. Use it whenever a clue is ambiguous.

Card 4
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you remember the order of solving any complex puzzle?
Back: "D-C-E" — Definite first, Conditional next, Eliminate last. Fix definite clues, attach conditional clues to them, then eliminate impossible positions.

Card 5
Front: In a scheduling puzzle (Mon–Sun), what does "two persons between A and B" actually mean in terms of gap?
Back: It means a gap of 3 positions (e.g., A on Monday → B on Thursday). A common error is counting it as a gap of 2. "N persons between" = difference of N+1 in positions.

Card 6
Front: Examiner's trap: "A is taller than B but shorter than C" — what wrong arrangement do most students make?
Back: Students often write A > B > C. Correct order is C > A > B. The trap is reading both comparisons from A's perspective without converting them into a single chain.

Card 7
Front: In a box/stack puzzle, if "only one box is kept between P and R," how many arrangements does that single clue allow?
Back: Two mirror arrangements: P _ R or R _ P (with one box in the gap). Never assume left-to-right order unless the clue says "P is above R."

Card 8
Front: What is a "negative clue" and why is it powerful?
Back: A clue like "D does not live on the top floor" or "E is not adjacent to F." It doesn't place anyone directly but eliminates cells — in the final steps, negative clues often decide the answer.

Card 9
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you handle "immediate neighbour" vs "somewhere to the left"?
Back: Remember "I = Lock, S = Float." Immediate = locked adjacent pair (treat as one block). Somewhere = floating relation (only relative order, any distance).

Card 10
Front: In a circular seating puzzle with people facing the centre, where is a person's immediate right?
Back: On your left as you draw the diagram (anti-clockwise direction). Facing the centre reverses left-right; facing outward reverses it back. Always mark the facing direction first.

Card 11
Front: Examiner's trap: In "some persons face inside and some outside," what must you check before placing anyone's neighbour?
Back: Each person's facing direction individually. Left/right flips per person, so a single wrong facing assumption collapses the whole arrangement. Note facing beside every name.

Card 12
Front: How many people sit in a row if "A is 4th from the left and 5th from the right"?
Back: 4 + 5 − 1 = 8 people. Formula: Total = (position from left) + (position from right) − 1.

Card 13
Front: In a month-and-date puzzle (two dates per month, e.g., 7th and 22nd), what is the safest diagram format?
Back: A two-column table: months in rows, two date columns. Place each person in the correct month first, then resolve which of the two dates using gap clues.

Card 14
Front: What should you do when, after placing all clues, one cell still has two possible candidates?
Back: Re-read the unused or partially used clues — a negative clue or a "between" clue is almost always left over. If truly both fit, re-check an earlier assumption; a valid puzzle has exactly one solution.

Card 15
Front: Why should you draw the puzzle diagram with a pencil-style (rough) mindset and keep it small?
Back: Complex puzzles often need case-elimination and redrawing. A compact, erasable diagram saves time and prevents confusion between Case 1 and Case 2.

Card 16
Front: In a comparison puzzle with 5 people by height/weight/marks, what is the fastest structure to build?
Back: A single vertical chain (top = highest). Convert every clue into "X > Y" form and merge chains at common names. Two separate chains that never connect means a clue is still unused.

Card 17
Front: Examiner's trap: "B sits second to the right of the person who faces A" — what do students commonly misread?
Back: They place B second to the right of A directly. The clue links B to A's opposite person, not to A. Always identify the intermediate reference person first.

Card 18
Front: What is the time strategy for a 5-question puzzle set in SSC/Bank exams?
Back: Invest 3–4 minutes to solve the arrangement fully, then answer all 5 questions in under a minute. If the diagram isn't complete in 4–5 minutes, mark and skip — a half-solved puzzle wastes more time than a skipped one.

Card 19
Front: In a "year of joining" puzzle, what does "only one person joined between X (2015) and Y" tell you about Y's year?
Back: Y joined in 2017 or 2013 — a gap of exactly 2 years. "One person between" always means a difference of 2 in the sequence.

Card 20
Front: What is the single biggest reason students get puzzle questions wrong even after solving the full arrangement?
Back: Answering from memory instead of re-reading the question. Questions often ask "who sits second to the left of X's neighbour?" — always trace such chained references on the diagram, never mentally.
