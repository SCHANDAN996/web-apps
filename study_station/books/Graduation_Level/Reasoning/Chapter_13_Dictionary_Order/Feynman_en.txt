# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Dictionary Order

## The Hardest Concept, Explained Like You're 12

Imagine you're at a railway station with your friend, and you need to find your names on a huge printed list of 500 passengers. The list is in alphabetical order. Your name is **SHARMA** and your friend's name is **SHARDA**.

You both start scanning. First letter: S = S. Same. Second letter: H = H. Same. Third: A = A. Same. Fourth letter — you have **R**, your friend has **R**. Same again! Fifth letter: you have **M**, your friend has **D**.

Now here's the magic moment: you don't need to look at anything else. **D comes before M in the alphabet**, so SHARDA is above SHARMA on the list. Done. The sixth, seventh letters — they don't matter at all.

**That is the entire chapter.** Dictionary order means: compare letter by letter from the left, and the *first* position where two words differ decides everything. Everything after that position is irrelevant.

## The One Trap That Fools Everyone

What if one word runs out of letters? Compare **CAT** and **CATALOGUE**.

C = C, A = A, T = T... and then CAT has nothing left, while CATALOGUE keeps going. Rule: **the shorter word comes first** — think of the "missing letter" as smaller than every letter of the alphabet. So CAT → CATALOGUE. In the exam, words like MENTAL and MENTALITY, or STAR and START, are placed side by side exactly to trap students who forget this.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "The Race of Letters" 🏁

Picture every word as a line of runners standing in lanes. The referee fires the whistle at **position 1**. All runners who tie move to position 2. The moment one runner is *behind* (a later alphabet letter), the race is over — that word loses and goes later in the dictionary.

**Memory line:** *"First difference decides; short word wins ties."*

For arrangement questions with 4–5 words, don't compare every pair. Instead:
1. Group words by their **first letter** — the groups are already in order.
2. Inside each group, look only at the **second letter**, and so on.
3. Ask "which word comes **3rd/last**?" — never sort the whole list if you only need one position.
