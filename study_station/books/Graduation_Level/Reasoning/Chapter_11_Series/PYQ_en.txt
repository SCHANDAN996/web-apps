# PYQ Analysis — Series (Verbal Reasoning)

## 1. How Exams Ask Questions from This Chapter

Series questions appear in almost every reasoning paper — SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS, IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RRB NTPC/Group D, and state PCS prelims. They are usually among the **first questions of the reasoning section** because they are quick to solve and quick to check. Examiners use them to test pattern recognition under time pressure.

The questions are asked in three standard formats:

1. **Find the missing term** — a series with one or two blanks (e.g., 3, 7, 15, ?, 63).
2. **Find the next term** — continue the given pattern.
3. **Find the wrong term** — one number breaks the pattern; identify it.

## 2. Sub-Topics That Recur Most Often

Based on the recurring pattern of past papers, the following sub-types dominate:

- **Difference-based series** — constant difference, increasing difference (difference itself in AP), and difference of differences. This is the single most common family.
- **Multiplicative and mixed operations** — ×2+1, ×3−2, alternating × and + patterns. Very frequent in banking exams.
- **Squares and cubes** — n², n²±k, n³±k, and squares of primes. SSC loves these.
- **Letter series** — positional shifts (+2, −3, alternating), reverse-order pairs, and letter groups with increasing gaps.
- **Alphanumeric / mixed series** — number-letter pairs where both components follow independent patterns.
- **Fibonacci-type and sum-based series** — each term is the sum (or difference) of the previous two.
- **Wrong-number series** — heavily used in IBPS/SBI; the pattern is usually multiplicative with a small additive twist.

## 3. Exam-Wise Tendencies (Qualitative)

- **SSC (CGL/CHSL/MTS):** Favours letter series, simple difference patterns, and squares/cubes. Difficulty is moderate; speed matters more than depth.
- **Banking (IBPS/SBI):** Favours wrong-number series and two-step multiplicative patterns (×2+3, ×1.5, etc.). Options are close together to punish approximation.
- **Railway (NTPC/Group D):** Straightforward patterns — AP, GP, squares, simple letter shifts. Rarely tricky.
- **State PCS prelims:** Occasionally embeds series inside statement-based or data-sufficiency wrappers.

## 4. Examiner's Traps — The Psychological Hooks

1. **The "almost-AP" trap:** First three terms suggest a constant difference, but the fourth breaks it (the difference itself is growing). Students lock in the wrong rule after two terms.
2. **Interleaved twin series:** Odd-position terms follow one rule, even-position terms another. The series looks chaotic until you split it.
3. **The near-square disguise:** Terms like 8, 24, 48, 80 are n²−1 (9−1, 25−1, 49−1, 81−1) — squares of odd numbers minus 1. Students hunt for differences and waste time.
4. **Letter-position confusion:** Counting A=0 instead of A=1, or miscounting wrap-around (Z→A). Examiners place the correct answer at the position you'd reach with the off-by-one error.
5. **Wrong-term series with two plausible fixes:** One wrong number, but changing it "correctly" requires spotting the *intended* rule, not just any rule that fits.
6. **Negative-marking bait:** A series that needs three levels of differencing — solvable, but not in 45 seconds. This is a deliberate time sink.

## 5. High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

**Q1.** 4, 9, 19, 39, 79, ?
(a) 159 (b) 160 (c) 169 (d) 149
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Pattern is ×2+1: 4×2+1=9, 9×2+1=19, 19×2+1=39, 39×2+1=79, 79×2+1=**159**.
**Trap:** Option (b) 160 catches students who compute 79×2=158 and add 2 by habit; option (d) catches those who do ×2−9. The hook is rushing the last step.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC pattern)

**Q2.** 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?
(a) 40 (b) 42 (c) 44 (d) 36
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Differences are 4, 6, 8, 10 — increasing by 2. Next difference = 12, so 30+12 = **42**. (Equivalently, terms are n²+n: 1·2, 2·3, 3·4 …)
**Trap:** Option (a) 40 tempts students who assume a constant difference of 10 after seeing only the last gap. Classic "almost-AP" hook.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC pattern)

**Q3.** 3, 7, 16, 35, 74, ?
(a) 153 (b) 148 (c) 152 (d) 150
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** ×2+1, ×2+2, ×2+3, ×2+4 … → 3×2+1=7, 7×2+2=16, 16×2+3=35, 35×2+4=74, 74×2+5 = **153**.
**Trap:** The additive constant grows (+1, +2, +3…). Students who fix on "×2+something" but keep it constant land on 149 or 150. This tests whether you verify the rule on *every* step, not just the first two.
**Source:** PYQ-style (Banking pattern)

**Q4.** Find the wrong term: 5, 11, 23, 47, 96, 191
(a) 11 (b) 23 (c) 96 (d) 47
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Pattern is ×2+1: 5×2+1=11, 11×2+1=23, 23×2+1=47, 47×2+1=95 (not 96), 95×2+1=191. Wrong term = **96**.
**Trap:** 96 looks natural after 47 (doubling region), and 191 is consistent with 95, not 96 — so the error hides mid-series. Students who only check forward from 96 find "96×2−1=191" and wrongly accept it.
**Source:** PYQ-style (IBPS pattern)

**Q5.** A, C, F, J, O, ?
(a) T (b) U (c) S (d) V
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Positions: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 — gaps of +2, +3, +4, +5. Next gap +6 → 15+6 = 21 = **U**.
**Trap:** Option (a) T comes from assuming a constant +5 gap (15+5=20=T). The increasing-gap structure is the hook; two terms are never enough to fix the rule.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC pattern)

**Q6.** 1, 4, 27, 256, ?
(a) 3125 (b) 1024 (c) 625 (d) 7776
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Terms are nⁿ: 1¹, 2², 3³, 4⁴, so next = 5⁵ = **3125**.
**Trap:** Students try differences or cubes and burn minutes. Option (d) 7776 = 6⁵ catches those who mix up base and exponent. Recognising nⁿ instantly is a memory item, not a calculation.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q7.** 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, 33, ?
(a) 65 (b) 64 (c) 63 (d) 66
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Differences: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — doubling. Next difference = 32 → 33+32 = **65**. (Equivalently ×2−1 each step.)
**Trap:** Option (b) 64 = 33×2−2, and (c) 63 = 33×2−3 — both punish misreading the operation as ×2−k with wrong k. Verify with two consecutive steps before answering.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC/Banking pattern)

**Q8.** Z, X, V, T, R, ?
(a) P (b) Q (c) O (d) N
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Reverse alphabet with step −2: Z(26), X(24), V(22), T(20), R(18), next = 16 = **P**.
**Trap:** Option (b) Q comes from step −1; option (c) O from step −3 applied once. Reverse-direction series cause position miscounts — write positions as numbers first.
**Source:** PYQ-style (Railway pattern)

**Q9.** 6, 11, 21, 36, 56, ?
(a) 81 (b) 91 (c) 76 (d) 86
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Differences: 5, 10, 15, 20 — multiples of 5. Next difference = 25 → 56+25 = **81**.
**Trap:** The differences look like "+5 each time" only at the second level; students who stop at first differences and average them get 76 or 86. Two-level differencing is the intended skill.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10.** Find the wrong term: 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 344
(a) 27 (b) 125 (c) 344 (d) 216
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Cubes: 2³=8, 3³=27, 4³=64, 5³=125, 6³=216, 7³=343 (not 344). Wrong term = **344**.
**Trap:** 344 is off by just 1 — pure near-miss bait. Students who don't know 7³=343 cold will accept it. Cube values up to 15³ must be memorised.
**Source:** PYQ-style (Banking pattern)

## 6. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Applied

- **15-second rule:** If within 15 seconds you cannot name the *family* of the pattern (difference, product, square/cube, letter-shift, twin series), mark and move on. Series questions are designed to be instant or expensive — there is rarely a middle ground.
- **45-second rule:** Even if you spot the family, if the rule needs more than two verification steps (e.g., three-level differencing, or a wrong-term series where two candidates look plausible), cap your attempt at 45 seconds.

**Applied to the questions above:**

- **Q6 (nⁿ series)** — *Skip if not instant.* Either you recognise 1, 4, 27, 256 as nⁿ within 5 seconds, or no amount of differencing will save you inside 45 seconds. Classic skip-or-instant question.
- **Q4 (wrong-term, ×2+1)** — *Borderline skip.* Wrong-term questions need the rule verified across the whole series, not just between two terms. If the rule isn't visible in 15 seconds, the full verification will exceed 45 — mark it and return only if time remains.
- **Q3 (×2+k with growing k)** — Solvable within 45 seconds *only* if you test the additive constant on at least three steps. If you catch yourself re-testing after 45 seconds, take the best-supported option and move.

## 7. Key Takeaways for Revision

1. Always test your rule on **at least three consecutive steps** before answering — most traps are built on two-step coincidences.
2. For letter series, **convert to positions (A=1…Z=26) immediately** — never shift letters mentally.
3. Memorise squares to 30², cubes to 15³, and powers of 2 to 2¹⁰ — near-square and near-cube disguises are the most common traps.
4. In wrong-term series, verify the rule **across the entire series**, including terms after the suspected error.
5. Series is a **speed chapter**: bank the instant ones, skip the sinks, and never let one series eat the time budget of three other questions.
