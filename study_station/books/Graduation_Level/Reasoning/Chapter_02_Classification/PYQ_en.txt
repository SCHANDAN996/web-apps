# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Classification (Odd One Out)

## 1. How Exams Ask Questions from This Chapter

Classification is one of the most reliable scoring areas in Verbal Reasoning. Across SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS, GD), Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI), Railways (NTPC, Group D) and State PCS prelims, the question format is almost always the same: **four or five options are given; three/four share a common property and one does not — find the odd one.**

The chapter appears in three standard forms:

1. **Word-based (verbal classification):** odd word among related words — e.g., three are planets, one is a satellite; three are vegetables, one is a fruit.
2. **Number-based:** odd number among numbers sharing a property — prime/composite, perfect square/cube, divisibility, digit-sum, digit-pattern (e.g., digits in ascending order).
3. **Letter/Pair-based:** odd letter-pair or letter-cluster — based on alphabet positions, gap patterns (+2, −3), vowel/consonant structure, or opposite-letter pairs.

## 2. Sub-Topics That Recur Most Often

- **General knowledge categories:** rivers vs. cities, mammals vs. birds, instruments vs. units, sports terms, country–capital–currency groupings. Exams love "almost-same" categories: e.g., three are *tributaries of the Ganga*, one is a tributary of the Yamuna.
- **Number properties:** prime vs. composite is the single most repeated idea; perfect squares/cubes and "sum of digits" patterns follow closely.
- **Letter-position arithmetic:** pairs like CE, FH, KM where letters are separated by a fixed gap; one pair breaks the gap rule.
- **Analogy-flavoured classification:** four pairs where the second element relates to the first in the same way (tool : function, animal : young one, country : currency), one pair breaks the relation.
- **Mixed/embedded traps:** an option that fits a *superficial* category (all are fruits) but the intended classification is deeper (three are citrus fruits).

## 3. Exam-Wise Tendencies (in words, not numbers)

- **SSC CGL / CHSL:** balanced mix of word, number and letter classification; letter-gap and prime/composite questions are favourites. Difficulty is moderate; traps rely on near-correct categories.
- **Banking (IBPS/SBI):** fewer pure GK categories, more letter-cluster and number-pattern items; often embedded in larger reasoning sets. Speed matters more than depth.
- **Railways (NTPC/Group D):** heavier on GK-based word classification (states, rivers, sports, instruments) and simple number properties; straightforward but vocabulary-driven.
- **State PCS prelims:** occasionally statement-flavoured classification ("which does not belong to the group") with factual GK categories; accuracy over speed.

## 4. The Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

1. **The near-correct category:** all four options look like "fruits", but three are *citrus* and one is not. The trap rewards superficial reading.
2. **The double-property number:** a number that is odd AND composite (e.g., 9, 15) placed among primes — students who check only "odd/even" fall for it.
3. **The reversed letter gap:** three pairs use +3 gap, one uses −3 (letters reversed). Looks identical at a glance.
4. **The famous-name bias:** a well-known city/river/person placed to make you assume it belongs (e.g., a famous port city placed among state capitals).
5. **The category-shift:** three items belong to one sense of a word (e.g., "bat, ball, stump" — cricket equipment) and one belongs to a different sense ("bat" the animal is the classic reverse trap).
6. **The "all are correct" illusion:** in number sets, two valid properties may exist; the examiner intends the *stronger* (more specific) one. Check which property isolates exactly one option.

## 5. Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions and Trap Analysis)

**Q1.** Find the odd one out: (a) Mars (b) Jupiter (c) Saturn (d) Moon
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are planets; the Moon is a natural satellite. *Trap:* all four are celestial bodies — the superficial category includes all four, so students hesitate. The intended classification is planet vs. satellite.
**Source:** NCERT Class 6–8 Science / PYQ-style

**Q2.** Find the odd one out: (a) 13 (b) 17 (c) 21 (d) 19
**Answer:** (c)
**Solution:** 13, 17 and 19 are prime numbers; 21 = 3 × 7 is composite. *Trap:* all four are odd numbers — checking only parity fails. Always test primality in odd-number sets.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q3.** Find the odd one out: (a) 16 (b) 36 (c) 48 (d) 64
**Answer:** (c)
**Solution:** 16, 36 and 64 are perfect squares (4², 6², 8²); 48 is not. *Trap:* all are even and divisible by 4 — the weaker property fits all four, so students who stop at divisibility get stuck. ⏱ *15-sec rule:* if squares don't strike you in 15 seconds, mark and move on; return only if time permits.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q4.** Find the odd one out: (a) CE (b) FH (c) KN (d) PR
**Answer:** (c)
**Solution:** C→E, F→H and P→R are all +2 gaps (C=3→E=5, F=6→H=8, P=16→R=18); K→N is a +3 gap (K=11→N=14). *Trap:* all four pairs "look" like close letters — the difference of one step is invisible at a glance. Always compute alphabet positions numerically instead of eyeballing. ⏱ *Skip rule:* letter-position arithmetic under time pressure is error-prone — if positions aren't computed within 45 seconds, skip.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q5.** Find the odd one out: (a) Ganga (b) Yamuna (c) Godavari (d) Kaveri
**Answer:** (b)
**Solution:** Ganga, Godavari and Kaveri flow directly into the sea (Bay of Bengal); the Yamuna is a tributary — it joins the Ganga at Prayagraj and does not reach the sea independently. *Trap:* all four are major Indian rivers, so the superficial category fits all four. The intended classification is main river vs. tributary. Lesson: pick the property that isolates exactly one option most specifically.
**Source:** NCERT Class 9 Geography / PYQ-style

**Q6.** Find the odd one out: (a) Thermometer (b) Barometer (c) Ammeter (d) Volt
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Thermometer, barometer and ammeter are measuring *instruments*; volt is a *unit* (of potential difference), measured by a voltmeter. *Trap:* "volt" sounds like "voltmeter" — the famous-name bias makes students group it with instruments.
**Source:** NCERT Class 8–10 Science / PYQ-style

**Q7.** Find the odd one out: (a) Orange (b) Lemon (c) Grapefruit (d) Mango
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Orange, lemon and grapefruit are citrus fruits; mango is not. *Trap:* all four are fruits — the superficial category fits all four, so students who stop at "fruit" see no odd one. The intended classification is citrus vs. non-citrus. Always check whether a *more specific* property isolates exactly one option.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q8.** Find the odd one out: (a) 121 (b) 144 (c) 169 (d) 132
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 121 = 11², 144 = 12², 169 = 13² — consecutive squares; 132 is not a perfect square. *Trap:* all are three-digit numbers with patterned-looking digits; 132 "looks" patterned. Memorise squares up to 20² to make this a 15-second question.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q9.** Find the odd one out: (a) Doctor : Patient (b) Teacher : Student (c) Lawyer : Client (d) Carpenter : Wood
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** In (a), (b), (c) the relation is *professional : person served*; in (d) it is *worker : material worked upon*. *Trap:* all four are "profession : something" pairs — the relation type, not the professions, is the classifier. ⏱ *45-sec rule:* relation-type questions deserve up to 45 seconds because misreading the relation is the main error; beyond that, skip.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10.** Find the odd one out: (a) 27 (b) 64 (c) 125 (d) 144
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 27 = 3³, 64 = 4³, 125 = 5³ are perfect cubes; 144 = 12² is a perfect square. *Trap:* 64 is both a square (8²) and a cube (4³) — the double-property number. Students who classify by "squares" wrongly mark 64 as fitting with 144. Check cubes first when 27/64/125 appear together.
**Source:** PYQ-style

## 6. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Reviewed Against This Chapter

- **Word/GK classification:** genuinely a **15-second** question. You either know the category or you don't — extra time rarely helps. If the category is unknown (e.g., unfamiliar instruments), mark and move.
- **Number classification:** **15 seconds** if you have squares/cubes/primes memorised; otherwise it becomes trial-and-error — cap at 45 seconds, then skip.
- **Letter-pair classification:** allow up to **45 seconds** for writing alphabet positions; if the gap pattern isn't visible after computing positions, skip — these have the lowest marks-per-minute when they don't click.
- **Relation-pair classification:** up to **45 seconds**; the error is misreading the relation, not slow calculation, so a careful single read beats two hurried ones.

**Overall verdict:** Classification is a *first-pass* chapter — attempt all word-based items immediately, attempt number/letter items only if the pattern surfaces within the time cap, and never let a single odd-one-out question consume more than 45 seconds.
