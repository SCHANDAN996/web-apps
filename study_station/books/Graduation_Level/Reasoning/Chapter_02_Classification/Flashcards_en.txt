Card 1
Front: In a "find the odd one out" question, what is the very first thing you should check before comparing meanings?
Back: Check the category level — are all options from the same broad class (e.g., all fruits, all instruments)? The odd one usually breaks the shared category, not the meaning.

Card 2
Front: Why is "Apple, Mango, Banana, Potato" a classic classification question?
Back: Potato is the odd one — it is a vegetable (a tuber), while the other three are fruits. The trap: potato is eaten like the others, so meaning alone misleads.

Card 3
Front: What mnemonic helps you remember the four main types of classification questions?
Back: "WPLN" — Word, Pair, Letter, Number. Classification questions come as odd word, odd pair, odd letter-group, or odd number.

Card 4
Front: In letter-group classification (e.g., ACE, BDF, CEG, DFJ), what pattern should you test first?
Back: Check the gap between consecutive letters. ACE, BDF, CEG all skip one letter (+2, +2 pattern); DFJ breaks it (+2 then +4), so it is the odd one.

Card 5
Front: Examiner's trap: In number classification, why is checking only "odd/even" dangerous?
Back: Because the examiner often sets three numbers sharing a deeper property (prime, perfect square, divisible by a specific number) while odd/even splits them 2–2, giving no unique answer. Always test multiple properties.

Card 6
Front: In pair classification like (Dog : Puppy), what exactly must the odd pair violate?
Back: The relationship type. If three pairs show "animal : its young one," the odd pair breaks that relation (e.g., Cow : Milk is animal : product).

Card 7
Front: What is the "one-vs-three" rule of classification?
Back: The correct odd one must be unique in some property while the other three share a common property. If your logic makes it 2-vs-2, the logic is wrong — search for a stronger property.

Card 8
Front: Why can a question have two seemingly correct answers, and how do you pick one?
Back: Choose the property that is most specific and intrinsic (e.g., "only even prime" beats "starts with T"). The examiner designs one property to be clearly stronger than the other.

Card 9
Front: Examiner's trap: In "Rose, Lotus, Marigold, Cauliflower," why is Cauliflower the answer and not Lotus?
Back: Three are flowers used decoratively; cauliflower is a vegetable (eaten). Trap: lotus grows in water, tempting a "water plant" logic — but that splits 1-vs-3 on habitat, while the intended split is flower vs vegetable.

Card 10
Front: What mnemonic order should you follow to test number properties quickly?
Back: "P-S-C-D" — Prime, Square, Cube, Divisibility. Test in this order; most number classification answers hide in one of these four.

Card 11
Front: In word classification, when all four words belong to the same category, what do you check next?
Back: Check sub-properties: gender, size, habitat, function, origin, or spelling features (vowel count, letter repetition). The odd one hides at the sub-level.

Card 12
Front: Why is "2, 3, 5, 9" a classification question where 9 is odd?
Back: 2, 3, 5 are prime numbers; 9 is composite (3×3). Trap: 9 is the only odd-looking "non-even" standout by size, but the real property is primality.

Card 13
Front: In letter classification, what does it mean when three groups are "vowel-starting" and one is not?
Back: The odd group starts with a consonant. Examiners use starting letter, vowel position, or alphabetical position sum as hidden properties — check all three.

Card 14
Front: What is the fastest elimination strategy when you cannot spot the property in 20 seconds?
Back: Test the most common properties in fixed order: category → odd/even or prime → letter gaps → relationship type. If none works, mark and skip — return only if time remains.

Card 15
Front: Why is "Ear : Hear :: Eye : See" style pair classification tricky when one option is "Nose : Smell"?
Back: It may not be tricky — but examiners swap the relation direction (e.g., "Hear : Ear") in one pair. Always verify the order of the relationship, not just the words involved.

Card 16
Front: In classification, why should you never pick an answer based on "this word is longer/shorter" unless nothing else works?
Back: Length/spelling properties are the weakest and are usually decoys. Examiners place them to trap students who miss the stronger semantic or mathematical property.

Card 17
Front: What makes "Circle, Triangle, Square, Cube" a classification question?
Back: Cube is three-dimensional; the other three are two-dimensional shapes. The property is dimension, not the number of sides — a common misread.

Card 18
Front: How do you handle a classification question where all four options seem unrelated?
Back: Look for a hidden common thread among any three (synonyms, things found in a kitchen, words with double letters). Three will always connect; your job is to find that connection, not to judge all four at once.
