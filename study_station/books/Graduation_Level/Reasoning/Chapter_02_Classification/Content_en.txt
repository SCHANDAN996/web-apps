# Classification (Odd One Out)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Understand what Classification questions really test.
2. Identify the **four big families** of classification: Word-based, Letter-based, Number-based, and Pair-based.
3. Apply a fixed 3-step checking order so you never freeze in the exam.
4. Spot the examiner's favourite traps — double relations, near-correct options, and confusing vocabulary.
5. Solve a standard question in **under 30 seconds** and know when to skip.

---

## 1. The Hook: Your Brain Already Does This Every Day

You walk into a kitchen and see: **Mango, Banana, Apple, Potato.** Without thinking, your brain screams — *Potato!* Why? Because three are fruits and one is a vegetable.

Congratulations — you have just solved a Classification question. This chapter is not about learning something new; it is about **making your natural instinct systematic and fast**, so that even when the examiner hides the odd one behind tricky words or numbers, you catch it every single time.

In SSC CGL, IBPS PO, RRB NTPC and State PCS prelims, Classification is one of the **highest-frequency, lowest-effort** topics in Verbal Reasoning. Four options, one odd one out, no calculation-heavy work. Master it, and these become your "free marks."

---

## 2. The Common Struggle (And Why It Happens)

Most students don't fail Classification because they don't know the answer. They fail because:

- **They find a relation, but the wrong one.** Example: "Dog, Cat, Lion, Cow" — a student picks *Lion* (wild animal), but could the examiner have intended *Cow*? Both logic paths seem possible! This is the classic trap, and we will kill it in Section 6.
- **They check randomly.** They look at meaning first, then letters, then back to meaning — wasting 90 seconds.
- **Tough vocabulary scares them.** Words like "Thermometer, Barometer, Odometer, Speedometer" look frightening but are actually easy once you stay calm.

The cure is a **fixed checking order**. Your brain loves checklists. Give it one.

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** Before reading further, close your eyes and say aloud — why do students usually lose marks in Classification even when the topic is easy?

## 3. What Is Classification, Formally?

**Classification** means grouping items by a shared property and identifying the one item that does **not** belong to the group.

Structure of every question:
- 4 or 5 options are given.
- 3 (or 4) share **one common property**.
- 1 breaks that property → that is your answer.

The golden rule: **The odd one must be odd by a property that the others ALL share.** A property shared by only two options is not enough.

## 4. The Four Families of Classification

### Family A: Word / Meaning-Based Classification

The common property is based on **meaning, category, or real-world knowledge**.

**Types you'll meet:**

| Sub-type | Example | Odd one |
|---|---|---|
| Category (animals, fruits, professions) | Rose, Lotus, Marigold, Mango | Mango (fruit, not flower) |
| Part vs Whole | Wheel, Engine, Steering, Car | Car (whole; others are parts) |
| Tool vs Work | Pen, Paper, Eraser, Write | Write (action; others are objects) |
| Synonym/Antonym groups | Happy, Joyful, Cheerful, Sad | Sad (opposite feeling) |
| Degree/Intensity | Warm, Hot, Scorching, Cold | Cold (opposite scale) |
| GK-based | Ganga, Yamuna, Godavari, Nile | Nile (not an Indian river) |

**Example (Graduation level):**
Options: (a) Thermometer (b) Barometer (c) Lactometer (d) Microscope
- Thermometer → measures temperature
- Barometer → measures pressure
- Lactometer → measures purity of milk
- Microscope → used to *see* tiny objects, not to *measure*
- **Answer: (d) Microscope** — the others are measuring instruments.

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** Solve this in your head: *Singer, Dancer, Painter, Song.* Which is odd, and why? (Answer: Song — the others are people; song is a creation.)

### Family B: Letter-Based Classification

Here meaning is useless. The property hides in the **alphabet positions, patterns, or letter types**.

**What to check (in this order):**
1. **Vowel/consonant pattern** — e.g., all have exactly 2 vowels, one doesn't.
2. **Position gaps** — e.g., in ACE, letters jump +2, +2.
3. **First/last letter relations** — e.g., last letter = first letter + 3.
4. **Repeated letters** — one option has no repetition.
5. **Alphabet position sums** — sum of letter positions is even/odd/prime.

**Example:**
Options: (a) CEG (b) FHJ (c) KMO (d) PRT
- C(3)→E(5)→G(7): gaps +2, +2
- F(6)→H(8)→J(10): gaps +2, +2
- K(11)→M(13)→O(15): gaps +2, +2
- P(16)→R(18)→T(20): gaps +2, +2
- All four match on gaps — so the gap pattern is a *decoy*. Check vowels: CEG has E, FHJ has no vowel, KMO has O, PRT has no vowel — that splits 2-2, so discard it too. Check starting letters: C=3, F=6, K=11, P=16. Differences: +3, +5, +5. Not clean. Check the **middle letters**: E=5, H=8, M=13, R=18. Hmm — E, M, R... Now check whether the **first letter is odd-positioned**: C=3 (odd), F=6 (even), K=11 (odd), P=16 (even) — 2-2 again. Finally check the **last letters**: G=7, J=10, O=15, T=20. G, O are odd positions; J, T even — 2-2 again.

This example shows a real danger: if your first property splits options 2-2 or fits all four, **discard it and move to the next check**. In an actual exam question, exactly one property will cleanly separate 3 from 1 — keep checking the list in order until you find it.

**Cleaner Example:**
Options: (a) BD (b) FH (c) JL (d) MO
- B(2)→D(4): +2; F(6)→H(8): +2; J(10)→L(12): +2; M(13)→O(15): +2. All match on the gap! Now check the starting letters: B=2, F=6, J=10 are at **even positions**, but M=13 is at an **odd position**. **Answer: (d) MO** — the only pair starting from an odd-positioned letter. **Moral: letter questions are pure pattern-checking — be systematic, not clever.**

### Family C: Number-Based Classification

The property hides in **number properties**. Check in this order:

1. **Odd/Even**
2. **Prime/Composite**
3. **Perfect square / Perfect cube**
4. **Divisibility** (by 3, 7, 11…)
5. **Digit operations** — sum of digits, product of digits, digit reversal
6. **Relations like n², n²+1, n³−1**

**Example:**
Options: (a) 16 (b) 36 (c) 64 (d) 50
- 16 = 4², 36 = 6², 64 = 8², 50 = not a perfect square.
- **Answer: (d) 50.**

**Trickier Example:**
Options: (a) 121 (b) 144 (c) 169 (d) 225
- All are perfect squares! 11², 12², 13², 15². Look deeper: 121, 144, 169 are squares of **consecutive** numbers 11, 12, 13 — but 225 = 15² skips 14. Also: 144 is the square of an **even** number; the others are squares of odd numbers. Two possible answers? In the real exam, only one interpretation will be "clean" across all four — here the examiner's intended answer is usually **(b) 144** (only even-number square). This is the *double-relation trap* — see the trap box at the end.

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back, list the 6 checks for number-based classification in order.

### Family D: Pair-Based Classification

Options are **pairs** — (word : word), (letter : letter), or (number : number). You must find the pair whose **internal relation** differs.

**Example:**
(a) 8 : 64 (b) 5 : 25 (c) 7 : 49 (d) 6 : 35
- 8→64 (square), 5→25 (square), 7→49 (square), 6→36 would be square, but it's 35.
- **Answer: (d).**

**Word-pair Example:**
(a) Doctor : Patient (b) Teacher : Student (c) Lawyer : Client (d) Carpenter : Wood
- In (a), (b), (c): professional → person served. In (d): professional → **material**, not a person.
- **Answer: (d).**

## 5. The Fixed 3-Step Checking Order (Your Exam Algorithm)

Whenever you see a Classification question, run this algorithm:

**STEP 1 (5 seconds): Meaning/Category scan.** Do 3 options obviously belong to one real-world category? If yes → done.

**STEP 2 (10 seconds): Structure scan.** If meaning fails, check structure — vowels, letter gaps, odd/even, prime, squares, digit sums.

**STEP 3 (10 seconds): Verify the oddity is UNIQUE.** Confirm your chosen property is shared by **all the others**, not just two of them. If two options both look odd by different logics, prefer the **stronger, more specific** property (e.g., "perfect square" beats "even number" because even numbers are too common).

Total: **~25–30 seconds.** If nothing clicks by 40 seconds → mark and move (see Skip Strategy section later in the book).

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** Recite the 3 steps aloud. What do you do in Step 3, and why does it matter?

## 6. Mnemonic Trick: "**M-S-V**" — *My Smart Verification*

- **M** = **Meaning** first (category, profession, GK).
- **S** = **Structure** next (letters, numbers, patterns).
- **V** = **Verify uniqueness** (the property must cover ALL other options).

Remember: *"Mera Sawaal Verify kar"* — check meaning, check structure, then verify. If you follow M-S-V in order, you will never waste time jumping randomly between logics.

**Bonus mnemonic for number checks — "O-P-S-D"**: **O**dd/Even → **P**rime → **S**quare/Cube → **D**igit sum. *"OPSD karo, answer khud bolega."*

## 7. Worked Examples (Graduation Level)

**Example 1:** (a) Copper (b) Iron (c) Silver (d) Wood
- Copper, Iron, Silver → metals. Wood → not a metal. **Answer: (d).** (5-second question — Step 1 of M-S-V.)

**Example 2:** (a) 27 (b) 64 (c) 125 (d) 144
- 27 = 3³, 64 = 4³, 125 = 5³, 144 = 12² (not a cube). **Answer: (d).**

**Example 3:** (a) February (b) April (c) June (d) November
- April, June, November → 30 days. February → 28/29 days. **Answer: (a).** (Classic — examiners love calendar-based classification.)

**Example 4:** (a) KQ (b) LR (c) MS (d) OU
- K(11)→Q(17): +6; L(12)→R(18): +6; M(13)→S(19): +6; O(15)→U(21): +6. All +6?! Recheck positions: K=11, L=12, M=13 are consecutive starts; O=15 skips N=14. So (d) breaks the *starting-letter sequence* K, L, M, (N), O. **Answer: (d)** — when the obvious pattern matches all, hunt for the hidden second pattern.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1: Multiple plausible relations (the double-relation trap).**
The examiner designs options where *two* answers seem correct by two different logics. Example: "Dog, Cat, Lion, Cow" — Lion is odd (wild), but Cow could also look odd (gives milk). Defence: choose the property that is **most specific and shared by exactly three** options. "Wild animal" cleanly separates Lion from three domestic animals — that wins. Vague properties ("found in villages") lose.

**Trap 2: Intimidating vocabulary.**
Words like *Barometer, Odometer, Altimeter, Ammeter* are thrown in to scare you into skipping. Defence: stay calm — these are usually **measuring instruments**, and the odd one is often a simple non-measuring device (e.g., Telescope). Difficult words ≠ difficult question. Never skip just because the words look scary.

**Trap 3: The "all match" illusion.**
You find a pattern (e.g., +6 gap) that fits **all four** options, panic, and guess. Defence: if one property fits everything, it is the *decoy* property. Immediately hunt for a **second, hidden property** (starting-letter sequence, odd/even position, vowel presence). The real discriminator is always there — the examiner just buried it under an obvious one.
