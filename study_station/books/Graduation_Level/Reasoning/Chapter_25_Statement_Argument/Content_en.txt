# Statement and Argument (कथन और तर्क)

## Chapter Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Tell a **strong** argument from a **weak** one in under 45 seconds.
2. Spot the six kinds of weak arguments that examiners use again and again.
3. Judge "Yes" and "No" arguments on the same issue independently.
4. Avoid answering from your personal opinion.

## 1. The Hook: The Debate Judge

Imagine you are the judge of a school debate on "Should mobile phones be allowed in class?" One student says, "Yes, because phones help us check facts during lessons." Another says, "No, because my uncle once dropped his phone." You do not have to agree with either — your job is only to decide **which reason deserves marks.**

That is exactly what a statement–argument question asks. You are not voting; you are judging the **quality of reasons**.

## 2. Why Students Get Stuck

- They answer with their own opinion ("I like phones, so 'Yes' is strong").
- They think every "No" argument is weak because the policy sounds good.
- They fall for arguments that sound emotional or dramatic.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Is your personal opinion on the issue part of the answer? (No — only the quality of the reason matters.)

## 3. What Makes an Argument Strong?

A strong argument is:

1. **Directly related** to the statement — it talks about the actual step being proposed.
2. **Important and serious** — it touches health, safety, education, economy, rights or the environment, not a trivial matter.
3. **Generally true** — it is based on facts or widely accepted reasoning, not on one example or a guess.
4. **Logically connected** — the reason actually leads to "Yes" or "No".

Example — *Should helmets be compulsory for two-wheeler riders?*
- "Yes, helmets reduce deaths in road accidents." → **strong** (serious, factual, relevant).

## 4. The Six Faces of a Weak Argument (Mnemonic: "SIMPLE-T")

| Weak type | What it looks like | Example |
|-----------|--------------------|---------|
| **S**uperlative / extreme | only, always, never, all, every | "No, all students are lazy." |
| **I**rrelevant | not about the step at all | "Yes, buses are painted red." |
| **M**ere example or tradition | one case, "this was always done" | "No, my uncle dropped his phone once." |
| **P**ersonal / trivial | comfort, looks, liking | "No, helmets spoil my hairstyle." |
| **L**ogic gap / assumption | the result does not follow | "Yes, then everyone will become rich overnight." |
| **E**xaggerated result + **T**autology | repeats the statement as its reason | "Yes, plastic should be banned because it should not be used." |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which word in "No, all doctors are rich" makes it weak?

## 5. Judging Each Argument Separately

Always judge argument I and argument II **separately**. Both can be strong at the same time — one gives a real benefit, the other a real cost. This is very common at Graduation level.

Example — *Should the retirement age be raised to 65?*
- I. Yes, experienced employees can serve longer. → strong
- II. No, it reduces job openings for the young. → strong
**Answer: Both I and II are strong.**

## 6. A 3-Step Method

1. **Read the statement** and note the exact step (ban, make compulsory, allow, reduce…).
2. **Test each argument** with the four questions: related? serious? generally true? logical?
3. **Check for SIMPLE-T words** (only, all, never, overnight, my uncle…). One such word usually makes an argument weak.

## 7. Summary Table

| Check | Strong if… | Weak if… |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| Relation | talks about the actual step | talks about something else |
| Importance | serious public matter | personal comfort or looks |
| Truth | general fact | one example, guess or rumour |
| Logic | reason leads to the conclusion | assumption or exaggeration |
| Words | measured language | only, all, never, overnight |

📅 **Review this chapter after 2 days**, then solve one practice set with a timer.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

1. **Your opinion vs the reason:** an argument you disagree with can still be strong. Judge the reason, not the side.
2. **The emotional argument:** a dramatic line ("children will suffer forever") without basis is weak, however moving it sounds.
3. **Both sides strong:** students often pick only one strong argument. When both give serious and real consequences, the answer is "Both are strong".
