Card 1
Front: What makes an argument "strong" in a statement–argument question?
Back: It is directly related to the step, important, generally true and logically connected to "Yes" or "No".

Card 2
Front: Does your personal opinion decide whether an argument is strong?
Back: No. You judge only the quality of the reason, not which side you agree with.

Card 3
Front: What does the mnemonic SIMPLE-T stand for?
Back: Superlative words, Irrelevant, Mere example/tradition, Personal/trivial, Logic gap, Exaggerated result and Tautology — the faces of a weak argument.

Card 4
Front: Which words usually signal a weak argument?
Back: only, all, every, always, never, overnight.

Card 5
Front: "No, helmets spoil my hairstyle." Why is it weak?
Back: It is a trivial personal matter compared with saving lives.

Card 6
Front: Can both arguments be strong?
Back: Yes — when one gives a real benefit and the other a real cost of the same step.

Card 7
Front: "Yes, other countries do it." Strong or weak?
Back: Weak by itself — it gives no reason why the step suits our situation.

Card 8
Front: What is a tautology argument?
Back: One that repeats the statement as its reason, e.g. "Ban plastic because plastic should not be used."

Card 9
Front: "No, my uncle once had a bad experience." Why is this weak?
Back: One example cannot support a general policy.

Card 10
Front: What is the trick "Would a sensible principal note it down?"
Back: Imagine a fair decision-maker. Serious points (health, money, rights) get noted — strong. Trivial ones do not — weak.

Card 11
Front: How should you judge argument I and argument II?
Back: Separately. Never decide one by comparing it with the other.

Card 12
Front: An argument predicts that a step will end a problem "overnight". Strong or weak?
Back: Weak — it promises an exaggerated, unrealistic result.

Card 13
Front: Examiner's trap: an emotional argument with no basis — how do you treat it?
Back: As weak. Emotion is not evidence.

Card 14
Front: Examiner's trap: both arguments give serious consequences, but you pick only one. What is the mistake?
Back: Missing that both are strong. At Graduation level, "Both strong" is a frequent answer.

Card 15
Front: When should you skip a statement–argument question?
Back: If after 45 seconds both arguments still seem equally uncertain; mark it and return later.

Card 16
Front: What are the four tests in order?
Back: Related → Important → Generally true → Logical.

Card 17
Front: "Yes, it will increase voter participation." Is it strong?
Back: Yes — it is relevant, important for democracy and logically linked to the step.
