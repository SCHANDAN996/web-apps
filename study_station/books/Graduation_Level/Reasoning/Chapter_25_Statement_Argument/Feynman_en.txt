# Feynman Technique — Statement and Argument

## The Hardest Idea: "Strong does not mean the side I agree with."

Students mix up two different questions: "Do I agree?" and "Is this a good reason?" The exam asks only the second one.

## A Story: The School Canteen Vote

The principal asks the class: "Should we stop selling samosas in the canteen?"

- Neha says: "Yes, fried food every day is bad for our health."
- Kabir says: "No, the canteen earns most of its money from samosas, and that money pays for the canteen staff."
- Tanvi says: "No, samosas are tasty."

Neha loves samosas, but she knows her own reason is strong — it is about health, it is true in general, and it is directly about the decision. Kabir's reason is also strong: it is about real money and real jobs. Tanvi's reason is weak — it is only about taste.

So the strong arguments are Neha's and Kabir's, even though they argue on **opposite sides**.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "Would a sensible principal note it down?"

Imagine a fair principal with a notebook. For each argument ask: "Would the principal write this down as a serious point?"
- Health, safety, money, jobs, rights, environment → **yes, note it** (strong).
- Taste, looks, "everyone", "never", "my cousin said" → **no** (weak).
