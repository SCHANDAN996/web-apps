# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Statement & Course of Action

## The Hardest Part, Explained Simply

The trickiest thing in this chapter is not understanding the statement — it is deciding **whether a proposed action is actually a valid response to it**. Most students fail because they pick actions that *sound* nice instead of actions that *logically follow*.

Let me tell you a story.

## The Story of Ravi and the Leaking Roof

Ravi, 12 years old, comes home from school and finds water dripping from the ceiling onto his books.

**Statement:** "The roof of Ravi's house is leaking badly during the monsoon."

Now, four neighbours suggest "courses of action":

1. **Neighbour A:** "Call a mason immediately and repair the roof."
2. **Neighbour B:** "Ravi should stop going to school until the rain stops."
3. **Neighbour C:** "The family should buy new books because the old ones got wet."
4. **Neighbour D:** "The government should be blamed for poor drainage in the colony."

Which ones actually *follow*?

- **A follows.** It directly attacks the problem stated (leaking roof) with a practical, immediate step.
- **B does not follow.** It punishes Ravi and solves nothing — the roof still leaks.
- **C does not follow.** It treats a *side-effect*, not the problem. New books will also get wet tomorrow.
- **D does not follow.** Blaming someone is not an action; it fixes nothing.

That is the entire chapter in one story: **a course of action must directly address the problem in the statement, be practical, and be logically connected — not emotional, not a blame-game, not a side-effect fix.**

## The Golden Filter (Ask These 3 Questions)

For every proposed action, ask:

1. **Does it attack the actual problem** mentioned in the statement? (Not a symptom, not a side issue.)
2. **Is it practical and legal?** (No "kill all corrupt people" type extremes.)
3. **Is it an action, not an opinion?** ("The government is careless" is a judgement, not a course of action.)

If the answer to any question is NO → the action does **not** follow.

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"FIX the Leak"**

Remember **F-I-X**:

- **F — Focused:** Does it fix the *stated* problem, not a side-effect? (Ravi's books ≠ the roof.)
- **I — Immediate & Implementable:** Can someone actually *do* it? (Blaming the government is not doable action.)
- **X — eXclude extremes & emotions:** Revenge, boycotts, "stop everything" actions are almost always wrong.

If an action passes F-I-X, it follows. If it fails even one, drop it.

## One More Trap: "Either / Or"

Sometimes two actions are genuine alternatives (repair the roof OR shift to a rented room). If both are practical and mutually exclusive, the answer may be "either I or II follows." But if one is practical and the other is an escape (like Ravi quitting school), only one follows. Never mark "both" just because both sound positive.
