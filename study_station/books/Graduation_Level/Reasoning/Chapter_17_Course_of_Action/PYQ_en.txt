# PYQ Analysis — Statement & Course of Action (English)

## 1. How Exams Use This Chapter

Statement & Course of Action appears regularly in **Banking exams (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RBI)** and **SSC CGL/CHSL**, and occasionally in **Railway (RRB NTPC)** and state PCS prelims. It is a *judgement-based* chapter: there are no formulas, only disciplined logic. Across exams, the questions cluster into a few repeating families:

- **Problem → Solution type**: A problem is stated (waterlogging, exam paper leak, rising accidents); you must judge which proposed action(s) logically and practically address it.
- **Only-one-follows traps**: Two actions are given where one is a genuine remedy and the other is extreme, impractical, or unrelated.
- **"Either I or II" traps**: Two mutually exclusive actions where exactly one is appropriate — students wrongly mark "both".
- **"Neither" traps**: Both actions sound dramatic but neither addresses the root cause.
- **Administrative realism**: Actions must be within the authority and capacity of the body mentioned (a school cannot amend the Constitution; a municipal body cannot print currency).

## 2. Sub-topics That Recur Most

1. **Civic/administrative problems** — traffic, sanitation, water supply, power cuts. The correct action is usually a *systemic* fix (policy, enforcement, infrastructure), not a cosmetic one.
2. **Institutional problems** — schools, banks, companies facing indiscipline, fraud, or falling performance. Correct action: inquiry + corrective measure; wrong action: instant dismissal/punishment without inquiry.
3. **Public safety & health** — epidemics, accidents, adulteration. Correct action: immediate relief *plus* long-term prevention.
4. **Extreme-action distractors** — "shut down the department", "arrest all employees", "boycott the event". These are almost always wrong.

## 3. Examiner's Psychological Traps (in words)

- **The "sounds strict = sounds right" trap**: Harsh actions feel decisive; examiners know students equate severity with correctness.
- **The partial-address trap**: An action that treats only the symptom (distributing umbrellas during floods) is offered alongside the real fix (drainage reform).
- **The authority mismatch trap**: The proposed action is sensible but impossible for the actor named in the statement.
- **The "both feel nice" trap**: Two actions, one preventive and one curative — sometimes both follow, sometimes only one; the wording of the statement decides.
- **The emotional appeal trap**: Actions appealing to public sentiment ("people should be emotionally appealed to") are rarely the *logical* course.

## 4. Time Rule Review (15-sec / 45-sec)

- **15-second questions**: Short statement, two clearly distinct actions, one obviously extreme. Read → spot the extreme → mark. These are the majority in SSC.
- **45-second questions**: Statement with a nuanced problem and two plausible actions, or "either/or" framing. Worth the time only if you can identify the root cause quickly.
- **Skip**: Questions where both actions are long, similarly worded, and the distinction depends on fine administrative detail — these eat 90+ seconds and remain 50/50. Mark and move.

## 5. Ten High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

**Q1.** Statement: Many pilgrims died in a stampede at a temple festival due to overcrowding.
Courses of Action:
I. The government should ban all religious gatherings in the state.
II. The administration should regulate entry through passes and deploy crowd-control measures at such events.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Banning all gatherings (I) is the classic *extreme-action trap* — disproportionate and impractical. II addresses the root cause (uncontrolled crowding) with a feasible administrative step.
**Trap used:** Severity = correctness illusion.

**Q2.** Statement: A large number of students failed in the board examination this year.
Courses of Action:
I. The board should conduct an inquiry into the causes of mass failure.
II. All the failed students should be expelled from their schools.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Inquiry (I) is the rational first step — you cannot fix a problem you haven't diagnosed. II is punishment without inquiry, a recurring wrong-option pattern.
**Trap used:** Instant-punishment distractor.

**Q3.** Statement: The municipal corporation has failed to clear garbage from the streets for two weeks, raising fears of an epidemic.
Courses of Action:
I. The corporation should immediately deploy additional sanitation workers and vehicles to clear the backlog.
II. Residents should be advised to dump their garbage inside their homes until the crisis passes.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is a direct, practical remedy within the corporation's capacity. II is absurd and unhygienic — a *cosmetic/absurd-action trap*.
**Trap used:** An option that "does something" but worsens the problem.

**Q4.** Statement: Cases of cyber fraud through fake banking calls have risen sharply in the city.
Courses of Action:
I. Banks should launch awareness campaigns warning customers never to share OTPs or passwords.
II. The police cyber cell should set up a dedicated helpline and trace the fraud networks.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** I is preventive, II is curative; both logically follow and are within the respective authorities. This is the genuine "both follow" pattern — don't let the "either/or" habit push you away from (c).
**Trap used:** Overcorrection — students who learned "usually only one follows" wrongly reject a legitimate both.

**Q5.** Statement: The prices of onions have tripled in the wholesale market within a month.
Courses of Action:
I. The government should fix the retail price of onions at last year's level by law.
II. The government should release buffer stock and ease imports to increase supply.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Price rise here is a supply problem; II attacks the cause. I is an *authority/practicality trap* — rigid price-fixing ignores market reality and creates shortages/black markets.
**Trap used:** An action that sounds protective but is economically unworkable.

**Q6.** Statement: Despite repeated warnings, employees of a private firm continue to leak confidential data to competitors.
Courses of Action:
I. The firm should identify the source of leaks through an internal investigation and take action against those found guilty.
II. The firm should stop sharing any information with any employee.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I follows the inquiry-then-action principle. II would paralyse the company — extreme and self-defeating.
**Trap used:** "Stop everything" extremism.
**Time rule:** 15-second question — II is visibly absurd; mark instantly.

**Q7.** Statement: A passenger train derailed because of a fractured track, killing several people.
Courses of Action:
I. The railway should order a high-level inquiry and fix accountability.
II. The railway should immediately inspect and repair tracks on all high-traffic routes.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** I delivers accountability for the past incident; II prevents the next one. Both are standard, feasible administrative responses.
**Trap used:** Students often pick only II ("prevention matters more") — but accountability inquiries are a standard correct action after disasters.

**Q8.** Statement: The state education department has found that 40% of government school teachers remain absent on working days.
Courses of Action:
I. All absent teachers should be dismissed from service immediately.
II. A biometric attendance system should be introduced and salaries linked to verified attendance.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Mass dismissal without due process (I) is the harsh-action trap and administratively impossible. II is a systemic, enforceable fix.
**Trap used:** Severity illusion again — the most frequently repeated trap in this chapter.

**Q9.** Statement: A multinational company proposes to set up a plant that will employ 5,000 people but may pollute the local river.
Courses of Action:
I. The project should be rejected outright to save the river.
II. The project should be cleared only after mandatory environmental safeguards and effluent-treatment conditions are imposed.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Outright rejection (I) sacrifices 5,000 jobs when a middle path exists. II balances development and environment — the mature administrative answer.
**Trap used:** "Either I or II" lure — the two actions are not truly exclusive; II subsumes the concern behind I.
**Time rule:** Borderline 45-second — the either/or framing tempts haste; skip if you cannot quickly see that II dominates I.

**Q10.** Statement: Frequent power cuts in the industrial area have forced several small factories to shut down.
Courses of Action:
I. The state electricity board should repair and upgrade the local distribution infrastructure.
II. Factory owners should be asked to run their units only at night.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I fixes the root cause (failing infrastructure). II shifts the burden onto victims and is impractical for most industries — a *burden-shifting trap*.
**Trap used:** An action that "manages" the problem instead of solving it.
**Time rule:** 15-second question — II is clearly unreasonable.

## 6. Pattern Summary for Revision

- Inquiry/investigation before punishment → almost always correct.
- Extreme, total, or absurd actions (ban all, dismiss all, stop all) → almost always wrong.
- Preventive + curative pair → often "both follow"; check each on its own merit.
- Action must match the authority and capacity of the actor in the statement.
- Root-cause fixes beat symptom-management and burden-shifting.
- 15-sec rule: if one option is visibly extreme/absurd, answer immediately. 45-sec rule: invest time only when both options look plausible. Skip when both are long and near-identical in tone.
