Card 1
Front: What is the very first step before solving any Decision Making question?
Back: Identify the core problem and the decision-maker's role. Ask: "Who am I in this situation, and what exactly must I decide?" Without this, every option looks equally attractive.

Card 2
Front: In Decision Making questions, why is the "most emotional" option usually wrong?
Back: Examiners reward balanced, lawful, and practical choices — not impulsive or sentimental reactions. The correct option solves the problem within rules while considering all stakeholders.

Card 3
Front: What does the mnemonic "P-O-L-A-R" stand for in Decision Making?
Back: Problem → Options → Legality → Accountability → Result. Check each option against these five filters; the option surviving all five is the answer.

Card 4
Front: What is the "eliminate extremes" trick in Decision Making?
Back: Options that are too harsh (dismiss the employee immediately) or too lenient (ignore the misconduct) are usually wrong. The correct answer is typically the firm-but-fair middle path.

Card 5
Front: A colleague is leaking confidential data. You are torn between friendship and duty. What wins in the exam?
Back: Duty and organizational interest always win over personal relationships in Decision Making questions. Report through proper channels — never confront privately or ignore it.

Card 6
Front: Examiner's trap: An option says "take strict action against the complainant for raising a false alarm." Why is this dangerous?
Back: It punishes the whistle-blower instead of investigating the issue. Correct decisions address the problem, not the person who reported it. This trap tests whether you shoot the messenger.

Card 7
Front: What should you do when two options both look correct?
Back: Choose the one that is (a) within your authority, (b) follows rules/procedure, and (c) resolves the root cause — not just the symptom. When still tied, prefer the option involving consultation or proper channel.

Card 8
Front: In "course of action" questions, when is an action considered valid?
Back: When it logically follows from the problem, is practical, legal, and directly reduces or solves the stated problem. Actions based on assumptions, revenge, or vague future promises are invalid.

Card 9
Front: What is the difference between an "assumption" and a "course of action"?
Back: An assumption is something taken for granted behind a statement; a course of action is a practical step to solve a problem. Decision Making questions test the latter — don't mix the two formats.

Card 10
Front: Why should you never pick an option that says "wait and watch" when immediate harm is occurring?
Back: "Wait and watch" is valid only when the situation is stable and more information is genuinely needed. If damage is ongoing (accident, fraud in progress, safety risk), immediate action options win.

Card 11
Front: What does the mnemonic "R-U-L-E" remind you to check in every option?
Back: Role (is it within my authority?), Urgency (does timing match the crisis?), Legality (is it lawful/procedural?), Empathy (does it consider people affected?). Fail any one → eliminate.

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: An option offers a "quick fix" that violates procedure slightly but helps everyone. Should you pick it?
Back: No. In exam logic, procedural violations are never justified by good intentions. The correct answer achieves the goal through proper channels — even if it seems slower.

Card 13
Front: In administrative Decision Making, who is the ideal decision-maker you should imagine yourself as?
Back: A responsible, impartial officer who follows rules, acts promptly, consults seniors when needed, and prioritizes public/organizational interest over personal convenience.

Card 14
Front: When a question describes a conflict between two employees, what type of option is usually correct?
Back: One that involves hearing both sides impartially, gathering facts, and then deciding per rules. Options favouring one side without inquiry, or ignoring the conflict, are traps.

Card 15
Front: How do you handle options containing words like "always," "never," or "immediately dismiss"?
Back: Treat absolute words as red flags. Decision Making rewards measured, situational responses. Absolute or extreme options are almost always incorrect.

Card 16
Front: What is the correct approach when you lack complete information in the given situation?
Back: Choose the option that gathers more information or consults relevant authority before acting — provided no emergency exists. Acting decisively on incomplete data is a classic trap.

Card 17
Front: In "passage-based" Decision Making sets, what should you read first?
Back: Read the situation once carefully, noting your designation, the stakeholders, and the constraint (time, rules, resources). Then evaluate each question's options strictly against those facts — not general opinion.

Card 18
Front: Why is "delegate the problem to a subordinate" usually a wrong option?
Back: Decision Making tests accountability. Passing your own responsibility downward shows evasion. Correct options show you owning the decision, though consulting seniors or experts is acceptable.

Card 19
Front: What is the 30-second rule for Decision Making questions?
Back: If an option fails the legality or role check, reject it in under 30 seconds without overthinking. Save time for the 2–3 genuinely close options where the R-U-L-E filter decides.

Card 20
Front: What single question separates the best option from a merely good one?
Back: "Does this option solve the root cause within rules and my authority?" A good option treats symptoms; the best option fixes the cause — legally, promptly, and fairly.
