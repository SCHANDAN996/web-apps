# 🧒 Critical Reasoning — Explained Like You're 12

## The Story of the Missing Mangoes

Imagine this. One morning, your neighbour Sharma ji storms into your house and announces:

> "Your dog ate my mangoes! I saw him near my tree yesterday, and today the mangoes are gone. Case closed!"

Now, most people would either argue or apologise. But you? You're going to do what a Critical Reasoning champion does — you're going to **take his argument apart like a toy car** and check every screw.

Sharma ji's argument has three parts:

1. **The Evidence (Premise):** "I saw your dog near my tree yesterday."
2. **The Conclusion:** "Your dog ate my mangoes."
3. **The Hidden Assumption:** The part he *didn't say out loud* — "If the dog was near the tree, then the dog must be the one who ate the mangoes."

That third part is the secret. Every argument in Critical Reasoning has a **hidden bridge** between the evidence and the conclusion. Sharma ji *assumed* the dog's presence proves guilt. But what if a monkey ate the mangoes? What if Sharma ji's own kids picked them? The moment you find that hidden bridge, you can either **strengthen** it ("The dog was seen with mango pulp on his face!") or **weaken** it ("A monkey was filmed stealing the mangoes!").

**That is the entire chapter.** Every Critical Reasoning question — assumption, strengthen, weaken, inference, conclusion, argument evaluation — is just a game of *"Find Sharma ji's hidden bridge."*

## The Three Questions You Always Ask

Whenever you read an argument in the exam, ask yourself, like a detective:

1. **What is the conclusion?** (What does the writer *want* me to believe?)
2. **What is the evidence?** (What facts are given to support it?)
3. **What is the gap?** (What must be true for the evidence to actually lead to the conclusion?)

The gap is where 90% of answers hide.

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"The Mango Test"** 🥭

Before picking any answer option, ask:

> **"Does this option touch the bridge between the evidence and the conclusion — or is it just wandering near the tree?"**

- An option that talks about the *topic* but not the *logical link* is a trap. (e.g., "Dogs like mangoes" — interesting, but doesn't prove *this* dog ate *those* mangoes.)
- The correct answer almost always **connects or breaks the link** between premise and conclusion.
- For **assumption** questions, use the **Negation Test**: say the option in reverse ("The dog was NOT the only animal near the tree"). If destroying the option destroys the argument, that option IS the assumption. If the argument survives, reject it.

One more trap the examiner loves: **extreme words**. Options with "always," "never," "all," "only" are usually wrong in inference questions — real conclusions are humble ("some," "may," "likely").

## Time Rule

- **15 seconds:** Spot the conclusion (usually after "therefore," "so," "hence," "thus").
- **30 seconds:** Find the evidence and the gap.
- **15 seconds:** Eliminate options that fail the Mango Test.
- If an argument feels too tangled after 60 seconds, mark it and move on — one tough argument is not worth three easy questions.
