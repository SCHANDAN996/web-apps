# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Critical Reasoning (Advanced Level)

## 1. How Exams Test This Chapter

Critical Reasoning appears across SSC CGL (Tier-I & II), SSC CHSL, IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RRB NTPC, and State PCS prelims in a handful of recurring formats. Instead of counting questions year by year (we do not have verified official data for that), here is what a careful reading of available papers shows about **patterns**:

**Recurring question families:**

1. **Statement & Assumption** — "Which assumption is implicit?" The single most repeated family. Exams love assumptions that *paraphrase* the statement (a trap) versus assumptions that the speaker must truly take for granted.
2. **Statement & Conclusion** — Conclusions that go *beyond* the statement ("all", "always", "only") are the standard trap.
3. **Statement & Course of Action** — Only practical, logical, problem-solving actions are valid; extreme or punitive actions are traps.
4. **Strong vs Weak Arguments** — "Should India…?" style. Emotional, one-sided, or example-based arguments are weak; structural, reasoned arguments are strong.
5. **Cause & Effect** — Two statements: common cause, direct cause-effect, or independent events? Chronology confusion is the favourite trap.
6. **Inference from a passage** (Banking exams especially) — "definitely true / probably true / cannot be determined" gradations.
7. **Assertion & Reason** — Both true but R not explaining A is the classic answer students miss.
8. **Strengthen / Weaken** (growing in Banking and CGL Tier-II) — identify what supports or attacks the argument's *conclusion*, not its premises.

**Exam-wise flavour (qualitative):**

- **SSC exams (CGL, CHSL):** short one-liner statements; assumption/conclusion dominate; options are usually "only I", "only II", "both", "neither".
- **RRB NTPC (Railway):** similar short-format assumption/conclusion and cause-effect items, usually at a slightly easier level than CGL.
- **IBPS / SBI PO (Banking):** longer passages, inference gradations, strengthen/weaken, and "which of the following can be concluded" — more time-consuming.
- **State PCS:** assertion-reason and course-of-action appear frequently, often with governance-themed statements.

**The examiner's favourite traps (in words):**

- **Almost-true conclusion:** restates the statement with one word upgraded ("some" → "all").
- **Emotional argument:** sounds morally right but gives no reason — a *weak* argument dressed as strong.
- **Chronology illusion:** B happened after A, so B "must" be A's effect — often they share a common cause.
- **Extreme course of action:** "dismiss all employees" — decisive-sounding but illogical.
- **Negative wording:** "Which is NOT implicit?" — students answer the positive version in a hurry.

---

## 2. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Review

- **15-second rule:** If after 15 seconds you cannot even classify the question type (assumption? conclusion? cause-effect?), mark and move on.
- **45-second rule:** Assumption/conclusion/argument questions with one-line statements should be solved within 45 seconds. If you are re-reading the statement a third time, your answer is probably being pulled by a trap option — pick by elimination and move.
- **Skip candidates:** long-passage inference questions (Banking style) with 4–5 line statements and "probably true / cannot be determined" gradations — budget these for the second pass, 90+ seconds each.
- **Never skip:** short assertion-reason and cause-effect questions — they are the fastest marks in this chapter.

## 3. Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions & Trap Analysis)

**Q1. Statement:** "The government has decided to levy a congestion tax on private vehicles entering the city centre during peak hours."
**Assumptions:**
I. People may shift to public transport to avoid the tax.
II. The tax can be collected effectively at entry points.
(a) Only I is implicit (b) Only II is implicit (c) Both are implicit (d) Neither is implicit
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Any policy decision assumes both that it will influence behaviour (I) and that it is implementable (II).
**Trap:** Students reject I thinking "may" makes it uncertain — but an assumption need only be *taken for granted by the decision-maker*, not guaranteed.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CGL pattern)

**Q2. Statement:** "Regular exercise reduces the risk of heart disease."
**Conclusions:**
I. All people who exercise regularly will never get heart disease.
II. Exercise is beneficial for heart health.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "Reduces risk" ≠ "eliminates risk"; I upgrades "reduces" to "never" — the classic almost-true trap. II is a safe restatement.
**Trap:** Absolute words ("all", "never") smuggled into the conclusion.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CHSL pattern)

**Q3. Statement:** "Several passengers were injured when a bus overturned on the highway due to over-speeding."
**Courses of Action:**
I. The driver should be suspended and an inquiry ordered.
II. Speed governors should be made mandatory on all highway buses.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** I addresses accountability for this incident; II addresses the systemic cause. Both are practical and logical.
**Trap:** Students mark II as "too extreme" — but a preventive regulation is a standard valid course of action, unlike punitive mass actions.
**Source:** PYQ-style (State PCS pattern)

**Q4. Statement:** Should India make voting compulsory for all eligible citizens?
**Arguments:**
I. Yes, it will ensure that election results truly reflect the will of the people.
II. No, voting is a personal choice and forcing it violates individual freedom.
(a) Only I is strong (b) Only II is strong (c) Both are strong (d) Neither is strong
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Both give reasoned, structural grounds — democratic legitimacy vs individual liberty. Strong arguments can exist on both sides.
**Trap:** Students pick the side they personally agree with. Strength is about *reasoning quality*, not your opinion.
**Source:** PYQ-style (IBPS pattern)

**Q5. Statements:**
I. The prices of vegetables have risen sharply this month.
II. Unseasonal rains damaged standing crops in several districts.
(a) I is the cause and II is the effect
(b) II is the cause and I is the effect
(c) Both are effects of a common cause
(d) Both are independent causes
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Crop damage (II) reduces supply, which pushes prices up (I) — a direct cause-effect chain.
**Trap:** Chronology illusion in reverse — students see "prices rose" first in the sentence and assume it is the cause. Read the logic, not the order of presentation.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CGL pattern)

**Q6. Assertion (A):** The Sahara desert is expanding southward.
**Reason (R):** Overgrazing and deforestation in the Sahel region degrade the land.
(a) Both A and R are true and R explains A
(b) Both are true but R does not explain A
(c) A is true, R is false
(d) A is false, R is true
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Land degradation in the Sahel is a documented driver of desertification — R directly explains A.
**Trap:** The famous "both true but not the explanation" option (b) tempts students who overthink; here the causal link is genuine.
**Source:** NCERT Class 11 Geography (Fundamentals of Physical Geography)

**Q7. Passage:** "Company X's profits rose by 20% this year. The company attributes this to its new cost-cutting policy introduced last year."
**Which of the following, if true, most weakens the company's claim?**
(a) The company also launched a new product line last year that became a bestseller.
(b) Cost-cutting reduced the company's expenses.
(c) Other companies in the sector also saw profit growth.
(d) The policy was implemented by an experienced manager.
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** (a) offers an alternative cause for the profit rise, directly attacking the attribution. (b) supports the claim; (c) is about the sector, not this company's attribution; (d) is irrelevant.
**Trap:** (c) feels like a weakener ("everyone grew") but does not break the internal link between the policy and Company X's profit.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SBI PO pattern)

**Q8. Statement:** "The school has instructed parents to ensure their children do not carry mobile phones to school."
**Assumptions:**
I. Parents are in a position to enforce this instruction.
II. Mobile phones are harmful in every possible way.
(a) Only I is implicit (b) Only II is implicit (c) Both are implicit (d) Neither is implicit
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The instruction presupposes parents can act on it (I). II is an extreme generalisation — the school need only believe phones are disruptive, not "harmful in every possible way".
**Trap:** Extreme-word assumption ("every possible way") — the single most repeated assumption trap.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CGL pattern)

**Q9. Passage (Banking style):** "The RBI's report notes that digital payment transactions in rural areas have grown, though cash remains the dominant mode for daily purchases in these regions."
**Which inference is definitely true?**
(a) Cash will soon disappear from rural areas.
(b) Digital transactions in rural areas have increased.
(c) Urban areas have fully adopted digital payments.
(d) Rural people distrust digital payments.
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** (b) is a direct restatement of "have grown". (a) is a prediction, (c) is outside the passage's scope, (d) invents a motive not stated.
**Trap:** (d) is psychologically plausible — inference questions punish "reasonable-sounding" additions beyond the text.
**Source:** PYQ-style (IBPS PO pattern)
**⏱ Skip note:** Passage-based inference items like this deserve the second pass if the passage exceeds 3 lines — do not burn 90 seconds here in the first round.

**Q10. Statement:** "Despite a nationwide awareness campaign, the number of road accidents caused by drunk driving has not declined."
**Which of the following best explains the paradox?**
(a) The campaign reached only urban audiences while enforcement checks on highways were simultaneously reduced.
(b) People generally ignore awareness campaigns.
(c) Road accidents are impossible to prevent.
(d) Drunk driving is a serious offence.
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** (a) supplies a concrete mechanism — awareness without enforcement fails — resolving the paradox. (b) and (c) are sweeping generalisations; (d) is true but explains nothing.
**Trap:** (d) is a "true but irrelevant" option — the most common paradox-question trap.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CGL Tier-II pattern)
**⏱ Skip note:** Paradox/explain questions with four long options often exceed 45 seconds; if two options both look plausible after one reading, mark and revisit rather than forcing a guess.

## 4. Quick Revision of the Chapter's PYQ Lessons

1. Assumption = what the speaker *takes for granted*, not what is desirable or true in general.
2. Conclusions with "all / always / never / only" are guilty until proven innocent.
3. Courses of action must be practical, legal, and problem-solving — never extreme or retaliatory.
4. Argument strength is about reasoning, not your personal opinion or emotional appeal.
5. In cause-effect, follow the logic chain, not the sentence order.
6. In inference, "definitely true" means *only* what the text says — plausible additions are traps.
7. Assertion-Reason: check the *link*, not just the truth of each part.
8. Apply the 15/45-second rule ruthlessly: short assumption/conclusion items first, long-passage inference and paradox items in the second pass.
