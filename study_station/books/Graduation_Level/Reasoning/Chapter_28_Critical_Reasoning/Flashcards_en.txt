Card 1
Front: What are the three building blocks of any critical reasoning argument?
Back: Premise (given facts), Conclusion (the claim being argued), and Assumption (the unstated link between them). Every strengthen/weaken question attacks one of these three.

Card 2
Front: In a "weaken the argument" question, what is the single most powerful target?
Back: The assumption. If you break the hidden link between premise and conclusion, the argument collapses — attacking a premise is weaker because premises are stated as facts.

Card 3
Front: What mnemonic helps you remember the order of solving any CR question?
Back: "C-Q-P-A" — Conclusion first, then Question type, then Premises, then Answer choices. Finding the conclusion before reading options prevents trap answers from pulling you off track.

Card 4
Front: How do you spot the conclusion of an argument in under 5 seconds?
Back: Look for indicator words: "therefore," "thus," "hence," "so," "clearly," "it follows that." If none exist, ask: "What is the author trying to make me believe?"

Card 5
Front: What is the "Negation Test" and when do you use it?
Back: Used in assumption questions: negate the answer choice — if the argument falls apart when the choice is false, that choice is a necessary assumption. If the argument survives, reject the choice.

Card 6
Front: Examiner's trap: an option that is factually TRUE but does not affect the argument — why is it wrong?
Back: In strengthen/weaken questions, relevance beats truth. An option can be 100% true in real life yet be the wrong answer if it neither supports nor damages the link between the given premise and conclusion.

Card 7
Front: What is the difference between a "necessary assumption" and a "sufficient assumption"?
Back: Necessary: the argument cannot work without it (test by negation). Sufficient: if true, it guarantees the conclusion. Exam questions asking "the argument assumes" almost always mean necessary.

Card 8
Front: In an inference ("must be true") question, what is the golden rule for choosing the answer?
Back: The correct inference uses ONLY the information given — no outside knowledge, no exaggeration. Reject options with extreme words (all, never, must always) unless the passage itself is that extreme.

Card 9
Front: What mnemonic reminds you which words make an inference option suspicious?
Back: "A-N-O" — All, Never, Only. Extreme words in options are usually traps in inference questions; safe answers use "some," "may," "can," "likely."

Card 10
Front: What is a "cause-and-effect" flaw, and how do examiners test it?
Back: The argument assumes X caused Y just because X and Y occurred together (correlation ≠ causation). To weaken: show a third factor caused both, or that Y caused X (reversed causality).

Card 11
Front: In "strengthen" questions, what does a correct answer typically do?
Back: It either (a) plugs a gap in the assumption, (b) rules out an alternative explanation, or (c) provides supporting evidence/analogy that makes the conclusion more likely.

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: what is the "out of scope" option and why do students pick it?
Back: It introduces new information (new groups, new time periods, new topics) not discussed in the argument. Students pick it because it sounds intelligent — but CR answers must stay within the argument's scope.

Card 13
Front: How do you handle a "paradox/explain the discrepancy" question?
Back: Both given facts are true; your job is to find the option that makes them compatible. Never pick an option that denies or contradicts either fact — the right answer resolves, not rejects.

Card 14
Front: What is the "boldface" question type asking you to identify?
Back: The ROLE each bolded statement plays in the argument — e.g., premise supporting the main conclusion, an opposing view the author rejects, or the main conclusion itself. Map the argument's structure before reading options.

Card 15
Front: In "evaluate the argument" questions, what form does the correct answer take?
Back: A question (or piece of information) whose answer would help decide whether the argument is strong or weak — i.e., different answers to it would strengthen OR weaken the conclusion.

Card 16
Front: What is the "sampling flaw" in arguments based on surveys or studies?
Back: The argument generalizes from a sample that is too small, biased, or unrepresentative. To weaken: show the sample doesn't represent the whole group; to strengthen: show it does.

Card 17
Front: Why should you read the question stem BEFORE the argument in CR?
Back: Knowing the task (weaken? infer? assume?) tells you what to hunt for while reading — assumption hunt for weaken, conservative reading for inference. It saves re-reading time (aim: ~90 seconds per CR question).

Card 18
Front: What is the "opposite answer" trap in strengthen/weaken questions?
Back: An option that does the reverse of what is asked — e.g., weakens when you were asked to strengthen. It happens when students forget the question mid-solving. Fix: underline "weaken/strengthen/EXCEPT" in the stem before reading options.

Card 19
Front: In "EXCEPT" questions, what is the correct strategy?
Back: Four options will do the stated task (e.g., weaken); the answer is the one that does NOT — it may strengthen or be neutral. Eliminate the four that clearly fit; don't hunt for the "most opposite" option.

Card 20
Front: What is an analogy-based argument and its built-in weakness?
Back: It argues that because two things are similar in some ways, what holds for one holds for the other. Weaken it by showing a relevant difference between the two; strengthen by showing the compared features are truly alike.
