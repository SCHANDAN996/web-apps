# 🧒 The Feynman Way: Inequality Explained Like You're 12

## The Story of the Hungry Crocodile

Imagine a river. On one side stands a mango. On the other side, a banana. Between them floats a hungry crocodile.

Now here's the thing about this crocodile — **he is greedy**. He always opens his mouth towards the *bigger* meal. Always. No exceptions.

- If the mango is bigger than the banana, his mouth opens towards the mango: **mango > banana**
- If the banana is bigger, his mouth flips: **mango < banana**
- If both are exactly the same size, he can't decide, so he just sits there with a flat face: **mango = banana**

That crocodile's mouth **is** the inequality sign. That's it. That's the whole chapter's foundation. The open side always faces the bigger thing. The pointy side always pokes the smaller thing.

## The Hardest Part: "Either-Or" (The Two-Door Puzzle)

Now let's tackle the thing that tricks even grown-ups in exams: **complementary pairs** — the famous "either conclusion I or II follows" questions.

Here's a story. You're standing in front of two doors. Behind one door is a prize. You don't know which. Your friend says:

- **Statement 1:** "The prize is behind Door A."
- **Statement 2:** "The prize is behind Door B."

You can't prove Statement 1. You can't prove Statement 2. But here's the magic — **one of them MUST be true**, because the prize has to be somewhere! So even though neither statement is *definitely* true on its own, **together they cover every possibility**. That's "either-or."

In inequality, this happens when two conclusions:
1. **Cover all possibilities** between two elements (like A > B and A ≤ B — together that's everything!), **and**
2. **Neither is definitely true alone**, **and**
3. **Talk about the same two elements.**

When all three conditions click — shout "EITHER-OR!" and move on.

## The Bridge Rule (Chains)

One more idea. If Ravi is taller than Mohan, and Mohan is taller than Sohan, do you need to measure Ravi and Sohan side by side? No! You just *walk across the bridge*: Ravi > Mohan > Sohan, so Ravi > Sohan.

But — and this is where the crocodile bites careless students — **the bridge only works if all the mouths face the same way.**

- A > B > C → bridge works → A > C ✅
- A > B < C → the mouths crash into each other → **no bridge, no conclusion** ❌

When signs point at each other (opposite directions), the relationship between the two ends is **unknown**. Write "CND" (Cannot Be Determined) and don't let the examiner seduce you into guessing.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "The Crocodile Traffic Rule"

🐊 **"Same direction = drive through. Opposite direction = dead end."**

Before solving any conclusion, trace the path from the first letter to the second:
- All signs open the same way (>, >, ≥) → drive through, conclusion possible.
- Signs clash (< meets >) → dead end, that conclusion dies instantly.

This one rule kills 70% of wrong options in under 5 seconds.

## Watch Out: ≥ and ≤ Are Soft Signs

"A ≥ B" means A is bigger **or equal**. So if the statement says A ≥ B and the conclusion says A > B — is it true? **No!** Because A and B might be equal. The strict crocodile mouth (>) is not guaranteed by a soft sign (≥). Examiners *love* this trap. Remember: **a soft sign can never promise a hard sign.**
