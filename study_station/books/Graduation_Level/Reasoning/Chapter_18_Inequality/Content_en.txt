# Inequality (असमानता)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Read and interpret all inequality symbols instantly (>, <, ≥, ≤, =, ≠).
2. Convert coded statements into standard inequality form.
3. Combine two or more statements to find a definite relationship.
4. Decide between "definitely true", "definitely false", and "either-or" conclusions.
5. Solve an inequality question in under 30 seconds.

---

## The Hook: One Symbol, One Mark, Ten Seconds

Inequality is a regular feature of the reasoning sections of banking exams (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO) and also appears in SSC and other government exams. Each question takes a trained student barely 15–30 seconds — while an untrained student burns 2 minutes and still picks the wrong option. The difference is not intelligence. It is one small skill: **knowing when a conclusion is *definitely* true and when it is only *maybe* true.** Master that one idea, and these become some of the fastest marks in your reasoning section.

## The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Most students stumble on Inequality for three reasons:

- They try to "feel" the answer instead of chaining the symbols logically.
- They forget that **≥ means "greater OR equal"** — so "A > B" is NOT guaranteed by "A ≥ B".
- They panic when the symbols are coded (like `A ★ B` meaning `A > B`).

Every one of these is fixable in the next 20 minutes. Let's go chunk by chunk.

---

## Chunk 1: The Six Symbols — Your Alphabet

| Symbol | Meaning | Memory cue |
|---|---|---|
| > | Greater than | The open mouth eats the bigger number |
| < | Less than | Same mouth, other side |
| ≥ | Greater than **or equal to** | "At least" |
| ≤ | Less than **or equal to** | "At most" |
| = | Equal to | — |
| ≠ | Not equal to | — |

**Golden rule of reading:** The symbol always opens toward the bigger quantity. `A > B` means A is bigger.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking up — what does `P ≤ Q` mean in words? Say it aloud: "P is less than or equal to Q." Now write the symbol for "X is at least Y". (Answer: X ≥ Y.)

---

## Chunk 2: Combining Statements — The Chain Rule

Examiners give you statements like:

> A > B, B ≥ C, C = D

and ask whether a conclusion like `A > D` follows.

**The method: build one chain.**

A > B ≥ C = D

Now compare A and D by travelling along the chain. The rule:

- If the path from A to D contains **only >, ≥, =** (all "forward-friendly"), then A > D holds **only if at least one strict `>` exists** on the path. Here A > B is strict, so **A > D is definitely true.**
- If the path contains **both > and < facing each other** (e.g., A > B < C), the chain **breaks** — no definite relation between A and C.

**The Gate Rule (mnemonic):** Think of `>` and `<` as gates.
- Gates opening the **same way** (>, ≥, =) → you can walk through; strict gate (>) anywhere means strict result.
- Gates **clashing** (A > B < C) → the door slams shut. Relation: **cannot be determined.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Statements: M ≥ N, N > O. Does M > O follow? (Yes — same-direction gates, and N > O is strict.) Does M = O follow? (No — M is strictly greater.)

---

## Chunk 3: The Three Verdicts

Every conclusion gets exactly one of three verdicts:

1. **Definitely True** — follows in every possible case.
2. **Definitely False** — contradicted in every possible case.
3. **Cannot Be Determined (CBD)** — true in some cases, false in others.

The trap is verdict 3 disguised as verdict 1. Example:

> Statement: A ≥ B. Conclusion: A > B.

Many students mark "true". Wrong! A could equal B. Since A > B is not true in *every* case, the verdict is **CBD** (as a standalone conclusion, it does not follow).

**Mantra:** *"≥ gives you 'at least', never 'strictly more'."*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Statement: X ≤ Y. Conclusion: X < Y. Follows? (No — X could equal Y.)

---

## Chunk 4: Either-Or (Complementary Pair)

Sometimes neither conclusion follows alone, but **together they cover all possibilities**. Then the answer is "either I or II follows".

Conditions for either-or:
1. Both conclusions have the **same two elements**.
2. Both are **individually not definite**.
3. Together they are **exhaustive** (e.g., A > B and A ≤ B cover everything).

Example:

> Statements: P ≥ Q, Q = R. Conclusions: I. P > R  II. P = R

P ≥ R is definite, but P > R alone isn't, and P = R alone isn't. Together they exhaust all cases → **Either I or II follows.**

---

## Chunk 5: Coded Inequalities

Banking exams love codes:

> 'A @ B' means A > B; 'A # B' means A < B; 'A $ B' means A = B; 'A % B' means A ≥ B; 'A & B' means A ≤ B.

**Strategy:** Before touching the conclusions, **decode every statement into plain symbols** on your rough sheet. Never solve in coded form — that's where silly errors breed.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If 'A % B' means A ≥ B and 'A @ B' means A > B, decode: "P % Q, Q @ R". (P ≥ Q, Q > R → so P > R follows.)

---

## Chunk 6: The 30-Second Solving Ritual

1. **Decode** (if coded) — write plain symbols.
2. **Chain** — merge statements into one line.
3. **Check each conclusion** — same-direction gates? Strict gate present?
4. **Check either-or** — same elements, both indefinite, exhaustive?
5. **Mark and move** — never re-read.

---

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Multiple possible relations shown in options.**
The examiner writes a conclusion like "A ≥ B" when the chain only proves "A > B". Students think "greater means greater-or-equal is also satisfied" and mark it true. Technically A > B does imply A ≥ B is true — but the reverse trap is deadlier: chain proves A ≥ B, option says A > B. That does NOT follow. Always check the *direction* of the implication.

**Trap 2 — Confusing coded language.**
Codes like '@', '#', '$' are deliberately chosen to look alike, and the meanings are scrambled across questions in the same paper. Decoding mentally (instead of on paper) causes sign-flips. Write the decoded chain every single time — it costs 5 seconds and saves the mark.

**Trap 3 — Double-relation (either-or) questions.**
The examiner gives two conclusions that *look* like they both fail, pushing you toward "neither follows". But if the pair is complementary (A > B / A ≤ B, or A ≥ B / A < B), the correct answer is "either-or". Before marking "neither", always run the 3-condition either-or check.

## Summary Table

| Concept | Rule |
|---|---|
| Chain same direction (>, ≥, =) | Relation exists; strict if any `>` present |
| Chain clashing (> … <) | Cannot be determined |
| ≥ or ≤ in statement | Never proves a strict (> or <) conclusion |
| Conclusion verdicts | Definitely true / definitely false / CBD |
| Either-or | Same elements + both indefinite + exhaustive |
| Coded symbols | Decode on paper first, always |
