# Data Sufficiency — The Chapter That Tests Your Judgement, Not Your Calculation

## 1. Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Understand what a Data Sufficiency (DS) question is really asking.
- Master the standard 5-option answer framework used in Banking and SSC exams.
- Decide **sufficiency without solving** — the single biggest time-saver in reasoning.
- Spot the examiner's favourite traps: hidden assumptions, combined statements, and "looks sufficient but isn't" data.
- Build a personal 30-second decision routine for every DS question.

---

## 2. The Hook: The Question That Doesn't Want an Answer

Imagine a shopkeeper asks you: *"Do you have enough money to buy this pen?"*

You check your pocket. You have ₹50. The pen costs ₹20.

Now — did you need to **buy** the pen to answer his question? No. You only needed to know whether your money was **enough**.

That is the entire soul of Data Sufficiency. The question never asks *"What is the answer?"* It asks: *"Can the answer be found from the given information?"* Students who start calculating have already lost 40 seconds. Students who only **check sufficiency** finish in 20.

---

## 3. The Common Struggle (And Why It's Normal)

Almost every aspirant makes the same three mistakes on first contact with DS:

1. **Solving the full problem** instead of stopping at "yes, it can be solved."
2. **Assuming extra information** — e.g., assuming a number is positive, or a figure is drawn to scale.
3. **Mixing the statements** before checking each one alone.

If you've done any of these, congratulations — you're human. This chapter rewires exactly these three habits.

---

## 4. The Standard Format

A DS question has three parts:

- **The Question** — e.g., "What is the value of x?"
- **Statement I** and **Statement II** — two pieces of information.
- **The Options** — usually:

> **(a)** Statement I alone is sufficient, but II alone is not.
> **(b)** Statement II alone is sufficient, but I alone is not.
> **(c)** Either statement alone is sufficient.
> **(d)** Both statements together are needed (neither alone suffices).
> **(e)** Even both together are not sufficient.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. Write the five options from memory. Which option means "each statement works independently"? (Answer: (c).)

---

## 5. The Golden Rule: Sufficiency ≠ Solution

Read this twice:

> **A statement is "sufficient" if it leads to ONE unique answer — even if you never compute that answer.**

**Example.** Question: *Is x > 0?*
Statement I: x² = 9.

Is Statement I sufficient? x could be +3 or −3. Two possibilities → **NOT sufficient.** Notice we never "solved" anything heavy; we only checked uniqueness.

**Example.** Statement II: x³ = 27.
Only x = 3 works → unique → **sufficient.** Answer: (b).

🧠 **Active Recall:** In your own words, what makes a statement "sufficient"? (One unique answer — yes/no questions need a *definite* yes or a *definite* no.)

---

## 6. The Three-Step Decision Routine (30 Seconds)

For every DS question, follow this order — never skip a step:

**Step 1 — Statement I alone.** Cover Statement II with your hand. Can I alone give a unique answer? Note YES/NO.

**Step 2 — Statement II alone.** Now cover Statement I. Can II alone give a unique answer? Note YES/NO.

**Step 3 — Combine only if needed.** Only if BOTH were NO, merge them and check again.

Then map to options:

| Step 1 (I alone) | Step 2 (II alone) | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| YES | YES | (c) Either alone |
| YES | NO | (a) I alone |
| NO | YES | (b) II alone |
| NO | NO → combined YES | (d) Both together |
| NO | NO → combined NO | (e) Not sufficient |

**Mnemonic — "I–II–Us":** *"First I, then II, then Us (together)."* If you remember the phrase **"I–II–Us"**, you will never combine statements prematurely — the #1 trap in DS.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why must you never combine statements before testing each alone? (Because if either alone works, the answer is (a), (b) or (c) — combining wastes time and can mislead you into picking (d).)

## 7. Worked Mini-Examples (Advanced Level)

**Example 1 — Number puzzle.**
Q: What is the two-digit number?
I. The sum of its digits is 9.
II. The number exceeds the number obtained by reversing its digits by 27.

- I alone: 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 81, 90 — many options → NO.
- II alone: many pairs differ by 27 (e.g., 41−14, 52−25, 63−36…) → NO.
- Together: digit sum 9 and difference 27 → only 63 fits (63 − 36 = 27, 6+3 = 9). Unique → **Answer (d).**

**Example 2 — Blood relation.**
Q: How is P related to Q?
I. P is the son of Q's father.
II. Q is the sister of R.

- I alone: P is Q's brother — but wait, is P necessarily male? "Son" fixes gender → P is Q's brother. Unique relation → sufficient? Careful: "Q's father" — P is son of Q's father, so P is Q's brother (or Q himself? No — P and Q are distinct persons). Sufficient → **Answer (a)** (II alone says nothing about P).

**Example 3 — The "yes/no" trap.**
Q: Is A the tallest among A, B, C?
I. B is taller than C.
II. A is taller than B.

- I alone: nothing about A → NO.
- II alone: nothing about C → NO.
- Together: A > B > C → definite YES → **Answer (d).** Note: a definite "yes" is just as sufficient as a definite "no."

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a yes/no DS question, is "definitely NO" a sufficient answer? (Yes! Sufficiency means a *definite* answer, not a positive one.)

---

## 8. Advanced Pointers for Graduation-Level Exams

- **Inequalities & coded relations:** Check whether the statement fixes the *direction* of the relation uniquely. "A > B" plus "B > C" fixes A > C — sufficient even without numbers.
- **Syllogism-style DS:** Sometimes a statement gives a *possible* conclusion, not a *certain* one. Possibility ≠ sufficiency.
- **Geometry DS:** Never trust the diagram. Only trust what is *stated*.
- **Hidden assumptions:** "Average of 5 numbers is 20" tells you the sum (100) — but nothing about any individual number.
- **Redundant data:** Sometimes both statements say the same thing in disguise. If I alone is sufficient and II merely repeats it, the answer is still (c) if II alone also works.

---

## 9. Summary Table

| Concept | Rule |
|---|---|
| What DS asks | "Can it be answered?" — not "What is the answer?" |
| Sufficient means | Exactly ONE unique answer (definite yes/no counts) |
| Order of checking | I alone → II alone → Together (mnemonic: **I–II–Us**) |
| Never do | Solve fully, assume unstated facts, trust diagrams |
| Both alone work | Option (c) — either alone sufficient |
| Neither alone, together works | Option (d) |
| Nothing works | Option (e) — and that's a *valid* answer, not a failure |

📌 **Review this chapter after 2 days.** Redo the three worked examples with a covered answer key, and recite the "I–II–Us" routine once before every practice set.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — "Looks sufficient, isn't."**
The examiner designs Statement I to *feel* complete. Example: "x is a prime number less than 10." Feels precise — but x could be 2, 3, 5 or 7. Four possibilities → NOT sufficient. Always count the possible answers before declaring sufficiency.

**Trap 2 — Confusing/complex wording to rattle you.**
Statements like "P is the maternal uncle of the only daughter of Q's paternal grandfather" are deliberately tangled. Don't panic — convert the sentence into a small family tree on paper, step by step. The difficulty is in the *language*, not the logic. Examiners reward students who decode slowly and punish those who guess fast.

**Trap 3 — Double-relation questions (two valid interpretations).**
Example: "A is the brother of B." Is B male or female? Unknown! If a statement leaves gender, sign (±), or direction ambiguous, and the question depends on it, the statement is NOT sufficient — even though it *sounds* informative. Also beware: combining two statements can *still* leave the ambiguity alive, pushing the answer to (e) when you expected (d).

**Bonus Trap — Combining too early.**
If Statement I alone already answers the question, the examiner hopes you'll read Statement II, merge them mentally, and wrongly mark (d). Discipline yourself with "I–II–Us": test each statement in isolation first, every single time.
