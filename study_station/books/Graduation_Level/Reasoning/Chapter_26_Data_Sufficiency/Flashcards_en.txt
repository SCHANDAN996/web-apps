Card 1
Front: What is the single golden rule of Data Sufficiency?
Back: You are NOT asked to solve the question — only to decide WHETHER the given data is enough to solve it. Stop the moment sufficiency is decided.

Card 2
Front: In Data Sufficiency, what does option "Statement I alone is sufficient" actually demand?
Back: Statement I must give a UNIQUE answer to the question by itself — without using any information from Statement II.

Card 3
Front: If a statement gives two possible answers (e.g., x = ±3), is it sufficient?
Back: No. Sufficiency requires a UNIQUE answer. Two possible values = NOT sufficient, even though you "solved" the equation.

Card 4
Front: What is the standard 5-option answer pattern in Data Sufficiency questions?
Back: (a) I alone sufficient (b) II alone sufficient (c) Either I or II alone sufficient (d) Both together needed (e) Both together not sufficient.

Card 5
Front: When is the answer "Either statement alone is sufficient"?
Back: When Statement I alone gives a unique answer AND Statement II alone also gives a unique answer — independently of each other.

Card 6
Front: What mnemonic helps you remember the correct ORDER of testing statements?
Back: "One, Two, Together" — test Statement I alone first, then Statement II alone, and only then combine both. Never jump to combining first.

Card 7
Front: What is the "RED flag" mnemonic for examiner traps in Data Sufficiency?
Back: R = Real answer not needed (don't fully solve), E = Each statement tested alone first, D = Double answers mean Deficient (not sufficient).

Card 8
Front: Examiner's trap: A question asks "Is x > 5?" and Statement I says x = 7. Is the statement sufficient even though the answer is just "Yes"?
Back: Yes! A definite "Yes" or a definite "No" both count as sufficient. Only "cannot be determined" means insufficient.

Card 9
Front: Examiner's trap: Statement I alone gives x = 4, and Statement II alone also gives x = 4. Which option do students wrongly pick?
Back: They wrongly pick "both together needed." Correct answer: "Either statement alone is sufficient" — each works independently.

Card 10
Front: In a "Who is tallest among A, B, C, D?" question, what makes a statement sufficient?
Back: It must fix a UNIQUE person at the top. A statement giving only partial order (e.g., A > B, C > D) is not sufficient alone.

Card 11
Front: In coding-based Data Sufficiency, when is a statement sufficient to find the code of a word?
Back: When comparing the given sentences lets you isolate exactly ONE common word and ONE common code — a unique one-to-one match.

Card 12
Front: In blood-relation Data Sufficiency, why is "M is the sister of N's father" alone often insufficient to answer "How is M related to N's mother?"
Back: Because the statement fixes M's relation to N (M is N's aunt) but says nothing about N's mother. Always check whether the statement actually covers the exact relation ASKED, not just any relation.

Card 13
Front: If Statement I alone is sufficient, which options are immediately eliminated?
Back: Options (b) "II alone", (d) "both together needed", and (e) "not sufficient even together" are eliminated — only (a) or (c) remain.

Card 14
Front: What should you do when both statements together still leave two possibilities?
Back: Choose "Both statements together are NOT sufficient" — combining data does not guarantee sufficiency.

Card 15
Front: In number-based Data Sufficiency, is "x is a prime number between 10 and 20" sufficient to find x?
Back: No — 11, 13, 17, 19 all fit. Multiple values = not sufficient. A unique value is mandatory.

Card 16
Front: What is the biggest time-wasting mistake in Data Sufficiency?
Back: Fully solving the problem after sufficiency is already decided. Once you know a unique answer exists, STOP — the actual value is irrelevant.

Card 17
Front: Examiner's trap: The question stem itself contains hidden information. Give an example.
Back: "Is the integer x even?" — the word "integer" in the stem is usable data. Students ignore stem information and wrongly mark statements insufficient.

Card 18
Front: In direction/distance Data Sufficiency, what two things must be known to locate a point uniquely?
Back: Both the direction AND the distance from a fixed reference point. Knowing only one of them is insufficient.

Card 19
Front: How much time should you ideally spend per Data Sufficiency question?
Back: About 40–60 seconds. If deciding sufficiency itself needs heavy calculation, mark and skip — return only if time remains.

Card 20
Front: What is the quick decision flowchart for any Data Sufficiency question?
Back: Step 1: Try I alone → unique answer? Step 2: Try II alone → unique answer? Step 3: If neither, combine → unique answer? Match the result to the 5 options.
