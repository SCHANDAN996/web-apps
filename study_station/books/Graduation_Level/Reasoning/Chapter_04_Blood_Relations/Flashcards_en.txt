Card 1
Front: In coded blood relations, what is the very first step before solving any expression like "A + B × C"?
Back: Decode the symbols first — write down what each symbol (+, ×, −, ÷) means (e.g., + = father, × = sister), then convert the whole expression into a plain relationship chain before answering.

Card 2
Front: Why should you always draw a family tree instead of solving coded relations mentally?
Back: Coded chains of 3–4 relations overload short-term memory. A quick tree (males in squares/+, females in circles/−, couples joined by =, generations stacked vertically) removes gender and generation confusion instantly.

Card 3
Front: What does the "generation gap" rule say when counting relations in a coded chain?
Back: Each parent/child symbol moves you one generation up or down; sibling and spouse symbols keep you in the same generation. The final answer depends on the net generation difference between the two persons asked about.

Card 4
Front: In "P is the son of Q's mother's brother", what is P to Q?
Back: Cousin. Q's mother's brother is Q's maternal uncle (mama); the uncle's son is Q's cousin. Examiner's trap: many students answer "nephew" by skipping one generation.

Card 5
Front: What is the mnemonic trick to remember gender symbols in a family tree?
Back: "Squares are Sons, Circles are Sisters" — males in squares (or marked +), females in circles (or marked −). One glance at the shape tells you the gender without re-reading the question.

Card 6
Front: What is the "back-to-front" reading trick for coded expressions like "A + B − C × D"?
Back: Read the chain from the last person backwards to the first when the question asks "How is A related to D?" — fix D's position, then climb the chain relation by relation. This avoids reversing relationships midway.

Card 7
Front: Examiner's trap: If gender of a person is not given anywhere in the coded chain, what should you do?
Back: Do NOT assume gender from the name (e.g., "Kiran", "Suman" are gender-neutral). If the answer needs gender and it isn't determinable, the correct option is "cannot be determined" — a favourite trap option.

Card 8
Front: In the expression "A × B" where × means "wife of", who is male and who is female?
Back: A is the wife (female), B is the husband (male). Trap: students often assign gender to the wrong person because the symbol sits between them — always attach the relationship to the person before the symbol.

Card 9
Front: What is the difference between "maternal uncle" and "paternal uncle" in exam questions?
Back: Maternal uncle = mother's brother (mama); paternal uncle = father's brother (chacha). Coded questions test whether you track which side of the family (mother's or father's) the chain passes through.

Card 10
Front: How do you handle a "pointing to a photograph" question like "He is the son of my grandfather's only son"?
Back: Replace "my" with yourself and simplify step by step: grandfather's only son = my father; father's son = me (or my brother). So the person is the speaker himself or his brother. Always reduce "only son/daughter" clues first.

Card 11
Front: What does the phrase "only daughter of my mother" always reduce to?
Back: Myself (if the speaker is female) or my sister. "Only" is the key word — it eliminates all other siblings and is the examiner's favourite way to hide a simple answer.

Card 12
Front: Mnemonic trick: how do you quickly remember which relations are "same generation"?
Back: "SSS stays Same" — Siblings, Spouse, and cousins (Same-generation Set) never change the generation level. Only parent/child links move you up or down the tree.

Card 13
Front: In a coded chain, if A is B's father and B is C's sister, how is A related to C?
Back: A is C's father. B being C's sister means B and C share parents, so B's father is also C's father. Since A is stated to be the father, A's gender is already fixed as male.

Card 14
Front: Examiner's trap: "A is the brother of B. B is the daughter of C." Can you conclude A is the son of C?
Back: Yes — B is C's daughter, and A is B's brother, so A is also C's child and male, hence C's son. The trap version adds a step like "C is the wife of D" and asks about D — then A is D's son too, since C's children are D's children.

Card 15
Front: What is the fastest way to verify your answer in a coded blood relation question?
Back: Reverse-check: take your answer (e.g., "A is D's uncle") and rebuild the chain from D back to A using the decoded symbols. If the reverse path matches the original expression, the answer is confirmed — takes under 10 seconds.

Card 16
Front: How are "sister-in-law" relations formed, and why do students get them wrong?
Back: Sister-in-law arises mainly two ways: your spouse's sister, or your brother's wife. Students err by assuming only one route exists — in coded chains, trace exactly which route the symbols create before answering, and never assume a relation that the chain does not explicitly build.

Card 17
Front: In "P ÷ Q + R − S" type questions, why must you decode left to right strictly?
Back: Each symbol links only its two immediate neighbours, and the chain builds cumulatively. Skipping or reordering breaks the generational logic — e.g., "P ÷ Q" fixes P and Q's relation first, then R attaches to Q, then S to R.

Card 18
Front: What should you do when a coded question gives two different meanings for the same symbol in two statements?
Back: Treat each statement as a separate code set — never mix them. Re-read the question: usually each sub-part defines its own symbols. Mixing codes across statements is a classic examiner trap in complex sets.

Card 19
Front: Why is "cousin" often the safest answer when the chain passes through an uncle or aunt?
Back: Any child of your parent's sibling (uncle or aunt) is your cousin, regardless of gender. Since gender of the cousin is often undeterminable, "cousin" is correct where "nephew" or "brother" would be wrong.

Card 20
Front: What is the single biggest time-saver in complex blood relation puzzles with 5+ members?
Back: Fix one anchor person (usually the speaker or the person most relations refer to), place them in the middle of your tree, and attach every other member to the tree as you decode — never hold more than one relation in your head at a time.
