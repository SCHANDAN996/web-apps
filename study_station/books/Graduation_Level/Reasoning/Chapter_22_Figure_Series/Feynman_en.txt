# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Figure Series & Classification Explained Like You're 12

## The Story: The Magic Photo Album

Imagine your grandmother has an old photo album. On page 1, there's a photo of a mango tree with **1 flower**. Page 2: the same tree with **2 flowers**. Page 3: **4 flowers**. Page 4: **8 flowers**.

You flip to page 5 — it's blank! Grandma smiles and asks, *"Beta, draw what comes next."*

You don't panic. You don't guess randomly. You ask one simple question:

> **"What changed between each photo?"**

1 → 2 → 4 → 8... each time the flowers **doubled**. So page 5 must have **16 flowers**. Done!

**That is ALL a Figure Series question is.** The exam paper is just grandma's photo album — a row of boxes where something keeps changing, and you must find the next photo. The figures look scary (arrows, dots, triangles, shaded corners), but underneath, they are only flowers doubling on a tree.

## The Hardest Part, Made Simple: "Too Many Things Change at Once!"

Here's what makes advanced-level series tough: in grandma's album, only the flowers changed. But exam figures are like a photo where **the tree grows, the sun moves, AND a bird flies to a new branch — all in the same picture!**

The 12-year-old's mistake: trying to watch everything at once. Your eyes jump around, nothing makes sense, and you pick the option that "looks close."

**The Feynman fix — the "One Detective, One Clue" method:**

Be a detective who interrogates **one suspect at a time**:

1. **Lock onto ONE element** (say, the black dot). Track ONLY it across all boxes. Where does it go? (Clockwise? Two steps? Corner to corner?)
2. Once its rule is clear, **freeze it** — now track the next element (the arrow).
3. Repeat for the third element (shading).

Each element follows its own simple rule. The "hard" figure is just **3 easy puzzles stacked on top of each other**. Solve them one by one, then combine.

## Classification (Odd-One-Out): The Birthday Party Trick

Classification is even easier to feel. Imagine 5 kids at a birthday party. Four are wearing **red caps**, one is wearing **blue**. Who's the odd one out? Instant answer, right?

Exam classification is the same party — except the "caps" are hidden properties:

- Number of sides (4 figures have an even number of lines, one has odd)
- Rotation (4 are the same figure rotated, one is a **mirror image** — the classic trap!)
- Position (in 4 figures the dot is *inside* the circle, in one it's *outside*)
- Element count (4 figures have 3 shapes, one has 4)

**The rule:** Don't ask "what is this figure?" Ask **"what property do four of them share?"**

## 🎯 The Unforgettable Trick: "DOT-SPIN-COUNT"

Whenever a figure question stares at you blankly, whisper these three words:

- **DOT** — Track dots, arrows, small symbols first. They usually move in fixed steps (1 corner clockwise, 2 corners, etc.). They're the easiest rule to crack and often eliminate 2 options instantly.
- **SPIN** — Check rotation. Is the whole figure spinning 45°/90°? Is it flipping (mirror)? Remember: **rotation keeps the figure identical; a mirror image reverses it** — examiners hide mirror images among rotations in classification questions.
- **COUNT** — Count everything: sides, lines, circles, shaded regions, intersections. If numbers go 2, 3, 4, 5... or 2, 4, 8... you've found the rule.

**DOT-SPIN-COUNT, in that order.** Dots first (fastest), spin second, counting last (slowest but catches everything).

## ⚠️ The Examiner's Trap (सावधान!)

The deadliest trap in figure series: **two rules running together, and one option follows only ONE of them.** Example: the dot moves clockwise AND the shading alternates. Option (b) has the dot right but shading wrong; option (c) has both right. Students in a hurry grab (b). **Always verify your answer against ALL elements before marking.**
