# Figure Series & Classification

## Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Identify the **pattern** in a series of figures (rotation, addition, deletion, movement, shading).
2. Spot the **odd figure** in a classification question within seconds.
3. Break any complex figure into its **basic elements** and track each element separately.
4. Recognise the examiner's favourite traps and avoid them under time pressure.

---

## 1. The Hook: Why This Chapter Decides Your Rank

A truth most aspirants discover too late: in SSC CGL, IBPS PO and RRB NTPC, non-verbal reasoning questions are among the **fastest marks in the entire paper**. No calculation. No grammar rules. No current affairs. Just your eyes and a pattern.

A well-trained student solves a figure series question in seconds; an untrained student stares at it for minutes and still guesses. Over a full paper, this single skill can significantly boost your score — often the difference between "missed the cutoff" and "selected."

The good news? This is a **pure skill**, not knowledge. Skills improve with the right method. Let's build yours.

---

## 2. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Almost every student says the same thing: *"I look at the four figures and my mind goes blank. Everything looks the same."*

That happens because you are looking at the figure as a **whole picture**. Your brain is trying to memorise images instead of reading a story. Toppers do something different — they **decompose**. They ask: *What are the parts? What is each part doing?*

Once you track parts instead of pictures, the "blank mind" problem disappears.

---

## 3. The Golden Method: D-E-C-O-D-E

Use this mnemonic for every figure question:

- **D** – **Divide** the figure into elements (dots, lines, arrows, shapes, shading).
- **E** – **Examine** one element at a time.
- **C** – **Count** (number of sides, lines, dots — is it +1, −1, doubling?).
- **O** – **Orientation** (is something rotating? By how much — 45°, 90°, 180°?).
- **D** – **Direction** (clockwise or anticlockwise?).
- **E** – **Eliminate** options that break even one rule.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. Write the six letters of DECODE and what each stands for. Check yourself.

---

## 4. The Five Core Patterns in Figure Series

Almost every series question is built from one or more of these five engines:

### 4.1 Rotation
An element rotates by a fixed angle each step — usually **45°, 90°, 135° or 180°**, clockwise or anticlockwise.
*Example:* An arrow pointing up → right → down → left is rotating 90° clockwise each step.

**Trap alert:** Sometimes one element rotates clockwise while another rotates anticlockwise in the same figure. Track them separately.

### 4.2 Addition / Deletion of Elements
Each step adds one line, one dot, or one side — or removes one.
*Example:* A triangle (3 sides) → quadrilateral (4) → pentagon (5) → next must be a hexagon (6).

### 4.3 Movement / Shifting
An element jumps positions — corner to corner, or one step along the boundary.
*Example:* A dot moves corner → corner → corner in clockwise order around a square.

### 4.4 Shading / Inversion
Black becomes white, shaded regions shift, or the figure flips (mirror image).
*Example:* The shaded half of a circle rotates 90° each step.

### 4.5 Combination (the Advanced level)
Two or three patterns run **simultaneously**. This is what Graduation-level exams love.
*Example:* The outer shape gains one side each step **while** an inner dot moves one corner anticlockwise **while** shading alternates.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Name the five core patterns without looking. Which one is most common at the advanced level? (Answer: Combination.)

---

## 5. The 90% Rule: Check the Last Step First

Here is a professional shortcut. Most students try to guess the rule from figures 1→2→3 and then predict figure 5. Instead, **look at the last given figure and the answer options together**. Ask: *What single change would turn the last figure into each option?* The option whose change matches the established rhythm wins.

This works because examiners design wrong options to match *some* steps but break the pattern at the end.

---

## 6. Figure Classification (Odd One Out)

In classification, four or five figures are given and you must find the one that **does not belong**. The logic is always a **property**, not a feeling. Common properties tested:

1. **Number of elements** — four figures have 3 lines inside; one has 4.
2. **Rotation vs Reflection** — four figures are rotations of each other; one is a mirror image. *(This is a commonly tested classification trap.)*
3. **Position relationship** — in four figures the dot is inside the smaller shape; in one it is outside.
4. **Symmetry** — four figures have a line of symmetry; one does not.
5. **Enclosure** — in four figures one shape is completely inside another; in one they merely touch or overlap.
6. **Same vs different elements** — four figures contain two identical shapes; one contains two different shapes.

**Method:** Do NOT ask "which looks different?" Ask "what rule do four of them follow?" Then find the rebel.

🧠 **Active Recall:** A question shows five figures. Four are rotations of the same shape; one is its mirror image. Which is the odd one? Why is this trap so effective? (Because rotated and mirrored figures *feel* identical at a glance.)

---

## 7. Rotation vs Mirror Image — The Deciding Skill

This deserves its own section because it appears in **both** series and classification.

**The Clock Test:** Pick any asymmetric feature of the figure (like a flag on an arrow). Mentally trace from the main body to that feature. If the trace goes clockwise in all figures, they are rotations of each other. If one figure's trace goes anticlockwise, it is a **mirror image** — the odd one out.

Practice this on paper with the letters **F, L, P, R** — rotate them, then flip them, until your eye catches the difference instantly.

## 8. A Worked Example (Combination Pattern)

**Series:** A square contains a dot and a small arrow.
- Figure 1: dot at top-left corner, arrow pointing up.
- Figure 2: dot at top-right, arrow pointing right.
- Figure 3: dot at bottom-right, arrow pointing down.
- Figure 4: dot at bottom-left, arrow pointing left.
- **Figure 5 = ?**

**Decode:**
- **Dot:** moves one corner **clockwise** each step → next: top-left.
- **Arrow:** rotates **90° clockwise** each step → next: pointing up.
- **Answer:** the option with dot at top-left and arrow pointing up — which is identical to Figure 1 (the cycle restarts).

Notice: two independent patterns, one figure. That is the advanced game.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In the example above, after how many steps does the full pattern repeat? (Answer: 4 — both cycles have period 4.)

## 9. Summary Table

| Pattern | What to Track | Typical Step |
|---|---|---|
| Rotation | Angle & direction of an element | 45° / 90° / 180°, CW or ACW |
| Addition/Deletion | Count of lines, dots, sides | +1 or −1 per step |
| Movement | Position of dot/element | Corner-to-corner, along edges |
| Shading/Inversion | Black-white regions, flips | Alternate or rotating shade |
| Combination | 2–3 elements simultaneously | Independent rules per element |
| Classification | The property 4 figures share | Rotation vs mirror is a top trap |

**Time rule:** Aim to solve within 20–30 seconds per question. If no pattern emerges in 30 seconds, mark and move on — return later with fresh eyes.

---

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Blurry figures on screen.** In CBT exams, small dots, thin lines and light shading render poorly. Train yourself to look for **structure first, details second**. If a figure looks ambiguous, compare it with its neighbours — the pattern usually clarifies what the unclear element must be.

**Trap 2 — Hidden differences in look-alike figures.** Examiners place two nearly identical options side by side, differing only in one tiny element (a dot's position, an arrowhead's direction). Never select an option after matching just one element — verify **all** elements against your decoded rule before clicking.

**Trap 3 — Slight change in rotation/reflection.** A figure that rotates 90° for three steps may rotate 135° in the fourth, or a series may secretly switch from rotation to reflection midway. Always verify the rule holds for **every consecutive pair**, not just the first two. In classification, remember: four rotations + one mirror image is the examiner's favourite arrangement.
