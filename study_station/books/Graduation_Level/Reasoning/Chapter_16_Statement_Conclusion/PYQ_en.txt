# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Statement & Conclusion (Advanced Level)

## 1. How Exams Ask This Chapter

Statement & Conclusion is a permanent fixture of the reasoning sections of SSC CGL, IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RRB NTPC and State PCS prelims. Across these exams, the questions follow a small set of recurring patterns:

- **Two-conclusion format dominates.** "Statement followed by Conclusions I and II — which follows?" is the default frame in SSC and Banking exams. Single-conclusion and three-conclusion variants appear occasionally.
- **The statement is usually one or two sentences** — a policy announcement, a survey finding, an advertisement line, or an advice/warning.
- **The most repeated sub-types**, in rough order of how often they appear:
  1. Cause–effect conclusions (the statement implies a reason; the conclusion overstates it)
  2. Assumption-flavoured conclusions ("the speaker believes…")
  3. Extreme-word traps ("all", "never", "only", "must")
  4. Course-of-action disguised as conclusion ("the government should…")
  5. Direct restatement (a conclusion that merely repeats the statement in new words — this DOES follow)
- **Negative framing is a favourite device**: "Which conclusion does NOT follow?" — candidates who read in a hurry mark the one that follows.

## 2. Exam-wise Tendencies (in words, not numbers)

- **SSC CGL / CHSL**: Short, everyday statements (advertisements, notices, proverbs). Traps rely on extreme words and on conclusions that sound morally right but are not logically supported.
- **IBPS / SBI PO**: Longer statements, often economic or social in tone. Conclusions test fine distinctions — partial vs total, possible vs certain. "Either I or II follows" pairs (complementary conclusions) appear here more than in SSC.
- **RRB NTPC / Group D**: Simple, direct statements; the trap is usually a conclusion that adds outside general knowledge.
- **State PCS prelims**: Statement–conclusion is often merged with statement–assumption; expect statements about governance and schemes, with conclusions that quietly convert "some" into "all".

## 3. The Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

1. **The "sounds true in real life" hook** — the conclusion matches your general knowledge, so you accept it. Logic allows only what the statement supports.
2. **The extreme-word hook** — "All politicians are corrupt" type conclusions. One sweeping word makes it invalid.
3. **The restatement-in-disguise** — a conclusion that simply paraphrases the statement. Many candidates reject it thinking "it's too obvious"; actually it follows.
4. **The cause-reversal hook** — statement says "X led to Y"; conclusion says "Y led to X".
5. **The emotional hook** — a conclusion that is morally appealing ("honesty is the best policy") but logically unconnected.
6. **The "some → all" slide** — statement speaks of a few cases; conclusion generalises to everyone.
7. **The either–or pair** — two complementary conclusions (one positive, one negative on the same subject). When neither is definite, "either follows" is correct — candidates who don't know this rule waste time.

## 4. Ten High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

**Q1.** Statement: "Buy our new soap — it contains neem, which kills germs."
Conclusions: I. Neem has germ-killing properties. II. Other soaps do not contain neem.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The advertisement itself asserts that neem kills germs, so I follows directly. II is the trap — the ad never compares with other soaps; the candidate's assumption that "our product is unique" is outside knowledge.
**Trap used:** uniqueness assumption.

**Q2.** Statement: The company has decided to increase the salary of its employees by 15% from next month.
Conclusions: I. The company was earlier paying low salaries. II. The company expects employees to work harder after the hike.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (d)**
**Solution:** A hike does not prove the earlier salary was "low" — it may already have been high. Nor does the statement reveal the company's motive. Both conclusions smuggle in assumptions.
**Trap used:** motive-reading and comparative-word ("low") hooks.

**Q3.** Statement: "The best way to escape a problem is to solve it." — a speaker.
Conclusions: I. Solving a problem is better than avoiding it. II. Every problem can be solved.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The speaker clearly prefers solving over escaping, so I follows. II is an extreme generalisation — the quote never claims solvability of *every* problem.
**Trap used:** extreme word "every".

**Q4.** Statement: Cases of food poisoning were reported from a hotel in the city; the municipal authority sealed the hotel.
Conclusions: I. The hotel will never reopen. II. The municipal authority took action to protect public health.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Sealing is a health-protection action, so II is a fair inference. "Never reopen" is a permanent prediction from a temporary action — classic extreme-word trap.
**Trap used:** "never".

**Q5.** Statement: The government has made wearing helmets compulsory for two-wheeler riders.
Conclusions: I. Accidents will stop completely. II. The government wants to reduce head injuries among riders.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Compulsion signals intent to reduce injuries — II follows. "Stop completely" overstates; helmets reduce severity, they don't eliminate accidents.
**Trap used:** absolute outcome ("completely").

**Q6.** Statement: In a survey, 60% of the students of City X who took coaching cleared the exam.
Conclusions: I. Coaching guarantees success. II. Some students of City X who took coaching did not clear the exam.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** If 60% cleared, 40% did not — II is a direct arithmetic restatement and follows. "Guarantees" contradicts the very figure given.
**Trap used:** candidates often reject II as "too obvious"; restatements DO follow.

**Q7.** Statement: The principal instructed the students to reach school by 7:45 a.m. during the examination week.
Conclusions: I. Students usually reach school late. II. The instruction applies only during the examination week.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The statement itself limits the timing to the exam week, so II follows. I invents a past habit — a special instruction does not prove habitual lateness.
**Trap used:** reading a hidden complaint into a neutral instruction.

**Q8.** Statement: "This book is written in such a simple style that even a beginner can understand it," said the publisher.
Conclusions: I. The publisher believes the book is easy to understand. II. All beginners will read this book.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is the speaker's stated belief — it follows. II converts "can understand" into "will read", an unjustified leap from ability to action.
**Trap used:** ability → action slide, plus "all".

**Q9.** Statement: The bank has reduced its home-loan interest rate by 0.5%.
Conclusions: I. More people may now take home loans from this bank. II. Other banks will also reduce their rates.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** A rate cut making loans more attractive is a reasonable, hedged ("may") inference — I follows. II predicts competitors' behaviour, which the statement says nothing about.
**Trap used:** predicting third-party reactions. Note the hedged word "may" is what saves I.

**Q10.** Statement: The weather department has forecast heavy rain in the coastal districts.
Conclusions: I. There will definitely be floods. II. The administration should be prepared for heavy rain.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Preparedness is a natural, measured response to a forecast — II follows. "Definitely floods" converts a forecast into certainty and adds an outcome (floods) the statement never mentions.
**Trap used:** forecast → certainty.

## 5. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Applied

- **15-second questions (attempt immediately):** Q1, Q5, Q6, Q8 — short statements, one obvious extreme-word or restatement trap. Read, spot the killer word ("never", "all", "guarantees", "completely"), mark, move on.
- **45-second questions (attempt, but with the clock in mind):** Q3, Q7, Q9 — require checking both conclusions carefully against the statement's exact scope.
- **Skip candidates:** Q2 and Q10-style items where *both* conclusions look plausible at first glance and the distinction depends on subtle motive-reading — if after ~45 seconds you cannot firmly eliminate one conclusion, mark for review and move on. In negative-marking exams, a guessed statement–conclusion question is a bad trade. Also skip any question whose statement is 3+ lines long when it appears late in the section; the reading cost alone breaks the 45-second budget.

## 6. Key Takeaways from the Pattern

- Master the extreme-word list: *all, every, never, only, must, always, completely, definitely, guarantees* — their presence in a conclusion makes it wrong in the overwhelming majority of cases.
- Hedged conclusions (*may, might, can, some, likely*) are usually correct.
- A conclusion that restates the statement in different words **follows** — do not reject it for being obvious.
- Never import real-world knowledge; the statement is the entire universe of the question.
- For "either I or II" options, check whether the two conclusions are complementary (same subject, one positive one negative, both indefinite) before spending time on deep analysis.
