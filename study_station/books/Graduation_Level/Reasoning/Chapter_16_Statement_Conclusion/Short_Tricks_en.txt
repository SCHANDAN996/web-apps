# 🪄 Short Tricks & Mnemonics — Statement & Conclusion

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 1: **"MUST vs MAY"**
> **"If it MUST be true, it stays. If it MAY be true, it walks away."**

A conclusion is valid only if it is **logically unavoidable** from the statement. If you can imagine even one situation where the statement is true but the conclusion is false — reject it.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 2: **"The 3 NOs of Conclusions"**
> **"No NEW facts, No EXTREME words, No OUTSIDE knowledge."**

- **N**EW — conclusion introduces something not in the statement → reject
- **E**XTREME — words like *all, always, never, only, completely* → usually reject
- **O**UTSIDE — uses your real-world knowledge instead of the statement → reject

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 3: **"SUGAR Trap"**
> **"S-U-G-A-R: Suggestions Usually Get Axed Rapidly."**

Conclusions that are *suggestions, advice, or courses of action* ("Government should…", "People must…") are **not** conclusions — they belong to Statement & Course of Action. Axe them.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 4: **"The Judge Rule"**
> **"Be a judge, not a journalist."**

A judge decides **only on the evidence presented** (the statement). A journalist adds background, opinions, and context. In the exam hall, your general knowledge is your enemy — the statement is the whole universe.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 5: **"ALWAYS is Always Wrong"**
> **"Words that shout, get thrown out."**

*All, every, always, never, none, only, definitely, certainly* — extreme words in a conclusion are wrong in ~most cases unless the statement itself uses equally absolute language.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 6: **"The Echo Test"**
> **"A true conclusion is an echo, not a new voice."**

Valid conclusions **echo** the statement (restate its direct implication). Invalid ones are a **new voice** (add prediction, opinion, or assumption). Ask: "Is this the statement speaking, or someone else?"

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 7: **"PROBABLY Passes"**
> **"Soft words survive: may, might, possibly, some, likely."**

The flip side of Mnemonic 5 — conclusions with **hedged/soft language** are more likely to be valid because they don't overclaim. But they still must connect logically to the statement!

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 8: **"The Two-Step Dance"**
> **"Step 1: Is it STATED or IMPLIED? Step 2: Is it CERTAIN?"**

For every conclusion, dance two steps:
1. Does it connect to the statement at all? (No → reject)
2. Is it **definitely** true, not just probably? (No → reject)

Only conclusions passing both steps survive.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 9: **"FROG — Future Results Often Guess"**
> **"Don't kiss the FROG: predictions about the future are guesses, not conclusions."**

"Sales will increase next year", "The policy will succeed" — future predictions are speculative. Unless the statement guarantees the outcome, spit out the FROG.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 10: **"The Mirror Trap"**
> **"A conclusion that merely repeats the statement word-for-word is a mirror, not a conclusion."**

Restating the statement exactly is NOT a conclusion — a conclusion must be a **derived** implication. But beware: a *correctly rephrased* direct implication IS valid. Mirror = reject; paraphrase with logic = accept.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 11: **"BOTH-OR Rule"**
> **"When two conclusions are complementary opposites (one positive, one negative), and neither is certain — the answer is 'Either I or II follows'."**

Classic pattern: "I. X will win. II. X will not win." — if the statement gives no certainty either way, it's an **either-or** case. Look for this pattern; examiners love it.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 12: **"The 20-Second Filter"**
> **"Read statement (8 sec) → Test Conclusion I (6 sec) → Test Conclusion II (6 sec)."**

Never re-read the statement for each conclusion. Read once, hold it in mind, and filter each conclusion through the **3 NOs** (Mnemonic 2).

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 13: **"WHO is Speaking?"**
> **"A conclusion about the speaker's intention/motive is a trap."**

"The minister said X, so the minister wants votes" — conclusions about **hidden motives, feelings, or intentions** of people in the statement are assumptions, not conclusions. Reject them.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 14: **"The Negative Flip"**
> **"If the statement is positive, a negative conclusion needs extra proof — and there is none."**

Statement: "Yoga improves health." Conclusion: "Those who don't do yoga are unhealthy." — This is the **negative flip trap**. A positive statement says nothing certain about its opposite. Reject.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 15: **"C-A-L-M before answering"**
> **"C**heck extremes → **A**void outside knowledge → **L**ook for certainty → **M**ark only what MUST follow."

The 4-letter final checklist before marking any answer.

---

# ⏭️ Skip Strategy — When to Skip a Statement & Conclusion Question

### Tip 1: **The 30-Second Wall**
If after 30 seconds you still cannot decide whether a conclusion "definitely follows", the question is deliberately ambiguous. **Mark your best instinct and move on** — Statement & Conclusion questions are designed to eat time through overthinking. Your first logical read is usually right.

### Tip 2: **Skip Multi-Conclusion Monsters (temporarily)**
If a question has **3 or more conclusions** with long, compound statements, skip it in the first pass and return in the second round. Each extra conclusion multiplies evaluation time. Solve the 2-conclusion questions first — they give marks faster.

### Tip 3: **Skip When Both Conclusions "Feel Right"**
If both conclusions feel equally valid after applying the MUST-vs-MAY test, you are likely importing outside knowledge. Don't burn 2 minutes forcing a decision — flag it, move on, and revisit with fresh eyes. In negative-marking exams, a confused guess costs more than a skip.
