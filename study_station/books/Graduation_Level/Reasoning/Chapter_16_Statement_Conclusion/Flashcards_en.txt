Card 1
Front: What is the golden rule for judging a conclusion in Statement & Conclusion questions?
Back: A conclusion is valid only if it follows necessarily and directly from the statement — no outside knowledge, no assumptions, no "probably true" ideas.

Card 2
Front: If a conclusion is "possibly true" but not "definitely true," should you accept it?
Back: No. In these questions, only conclusions that must be true (100% certain from the statement) are accepted. Probability is not enough.

Card 3
Front: What mnemonic helps you remember the test for a valid conclusion?
Back: "MUST, not MAY" — a conclusion must follow; if it merely may follow, reject it.

Card 4
Front: What is the "NEG test" (negation test) for checking a conclusion?
Back: Negate the conclusion. If the negated version contradicts the statement, the original conclusion is valid. If the statement stays silent about it, reject the conclusion.

Card 5
Front: A statement says "Some politicians are corrupt." Can the conclusion "All politicians are corrupt" follow?
Back: No. "Some" can never justify "all." Moving from a part to the whole is a classic invalid leap.

Card 6
Front: What is the examiner's favourite trap involving extreme words?
Back: Conclusions with words like "all," "always," "never," "only," "completely" are usually traps — the statement rarely supports such absolute claims. Treat extreme-word conclusions with suspicion.

Card 7
Front: A statement criticises one government scheme. A conclusion says "The government has failed on all fronts." Valid?
Back: No. Generalising from one specific case to everything is the over-generalisation trap. The conclusion goes far beyond the statement's scope.

Card 8
Front: Should you use your real-world knowledge (e.g., what you read in newspapers) while solving these questions?
Back: Never. The statement is your entire universe. Even if a conclusion is true in real life, reject it unless the statement itself supports it.

Card 9
Front: What mnemonic reminds you to stay inside the statement?
Back: "SOS — Stay On Statement." If it's not in the statement, it's not in the answer.

Card 10
Front: A statement says "Company X's profits rose after it hired a new CEO." Conclusion: "The new CEO caused the profit rise." Valid?
Back: No. Sequence is not causation. The statement only says profits rose after the hiring — it does not establish cause. This is the post-hoc trap.

Card 11
Front: In "either conclusion I or II follows" cases, what two conditions must hold?
Back: (1) The two conclusions must be contradictory/complementary (one positive, one negative on the same subject), and (2) both must individually seem plausible but neither is definitively provable — then "either-or" applies.

Card 12
Front: A statement says "Students who sleep well score better." Conclusion: "Students who don't sleep well always fail." Valid?
Back: No. Two traps here: (1) "score better" ≠ pass/fail, and (2) "always" is an extreme word the statement never supports.

Card 13
Front: What is the difference between a conclusion and an assumption?
Back: A conclusion comes after the statement (what follows from it); an assumption comes before (what the speaker already took for granted while making the statement). Don't mix the two question types.

Card 14
Front: A conclusion merely repeats the statement in different words. Should it be accepted?
Back: Yes — a restatement of the statement is necessarily true, so it follows. But beware: if the "restatement" quietly adds even one new element, it becomes invalid.

Card 15
Front: The statement is about "urban schools." A conclusion talks about "all schools in the country." What trap is this?
Back: Scope-shift trap — the conclusion widens the subject from a part (urban) to the whole (country). Always check that the conclusion's subject matches the statement's subject.

Card 16
Front: What should you do when both conclusions seem individually valid?
Back: Check each independently with the MUST test. If both pass, mark "both follow." Don't assume the question wants only one — but also don't mark "both" just because they sound similar.

Card 17
Front: What is the "emotional appeal" trap in Statement & Conclusion?
Back: Conclusions that sound morally right or desirable ("Honesty is the best policy," "Education should be free for all") tempt you to accept them. Unless the statement logically supports them, reject them — feelings are not logic.

Card 18
Front: How much time should you ideally spend on one Statement & Conclusion question in the exam?
Back: About 30–40 seconds. Read the statement once carefully, apply the MUST test to each conclusion, and move on. If a question feels ambiguous beyond 45 seconds, mark your best logical choice and skip ahead.

Card 19
Front: A statement says "Regular exercise reduces the risk of heart disease." Conclusion: "A person who exercises regularly will never get heart disease." Valid?
Back: No. "Reduces risk" does not mean "eliminates risk." The conclusion converts a probability reduction into an absolute guarantee — a distortion trap.

Card 20
Front: What is the single most common reason students lose marks in this chapter?
Back: Accepting conclusions that are true in real life or sound reasonable, instead of checking whether they follow necessarily from the given statement alone.
