# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Syllogism (Advanced)

## 1. How Exams Test This Chapter

Syllogism is one of the most reliable scoring areas in reasoning. Across SSC CGL, IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RRB NTPC and State PCS prelims, questions from this chapter appear in almost every reasoning section. The pattern has clearly shifted over the years:

- **Earlier pattern:** Simple two-statement, two-conclusion questions with direct Venn-diagram solutions.
- **Current dominant pattern:** Three or four statements, "only a few / only" type statements, possibility conclusions ("being a possibility"), and either–or (complementary) conclusion pairs.
- **Banking exams (IBPS/SBI):** Heavily favour "Only a few A are B" and possibility-based conclusions, often with 3 statements and 2–3 conclusions where you must decide "follows / does not follow / either–or".
- **SSC / Railway:** Mostly 2–3 statements with standard conclusions; negative conclusions and "some not" traps are common.

## 2. Sub-topics That Recur Most

1. **"Only a few" statements** — the single most repeated modern trap. "Only a few A are B" means *some A are B AND some A are not B*. Both parts must be tested.
2. **Possibility conclusions** — "All A being B is a possibility" follows whenever there is no definite contradiction between A and B.
3. **Either–or (complementary) pairs** — e.g., "Some A are B" vs "No A is B": when neither follows individually but together they exhaust all cases, the answer is "either I or II follows". Conditions: same subject–predicate, one positive + one negative, both indefinite.
4. **Negative statements with positive conclusions** — a positive conclusion can never follow from a set containing only negative statements.
5. **"Some not" conclusions** — students wrongly derive "Some A are not B" from "Some A are B". This is the classic near-correct trap.
6. **Distributed middle term checks** — conclusions drawn when the middle term is never distributed (invalid).

## 3. Examiner's Psychological Traps (in words)

- **Near-correct restatement:** Conclusion looks like a harmless rewording of a statement but quietly converts "some" into "all" or drops the "not".
- **Chronology/order confusion:** Reversing subject and predicate — "Some A are B" does NOT guarantee "Some B are A" is false (it actually converts), but "All A are B" does NOT convert to "All B are A".
- **Similar-name confusion:** Three terms with overlapping meanings (e.g., "doctors, surgeons, males") designed so you assume a relation that is never stated.
- **Negative wording:** "Which conclusion does NOT follow" or "definitely false" — students answer the opposite of what is asked.
- **Possibility bait:** A conclusion that is *actually true* in the real world but not derivable — exams test logic, not general knowledge.

## 4. High-Yield Question Types with Solutions

**Q1.** Statements: Only a few pens are pencils. All pencils are erasers.
Conclusions: I. Some pens are not pencils. II. Some erasers are pens.
**Answer:** Both follow.
**Solution:** "Only a few pens are pencils" = some pens are pencils AND some pens are not pencils → I follows directly. Since some pens are pencils and all pencils are erasers, those pens are erasers → II follows.
**Trap:** Students treat "only a few" as plain "some" and miss conclusion I.

**Q2.** Statements: All cats are dogs. No dog is a rat.
Conclusions: I. No cat is a rat. II. Some cats are not rats.
**Answer:** Both follow.
**Solution:** All cats are inside dogs, and dogs and rats are disjoint, so no cat can be a rat (I). II is just the particular form of I.
**Trap:** Thinking II needs separate proof — a universal negative implies its "some not" form.

**Q3.** Statements: Some books are pens. Some pens are pencils.
Conclusions: I. Some books are pencils. II. No book is a pencil.
**Answer:** Either I or II follows.
**Solution:** The two "some" statements share "pens" as middle term but give no definite link between books and pencils. I and II are a complementary pair (same terms, one positive, one negative, both indefinite) → either–or.
**Trap:** Marking "neither follows" and forgetting the complementary-pair rule.

**Q4.** Statements: No apple is a banana. No banana is a cherry.
Conclusions: I. Some apples are cherries. II. Some cherries are not apples.
**Answer:** Neither follows.
**Solution:** Two negative statements yield no definite positive relation, and no definite "some not" between apple and cherry either.
**Trap:** Assuming disjoint chains must connect the end terms.

**Q5.** Statements: All A are B. Some B are C.
Conclusions: I. All A being C is a possibility. II. Some A are C.
**Answer:** Only I follows.
**Solution:** Nothing forbids A overlapping C, so the possibility holds. But no definite A–C link is given, so II fails.
**Trap:** Marking II because the diagram *can* be drawn that way — "can be drawn" is not "must be true".

**Q6.** Statements: Only a few students are toppers. All toppers are hardworking.
Conclusions: I. Some students are not toppers. II. Some hardworking are students.
**Answer:** Both follow.
**Solution:** "Only a few" guarantees I. Some students are toppers, and all toppers are hardworking → those students are hardworking → II.
**Trap:** Rejecting II by reversing the terms carelessly — conversion of "some" is valid.

**Q7.** Statements: All pens are red. All red are blue.
Conclusions: I. All pens are blue. II. Some blue are pens.
**Answer:** Both follow.
**Solution:** Chain of universals: pens ⊆ red ⊆ blue → I. Conversion of "All pens are blue" gives "Some blue are pens" → II.
**Trap:** Thinking universal statements cannot be converted at all — they convert to "some", not to "all".

**Q8.** Statements: Some chairs are tables. No table is a fan.
Conclusions: I. Some chairs are not fans. II. No chair is a fan.
**Answer:** Only I follows.
**Solution:** The chairs that are tables cannot be fans → I definitely holds. But the remaining chairs may or may not be fans, so the universal II is not certain.
**Trap:** Upgrading a "some not" conclusion to "no" — the most common overreach.

**Q9.** Statements: All gold is silver. Some silver is costly.
Conclusions: I. Some gold is costly. II. All gold being costly is a possibility.
**Answer:** Only II follows.
**Solution:** No definite gold–costly link, so I fails; no contradiction either, so the possibility II holds.
**Trap:** Real-world knowledge ("gold is costly") pushing you to mark I — logic only.

**Q10.** Statements: Only a few A are B. Only a few B are C.
Conclusions: I. Some A are not B. II. Some B are not C.
**Answer:** Both follow.
**Solution:** Each "only a few" statement carries its own "some not" half. No cross-term conclusion is needed.
**Trap:** Hunting for an A–C conclusion and missing that both given conclusions come straight from the statements.

## 5. Skip Decisions under the 15-sec / 45-sec Rule

- **15-second rule (attempt instantly):** Q7-type pure universal chains and Q2-type "all + no" combinations — one quick Venn diagram settles them. Never skip these.
- **45-second rule (attempt with one careful diagram):** "Only a few" questions (Q1, Q6, Q10) — fast once you remember the dual meaning.
- **SKIP candidates:**
  - **Q3-type either–or with 3+ statements and multiple complementary pairs:** if you cannot confirm the either–or conditions (same terms, one positive, one negative, both indefinite) within ~45 seconds, skip and return — a wrong either–or call costs negative marking.
  - **Q5/Q9-type possibility questions with 4 statements:** when several term-pairs must each be checked for contradiction, the checking time balloons; if two pairs remain ambiguous after one diagram, skip rather than guess.

## 6. Time-Rule Review

The 15-sec/45-sec rule fits this chapter well: roughly the first half of a syllogism set (direct chains, single negative statements) is solvable in about 15 seconds each with a minimal diagram, while "only a few", possibility, and either–or items deserve up to 45 seconds. Anything still unresolved after one clean Venn diagram should be marked for review — in syllogism, a second diagram drawn in haste causes more errors than it fixes.
