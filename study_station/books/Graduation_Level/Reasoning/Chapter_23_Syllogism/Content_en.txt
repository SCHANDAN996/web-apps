# Syllogism (Advanced)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Solve syllogism questions using **Venn diagrams** — the only method that never lies.
2. Handle **"possibility" conclusions** (Some A being B is a possibility) with confidence.
3. Crack **"either-or" (complementary) pairs** and know exactly when they apply.
4. Deal with **negative statements** (No, Some not) without panic.
5. Spot the examiner's favourite traps and skip them in under 30 seconds.

---

## The Hook

Imagine this: the examiner gives you two innocent-looking sentences —

> *All pens are books. Some books are chairs.*

…and asks: *"Do some chairs have to be pens?"*

Your gut screams "Yes!" Your gut is wrong. And that is exactly why syllogism is the **highest-scoring trap chapter** in SSC CGL, IBPS PO, and every banking prelims. The questions are short, the marks are free — but only for students who stop trusting their gut and start trusting **diagrams**.

This chapter will rewire how you think. Give it one honest hour.

## The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Almost every aspirant makes the same three mistakes:

- **Using real-world logic.** In syllogism, "All dogs are cats" is a perfectly valid statement. The question is never "Is this true in life?" — it is "Does this *follow* from the statements?"
- **Drawing only one diagram.** A statement like "Some A are B" can be drawn in several ways. If your conclusion is true in only *one* of those drawings, it does **not** follow.
- **Fearing "possibility" questions.** These are actually the easiest marks once you learn one rule (coming soon).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Before reading further, answer from memory — in syllogism, do we check whether a conclusion is *true in the real world*, or whether it *must be true given the statements*?

## Chunk 1: The Golden Rule

> **A conclusion follows only if it is true in EVERY possible diagram of the statements.**

Not most diagrams. Not the first diagram you drew. **Every** diagram.

Statements: *All A are B. All B are C.*
Conclusion: *All A are C.* → True in every diagram → **Follows.** ✅

Statements: *Some A are B. Some B are C.*
Conclusion: *Some A are C.* → You can draw a diagram where A and C never touch → **Does not follow.** ❌

This one rule decides 80% of all syllogism questions.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Complete the rule — "A conclusion follows only if it is true in ______ possible diagrams."

## Chunk 2: The Four Statement Types (Venn Forms)

| Statement | Venn Diagram | What it guarantees |
|---|---|---|
| All A are B | Circle A fully inside circle B | Every A is B; some B may not be A |
| No A is B | Two separate circles | A and B share nothing |
| Some A are B | Overlapping circles | At least one A is B |
| Some A are not B | Part of A outside B | At least one A lies outside B |

**Memory trick — "A-N-S-S" (अनेस):** **A**ll (inside), **N**o (separate), **S**ome (overlap), **S**ome-not (part outside). Say it once, draw it twice, and it is yours forever.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking — how do you draw "No A is B"? And what does "Some A are B" guarantee at minimum?

## Chunk 3: Possibility Conclusions — The One Rule

Conclusions like *"Some A being B is a possibility"* or *"All A being B is a possibility"* follow when **nothing in the statements forbids it**.

> **Rule:** A possibility follows if there is NO definite negative relation (No / Some-not) blocking it between those two terms.

Statements: *All pens are books. Some books are chairs.*
Conclusion: *All chairs being pens is a possibility.* → No "No" statement blocks chairs and pens → **Follows.** ✅

But: Statements: *No pen is chair.* → Now *"Some pens being chairs is a possibility"* → **Does not follow.** ❌ (A definite "No" kills every positive possibility between those terms.)

🧠 **Active Recall:** What single thing kills a positive possibility conclusion?

## Chunk 4: Either-Or (Complementary) Pairs

Sometimes neither conclusion I nor II follows alone, but **one of them must be true**. Then the answer is "either I or II follows."

All three conditions must hold:

1. Both conclusions are **individually false** (do not follow).
2. Both have the **same subject and predicate**.
3. The pair is one of these complementary forms:
   - **Some + No** (Some A are B / No A is B)
   - **All + Some-not** (All A are B / Some A are not B)

Statements: *Some cars are bikes. No bike is a cycle.*
Conclusions: I. Some cars are cycles. II. No car is a cycle.
→ Neither follows alone; same terms; Some + No pair → **Either I or II follows.** ✅

⚠️ If even one conclusion follows on its own, "either-or" is **not** the answer.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Name the two complementary pairs and the three conditions for either-or.

---

## Chunk 5: Negative Statements & Chaining

- **"No" is symmetric:** No A is B ⟺ No B is A. Use this freely.
- **"All" is NOT reversible:** All A are B does *not* mean All B are A. (Only "Some B are A" is safe.)
- **Chaining rule:** To connect A to C through B, the middle term B must be **distributed** (covered by "All" or "No") at least once. *Some A are B + Some B are C* → no definite A–C link. Ever.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Can you reverse "All A are B"? What can you safely conclude from it about B and A?

## Summary Table

| Situation | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Conclusion true in every diagram | Follows |
| True in some diagrams, false in one | Does not follow |
| Positive possibility, no "No/Some-not" blocking | Follows |
| Possibility blocked by a definite negative | Does not follow |
| Both false + same terms + Some/No or All/Some-not | Either-or |
| All A are B → All B are A | Never valid |
| Some + Some chain | No definite conclusion |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Multiple possible relations shown in options.**
The examiner designs statements where a conclusion *looks* true in the obvious diagram but fails in an alternative one (e.g., "Some A are B" drawn with full overlap). Students who draw only one diagram fall for it. **Defence:** always ask — "Can I draw this differently so the conclusion breaks?"

**Trap 2 — Confusing/complex wording to rattle you.**
Statements like "All flowers are not fruits" (meaning *Some flowers are not fruits*) or conclusions phrased as "At least some…" are used to create panic, not difficulty. **Defence:** translate every sentence into one of the four standard forms (All / No / Some / Some-not) *before* drawing anything.

**Trap 3 — Double-relation (either-or disguised) questions.**
The examiner gives two conclusions that are individually false but form a complementary pair — and places "neither follows" as a tempting option. Students who forget the either-or conditions mark "neither" and lose a free mark. **Defence:** whenever both conclusions fail, immediately check: same terms? complementary pair? If yes → either-or.
