# Statement & Assumption

## 1. Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Understand what a **Statement** and an **Assumption** really mean.
- Identify whether an assumption is **implicit** (hidden) in a statement or not.
- Spot the difference between an assumption and a conclusion/inference.
- Recognise the examiner's favourite traps and avoid them.
- Solve Statement–Assumption questions in under 40 seconds with confidence.

---

## 2. The Hook: The Invisible Foundation

Imagine your friend says: *"Join my coaching institute and you will definitely clear SSC CGL."*

Sounds simple, right? But wait — your friend never *said* that coaching helps in clearing exams. He never *said* that you are capable of studying. He never *said* that SSC CGL can be cleared through preparation.

Yet, all of these ideas are **silently hiding** inside his sentence. Without them, his sentence makes no sense at all.

That hidden, unsaid foundation is called an **Assumption** — and finding it is exactly what this chapter teaches you. Every competitive exam (SSC CGL, IBPS PO, RRB NTPC, State PCS) loves this topic because it tests not your memory, but your **thinking**.

---

## 3. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Almost every student faces the same three problems here:

1. **"Both assumptions feel correct!"** — Because the examiner designs them to *feel* that way.
2. **"I assume too much."** — Students bring their own real-world knowledge into the question. Big mistake.
3. **"The statement is negative, so I get confused."** — Negative statements flip our logic.

The golden cure: **Judge only from the speaker's point of view.** Ask yourself — *"What must the speaker have already believed in his mind before saying this sentence?"* Nothing more, nothing less.

---

## 4. Chunk 1: What is a Statement?

A **Statement** is simply a sentence spoken or written by someone — an announcement, advice, notice, suggestion, or claim.

> Example: *"The government should ban single-use plastic to save the environment."*

That's it. The statement is the visible tip of the iceberg.

## Chunk 2: What is an Assumption?

An **Assumption** is the invisible part of the iceberg — something the speaker **takes for granted** while speaking, without actually saying it.

For the statement above, the speaker must be assuming:

- **Assumption I:** Single-use plastic harms the environment. ✅ (Implicit — otherwise why ban it?)
- **Assumption II:** A ban will actually reduce plastic use. ✅ (Implicit — otherwise the advice is pointless.)

But consider:

- **Assumption III:** The government has the constitutional power to ban plastic. ❌ (Not implicit — the speaker is only giving advice; he isn't thinking about constitutional powers.)

🧠 **Active Recall:** Before reading further — in your own words, what is the difference between a statement and an assumption? Say it aloud.

---

## 5. Chunk 3: The Golden Test — "Why Did the Speaker Say This?"

The single most reliable method:

> **Negation Test:** Mentally make the assumption *false*. If the statement now becomes meaningless or absurd, the assumption is **implicit**. If the statement still survives, the assumption is **not implicit**.

**Example:**
Statement: *"Buy Vitamin-C tablets to boost your immunity."*
Assumption: *"Vitamin-C helps immunity."*
Negate it: "Vitamin-C does NOT help immunity." → Now the advertisement becomes nonsense. ✅ **Implicit.**

**Counter-example:**
Assumption: *"These tablets are the cheapest in the market."*
Negate it: "They are not the cheapest." → The advertisement still works (maybe they're the best, not the cheapest). ❌ **Not implicit.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Apply the negation test to this: Statement — *"The principal requested students to maintain silence in the library."* Assumption — *"Students can understand and follow the request."* Is it implicit? (Yes — if students couldn't follow it, the request would be pointless.)

---

## 6. Chunk 4: Words That Signal "Implicit" vs "Not Implicit"

**Usually IMPLICIT assumptions involve:**
- The basic purpose behind the statement (advice works, notice will be read, warning will be heeded).
- A direct cause-effect that the statement depends on.
- The audience's ability to act on the statement.

**Usually NOT IMPLICIT assumptions involve:**
- Words like *"only"*, *"best"*, *"always"*, *"all"*, *"never"* — extreme claims the speaker never made.
- Extra information, comparisons, or statistics not needed by the statement.
- Restatement of the statement itself (that's not an assumption, that's a copy).
- Your own real-world knowledge ("In reality, bans don't work" — irrelevant!).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why is an assumption containing the word "only" almost always rejected? Think of your own example.

---

## 7. Chunk 5: Special Cases

**(a) Notice/Appeal statements** — *"Do not litter."* Hidden assumption: people will read and may comply. ✅

**(b) Advertisement statements** — *"Use Glow soap for fair skin."* Hidden assumption: people desire fair skin and ads influence buyers. ✅ But "Glow is the *best* soap" ❌ (extreme word).

**(c) Suggestion statements ("should")** — *"India should adopt a presidential system."* Hidden assumption: the speaker believes change is possible/beneficial. ✅ But "the presidential system is perfect" ❌.

**(d) Negative statements** — *"Do not cross the railway track."* Hidden assumption: crossing is dangerous / people might otherwise cross. ✅

---

## 8. Mnemonic Trick: **"S-P-E-A-K"**

Before marking any assumption, ask if the speaker truly had it in mind:

- **S** — *Speaker's mind only* (ignore your own knowledge)
- **P** — *Purpose* (does the assumption serve the statement's purpose?)
- **E** — *Extreme words?* (only/best/all → reject)
- **A** — *Apply negation test* (kill the assumption; does the statement die too?)
- **K** — *Keep it minimal* (the smallest belief needed, nothing extra)

If an assumption passes S-P-E-A-K, it is implicit. If it fails even one letter, drop it.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Recite S-P-E-A-K once without looking. Which letter do you find hardest to apply?

## 9. Summary Table

| Concept | Meaning | Quick Test |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | What is openly said | The visible sentence |
| Assumption | What is silently believed | Negation test — statement must collapse |
| Implicit assumption | Directly hidden in the statement | Passes S-P-E-A-K |
| Not implicit | Extra, extreme, or copied idea | Fails S-P-E-A-K |
| Extreme words | only, best, all, always, never | Almost always reject |
| Speaker's view | The only viewpoint that matters | Never use outside knowledge |

📌 **Review this chapter after 2 days.** Redo the negation test on 5 fresh statements from a newspaper — that is the fastest way to make this skill permanent.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — "Both look related, so mark both."**
The examiner deliberately writes two assumptions that *both* seem connected to the statement. But only one is truly *necessary* for the statement; the other is merely *possible* or *desirable*. Remember: related ≠ implicit. Run the negation test on each separately — the one whose negation destroys the statement is your answer.

**Trap 2 — Heavy vocabulary to confuse you.**
Statements using formal or legal-sounding words ("ameliorate", "stringent punitive measures") are designed to make you panic and over-assume. The trick: translate the statement into plain Hindi/English in your head first, *then* look for assumptions. Difficult words never change the logic.

**Trap 3 — The double-relationship question.**
Some questions hide an assumption inside another assumption, or make Assumption II a *conclusion* dressed as an assumption. If an option tells you what *will happen* (a result) rather than what the speaker *already believed* (a foundation), it is a conclusion — reject it. Assumptions look *backward* (what was believed before speaking); conclusions look *forward* (what follows after).
