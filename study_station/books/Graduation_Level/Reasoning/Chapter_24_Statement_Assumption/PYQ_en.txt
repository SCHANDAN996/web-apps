# PYQ Analysis — Statement & Assumption (Advanced Level)

## 1. How Exams Ask Questions from This Chapter

Statement & Assumption is a permanent fixture of the reasoning sections of SSC CGL, IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, Railway NTPC, and State PCS prelims. Across exams, the question format is remarkably stable:

- A short **statement** (one or two sentences) is given, followed by **two assumptions** labelled I and II.
- The candidate must decide: only I is implicit / only II is implicit / both / neither.
- Occasionally three assumptions are given, or the question asks which assumption "weakens" or "supports" the statement (Banking exams).

The chapter appears **regularly but in small numbers** — typically a handful of questions per paper in Banking mains and SSC CGL, and occasionally in Railway and State exams. It is a **high-accuracy, low-time** chapter: well-prepared candidates treat it as a scoring zone because the logic is fixed and learnable.

## 2. Sub-topic Weightage (in words)

Based on the recurring pattern of past papers, questions cluster into these types:

1. **Notice/Announcement/Instruction statements** (most frequent) — e.g., "The bank has instructed customers to update KYC." The hidden assumption is that the instruction *can be followed* and *will have an audience*. This is the single most repeated pattern.
2. **Advertisement/Appeal statements** — "Buy X soap for glowing skin." Assumption: appeals influence people.
3. **Cause–effect / policy statements** — "Government should privatise railways to improve efficiency." Trap: the assumption is NOT that privatisation is the *only* way.
4. **Suggestion/Advice statements** ("should/must") — assumption is that the problem exists and the suggestion is feasible.
5. **Negative-trap statements** — assumptions that merely restate the statement, or that use extreme words ("only", "all", "never") — these are almost always NOT implicit.

## 3. Exam-wise Distribution (qualitative)

- **IBPS/SBI PO & Clerk**: The strongest presence. Questions often appear in mains with 3-assumption formats and "which assumption is implicit" phrasing. Statements are longer and more abstract (economy, policy, banking).
- **SSC CGL / CHSL**: 1–2 questions, simpler statements, classic two-assumption format. Frequently mixed with Statement–Conclusion.
- **Railway (NTPC, Group D)**: Occasional, very direct statements (notices, instructions).
- **State PCS prelims**: Appears within "logical reasoning" blocks; statements often administrative/governmental in tone.

## 4. Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

1. **The Restatement Trap**: An assumption that simply paraphrases the statement looks "safe" but is NOT an assumption — an assumption must be *underlying*, not *stated*.
2. **The Extreme-Word Trap**: Assumptions containing "only", "all", "always", "never", "best" are designed to lure candidates who over-read. They are usually rejected.
3. **The Desirable-vs-Implicit Trap**: What is *desirable* or *good* is not necessarily *assumed*. "The government should provide free education" does not assume education is free nowhere.
4. **The Course-of-Action Confusion**: In Banking exams, assumption questions are mixed with course-of-action questions; candidates apply the wrong test (feasibility vs. underlying belief).
5. **The Both-Implicit Bias**: Examiners know candidates over-select "both". In a majority of well-set questions, only one assumption is implicit.

## 5. Top 10 High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

**Q1.** Statement: The municipal corporation has advised residents to boil drinking water during the monsoon.
Assumptions:
I. Residents may fall ill if they drink unboiled water.
II. Residents will follow the advice.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Advising presupposes a risk (I) and that advice can influence behaviour (II). Both are implicit.
**Trap:** Candidates reject II thinking "people may not follow" — but the *advisor* assumes they can/will; actual compliance is irrelevant.

**Q2.** Statement: "Use our coaching institute — the surest way to crack SSC CGL," an advertisement claims.
Assumptions:
I. Advertisements influence people's decisions.
II. This institute is the only way to crack SSC CGL.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Every advertisement assumes it can influence people (I). II uses the extreme word "only" — the advertiser does not assume exclusivity, only superiority.
**Trap:** "Surest way" in the statement tempts candidates to accept II. Extreme restatement ≠ assumption.

**Q3.** Statement: The government has decided to increase the number of trains on the Delhi–Mumbai route to reduce overcrowding.
Assumptions:
I. Overcrowding exists on this route.
II. Adding trains will reduce overcrowding.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** A remedy assumes the problem exists (I) and that the remedy will work (II).
**Trap:** None major — a standard policy-statement pattern; answer quickly.

**Q4.** Statement: "If you trouble me, I will slap you," A warned B.
Assumptions:
I. B may trouble A.
II. A can slap B.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** A warning assumes the warned act is possible (I) and the threatened consequence is executable (II). Classic SSC-style question.
**Trap:** Candidates overthink morality ("slapping is wrong") — irrelevant to implicitness.

**Q5.** Statement: The company has decided to give bonus to all employees to motivate them.
Assumptions:
I. Employees are currently not motivated at all.
II. Monetary incentives can motivate employees.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The action assumes money motivates (II). I is extreme — "not motivated at all" is not assumed; they may simply need *more* motivation.
**Trap:** I is the extreme-word trap in disguise ("at all").

**Q6.** Statement: The principal instructed the teachers to complete the syllabus by November.
Assumptions:
I. Teachers can complete the syllabus by November.
II. The syllabus is currently incomplete.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** An instruction assumes feasibility (I) and a pending task (II).
**Trap:** Some reject II as "obvious" — obvious underlying facts ARE assumptions.

**Q7.** Statement: "Join our gym and lose 10 kg in a month," reads a banner.
Assumptions:
I. People want to lose weight.
II. Everyone who joins will lose exactly 10 kg.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The appeal assumes demand exists (I). II is an extreme literal claim, not an underlying assumption.
**Trap:** II mirrors the statement's wording — restatement trap.

**Q8.** Statement: The bank should reduce interest rates to increase lending. (Banking-mains style, 3 assumptions)
Assumptions:
I. High interest rates are discouraging borrowing.
II. Reducing rates is the only way to increase lending.
III. The bank has the authority to change rates.
(a) I and III (b) Only I (c) I and II (d) All three
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The suggestion assumes a cause (I) and feasibility/authority (III). II fails on "only".
**Trap:** II is the classic extreme-word trap; III is often wrongly rejected as "too technical" — feasibility is always assumed in suggestions.

**Q9.** Statement: The traffic police have installed cameras at the crossing to catch violators.
Assumptions:
I. Violations occur at that crossing.
II. Cameras will eliminate all traffic violations in the city.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The action assumes violations happen there (I). II is wildly extreme ("all", "city" vs. one crossing).
**Trap:** II tests whether candidates notice scope inflation (one crossing → whole city).

**Q10.** Statement: The school has made it compulsory for students to wear identity cards on campus.
Assumptions:
I. Identity cards can help identify students.
II. Students will never lose their identity cards.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The rule assumes the cards serve their purpose (I). II uses "never" — rules don't assume perfect compliance, only general enforceability.
**Trap:** Candidates accept II thinking rules assume obedience — but "never lose" is an absolute, not an assumption.

## 6. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Review

- **15-second questions (attempt immediately):** Q1, Q3, Q4, Q6 — short statements, standard notice/instruction/warning patterns where both assumptions are clearly implicit. These follow memorised templates; do not spend more than 15 seconds.
- **45-second questions (attempt with care):** Q2, Q5, Q7, Q9, Q10 — contain extreme-word or restatement traps; verify each assumption against the "underlying, not stated, not extreme" test.
- **Skip candidates:** Q8-style questions (3 assumptions, abstract policy statements, Banking mains) — if the statement involves unfamiliar economic/policy context and multiple combination options (I & II, II & III, etc.), the elimination cost is high. If you cannot eliminate at least one assumption within 45 seconds, mark and move on; return only if time permits. Similarly, skip any question where the statement and assumptions are nearly identical in wording and you cannot separate "stated" from "underlying" quickly — these are deliberately ambiguous and low-yield.

**Rule of thumb:** Assumption questions are among the fastest in reasoning — a prepared candidate should average under 30 seconds. If a question breaks your pattern recognition, it is designed to; skip it rather than gamble.
