You know how your school has different notice boards — some announcements in English, some in Hindi, maybe a few in the local language? That’s exactly what happened with Ashoka. He wanted his messages to reach everyone, so he used different scripts for different regions. But here’s where examiners trap students. Let’s clear it up like a story.

Imagine you’re Ashoka. You’ve just won the Kalinga war, but the bloodshed changes you. Now you want to spread messages of kindness, truth, and Dhamma across your huge empire. You order your officers to carve these messages on rocks and pillars. But the people in the northwest (modern Pakistan/Afghanistan) don’t read the same script as people in central India. So what do you do? You use two main scripts.

The daily-life example:
Think of Brahmi as the “Hindi/English notice board” placed almost everywhere in your school. Almost all students read it. Now, there’s one small corner of the school where students read Urdu, which is written right-to-left. So the headmaster puts a separate notice there in Urdu. That Urdu-like notice is Kharoshthi. It’s still the same message, just written differently because that group reads from right to left.

Most Ashokan inscriptions are in Brahmi – the script that later became the grandmother of many Indian scripts. But in the extreme northwest – places like Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra – the same edicts are in Kharoshthi, a script written from right to left. The content is almost identical. So the examiner will ask: “All Ashokan edicts are in Brahmi. True or False?” That’s false, because of those few Kharoshthi ones. They may also ask: “Kharoshthi was used in which part of Ashoka’s empire?” Answer: North-western frontier.

The unforgettable trick:
Close your eyes and picture a map of India. Now look at the top-left corner — the northwest. That’s where Kharoshthi lived. K for corner, K for Kharoshthi. Or: “Top-left corner, Kharoshthi corner.” Simple.

Now, one more subtle trap: Ashoka’s edicts were in Prakrit language, not Sanskrit. Even the Greek and Aramaic inscriptions he made for the far northwest were in foreign languages, but the Indian ones were in Prakrit. So remember: Ashoka’s language = Prakrit, scripts = mostly Brahmi, some Kharoshthi.
