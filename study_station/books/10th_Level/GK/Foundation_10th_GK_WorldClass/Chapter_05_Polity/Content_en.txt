INDIAN POLITY

The Rulebook of the World’s Largest Democracy

*February 1948. A room full of brilliant minds, reams of paper, and an impossible deadline. The Drafting Committee had studied 60 constitutions, debated for 114 days, and handwritten every single page. When Dr. B.R. Ambedkar placed the final draft before the Constituent Assembly on November 4, 1948, India was about to gift itself a document that would govern over 1.4 billion dreams. “I feel that the Constitution is workable; it is flexible and it is strong enough to hold the country together both in peace time and in war time,” Ambedkar said. That handwritten original, now preserved in helium-filled cases, weighs 3.75 kg and remains the only constitution of its kind — entirely calligraphed, never typed. This chapter is your crash course into that very rulebook, one that examiners love to test and citizens must know.*

1. THE BIRTH OF A CONSTITUTION

The idea of a Constituent Assembly was first floated by M.N. Roy in 1934. It finally took shape under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946). The Assembly’s first meeting was on December 9, 1946. Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha was the provisional Chairman, and later Dr. Rajendra Prasad became the permanent President.

The Drafting Committee, chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, had seven members. The Constitution was adopted on November 26, 1949 (celebrated as Constitution Day) and came into full force on January 26, 1950 — Republic Day. The delay was deliberate; January 26 was chosen to honour the Purna Swaraj Declaration of 1930.

Sources of the Constitution
Our founders didn’t reinvent the wheel. They borrowed the best from across the world:

Country	Feature Borrowed
Britain	Parliamentary system, Rule of Law, Single Citizenship
USA	Fundamental Rights, Judicial Review, Impeachment of President
Canada	Federation with a strong centre, Residuary powers
Ireland	Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)
Australia	Concurrent List, Freedom of Trade and Commerce
Japan	Procedure Established by Law
USSR	Fundamental Duties (added later in 1976)

2. THE PREAMBLE — SOUL OF THE CONSTITUTION

The Preamble declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic. It also promises Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity.

The words “Socialist”, “Secular”, and “Integrity” were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976).

In the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), the Supreme Court ruled that the Preamble can be amended, but the basic structure of the Constitution cannot be destroyed.

The Preamble has been amended only once so far (42nd Amendment).

3. FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS (Articles 12–35) — YOUR ARMOUR

Think of Fundamental Rights as six shields the Constitution gives every citizen against state overreach.

Mnemonic → “Every Free Indian Reads Constitutional Chapters”

E quality (Art. 14–18)

F reedom (Art. 19–22)

I ndividuals against Exploitation (Art. 23–24)

R eligious Freedom (Art. 25–28)

C ultural and Educational Rights (Art. 29–30)

C onstitutional Remedies (Art. 32)

Dr. Ambedkar called Article 32 the “heart and soul of the Constitution” because it allows you to directly approach the Supreme Court if any Fundamental Right is violated. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed the Right to Property from the Fundamental Rights list; it is now a legal right under Article 300A.

During a National Emergency (Article 352), Article 19 is automatically suspended. However, Articles 20 and 21 (protection in respect of conviction and right to life) remain alive even during an Emergency.

4. DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES OF STATE POLICY (DPSP) — THE WISH LIST

Articles 36 to 51 contain the DPSPs. They are instructions to the government to create a welfare state. They are non-justiciable, meaning courts cannot enforce them. Yet, the Supreme Court has said DPSPs are the conscience of the Constitution and must be read harmoniously with Fundamental Rights.

DPSPs are classified into three groups:

Socialist — e.g., adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work (Art. 39)

Gandhian — e.g., village panchayats (Art. 40), promotion of cottage industries (Art. 43)

Liberal-Intellectual — e.g., Uniform Civil Code (Art. 44), separation of judiciary from executive (Art. 50)

Article 44 (Uniform Civil Code) and Article 45 (free and compulsory education for children) are the most questioned DPSPs. Article 45 later became a Fundamental Right through Article 21A by the 86th Amendment (2002).

🧠 Active Recall: Are DPSPs enforceable in court? Name the Article that aims for a Uniform Civil Code.

5. FUNDAMENTAL DUTIES (Article 51A) — THE PLEDGE

Originally, the Constitution had no Fundamental Duties. The 42nd Amendment (1976) added 10 duties inspired by the USSR Constitution. The 86th Amendment (2002) added an 11th duty — to provide opportunities for education to children between 6 and 14 years.

These duties are non-enforceable like DPSPs, but they remind citizens that rights and duties go hand in hand.

6. THE UNION EXECUTIVE — THREE PILLARS
6.1 The President (Article 52)

Constitutional head of India; first citizen.

Electoral College: Elected members of Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, and State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi, Puducherry, and now Jammu & Kashmir).

Mnemonic → “PREM”: Parliament Elected + Rajya Sabha Elected + State Assembly Members Elected. (Remember: Nominated members of Rajya Sabha and Legislative Councils do NOT vote in President’s election.)

6.2 The Vice-President (Article 63)

Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha.

Electoral College: ALL members of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha (elected + nominated). State Assemblies do NOT participate.

Mnemonic → “VP = All PMs”: Vice-President elected by All Parliament Members.

6.3 The Prime Minister and Council of Ministers (Articles 74–75)

The real executive power lies with the PM. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

A person can be a Minister for 6 months without being a Member of either House, after which they must get elected.

🧠 Active Recall: Who votes in the Presidential election but NOT in the Vice-Presidential election? (Hint: Think of State Assemblies.)

7. THE PARLIAMENT — TEMPLE OF DEMOCRACY

Lok Sabha (House of the People): Max strength 550 (530 from States + 20 from UTs). Term: 5 years.

Rajya Sabha (Council of States): Max strength 250 (238 elected by States & UTs + 12 nominated by President). It is a permanent house; one-third members retire every 2 years.

Money Bill (Article 110): Can be introduced only in Lok Sabha. Rajya Sabha can only recommend changes and must return it within 14 days. If not, the Bill is deemed passed.

Note: The nomination of 2 Anglo-Indian members to Lok Sabha was ended by the 104th Amendment (2019).

🧠 Active Recall: What happens if Rajya Sabha does not return a Money Bill within 14 days?

8. THE JUDICIARY — THE GUARDIAN
8.1 Supreme Court (Articles 124–147)

Maximum judges: 34 (1 Chief Justice + 33 others).

Judges retire at 65 years. Removed by impeachment (Parliament).

Jurisdiction: Original (disputes between states, etc.), Appellate, Advisory, and Writ Jurisdiction under Article 32.

8.2 High Court (Articles 214–237)

Each state has a High Court (some common for multiple states). Judges retire at 62 years.

8.3 Important Doctrines

Basic Structure Doctrine — Kesavananda Bharati (1973).

Judicial Review — borrowed from the USA.

Writs: Five types — Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
Mnemonic → “PM CHQ” (P for Prohibition, M for Mandamus, C for Certiorari, H for Habeas Corpus, Q for Quo Warranto).

🧠 Active Recall: What is the retirement age of a Supreme Court judge? Name all five writs.

9. STATE GOVERNMENT & LOCAL SELF-GOVERNMENT

Governor (Article 153): Appointed by the President; holds office at President’s pleasure. Sworn in by the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court.

Chief Minister: Real head of the state government.

73rd and 74th Amendments (1992): Gave constitutional status to Panchayats and Municipalities respectively. Panchayats (Article 243–243O) and Municipalities (Article 243P–243ZG) now form the third tier of government.

10. EMERGENCY PROVISIONS — WHEN EXCEPTIONS RULE
Type	Article	Declaration
National Emergency	352	War, external aggression, armed rebellion
President’s Rule (State Emergency)	356	Failure of constitutional machinery in a state
Financial Emergency	360	Threat to financial stability

National Emergency has been declared three times — 1962 (China war), 1971 (Pakistan war), 1975 (internal disturbance).

Under Article 356, maximum duration is 3 years with parliamentary approval.

⚠️ CAUTION: EXAMINER’S TRAP

Trap 1: President vs Vice-President Electoral College
Statement: “The President is elected by the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and all State Legislative Assemblies.”
Reality: It’s the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies, not all members (Legislative Council members don’t vote).
Trap Type: Similar Name Confusion — mixing “Parliament” and “Legislative Assembly”.

Trap 2: Article 32 vs Article 226
Statement: “Under Article 32, you can move the Supreme Court for violation of any legal right.”
Reality: Article 32 is only for Fundamental Rights. For other rights, you go to the High Court under Article 226.
Trap Type: Almost Correct Statement — 90% correct; the tiny word “any” changes the entire meaning.

Trap 3: Fundamental Rights during National Emergency
Statement: “During a National Emergency, all Fundamental Rights are automatically suspended.”
Reality: Only Article 19 gets suspended automatically. Articles 20 and 21 never suspend.
Trap Type: Overgeneralization — examiner uses “all” to catch you.

Trap 4: Fundamental Duties — Numbers Game
Statement: “The 42nd Amendment added 11 Fundamental Duties to the Constitution.”
Reality: The 42nd Amendment added 10 duties; the 86th Amendment (2002) added the 11th.
Trap Type: Chronological Confusion — mixing amendments and their years.
