World Geography (Basic) — the whole planet on one page of your mind.

◆ In 1519, Ferdinand Magellan's fleet left Spain sailing west. About three years later, in 1522, one surviving ship, the Victoria, came home — and its crew found a puzzle. Their careful logbook said one date, but the people on land said the next day. Nobody had lost count. By going round the Earth westward, along with the Sun, they had quietly "lost" one day. That single missing day is the reason the International Date Line exists — and it is a favourite exam question even today. Geography is full of such surprises. This chapter opens them one by one.

1. Our Address in Space: The Solar System

The Sun is a star at the centre of our solar system. Eight planets revolve around it. In order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

Mnemonic: "My Very Efficient Mother Just Served Us Nuts" — M V E M J S U N.

Mercury is the smallest planet and nearest to the Sun.

Venus is the hottest planet (its thick carbon dioxide blanket traps heat), the brightest planet in our sky, and is called "Earth's twin" because of similar size. It is also seen as the "Morning Star" or "Evening Star".

Earth is the "Blue Planet" because about 71% of its surface is covered by water. It is the only planet known to support life.

Mars is the "Red Planet" (iron oxide on its surface).

Jupiter is the largest planet. Saturn is famous for its bright rings.

Pluto was treated as the ninth planet until 2006, when the International Astronomical Union (IAU) reclassified it as a "dwarf planet".

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite. It takes about 27 days to go round the Earth, and it rotates once in the same time — so we always see the same side of the Moon. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth.

🧠 Pop quiz (Active Recall): Which planet is the hottest — Mercury or Venus? (Answer: Venus. Mercury is nearest, but Venus is hottest.)
Why do we always see the same face of the Moon? (Answer: its rotation and revolution take the same time.)

2. Shape and Motions of the Earth

The Earth is not a perfect sphere. It is slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the Equator. This shape is called a geoid ("Earth-like shape").

Rotation: The Earth spins on its axis from west to east once in about 24 hours. Rotation causes day and night. Because the Earth turns west to east, the Sun appears to rise in the east.

Revolution: The Earth goes round the Sun in about 365¼ days. The extra ¼ day adds up to one full day every four years — that is why February has 29 days in a leap year.

The axis is tilted. It makes an angle of 66½° with the orbital plane (that is 23½° from the vertical). Revolution plus this tilt causes the seasons.

Four key dates (Northern Hemisphere view):

21 June — Summer Solstice: Sun overhead on the Tropic of Cancer; longest day in the Northern Hemisphere.

22 December — Winter Solstice: Sun overhead on the Tropic of Capricorn; shortest day in the Northern Hemisphere.

21 March and 23 September — Equinoxes: Sun overhead on the Equator; day and night are equal all over the world.

Perihelion (Earth nearest to the Sun) is around 3 January. Aphelion (Earth farthest from the Sun) is around 4 July. Notice: the Earth is nearest to the Sun during the Northern winter. So distance does NOT cause seasons — the tilt does.

🧠 Quick check: On which date is the Sun overhead on the Tropic of Capricorn? (22 December)
What causes day and night — rotation or revolution? (Rotation)

Mnemonic for the solstices: "June — Jump up north (Cancer), December — Dive down south (Capricorn)."

3. Latitudes and Longitudes: The Earth's Address System

Latitudes (parallels) are imaginary circles parallel to the Equator. The Equator is 0°. Poles are 90°N and 90°S.

Important parallels:

Equator — 0°

Tropic of Cancer — 23½°N

Tropic of Capricorn — 23½°S

Arctic Circle — 66½°N

Antarctic Circle — 66½°S

There are 90 parallels in each hemisphere; counting the Equator, there are 181 parallels in all (each pole is counted as a point).

Heat zones: Torrid Zone (between the two Tropics; gets overhead Sun), Temperate Zones (between Tropics and Polar Circles), Frigid Zones (beyond the Polar Circles).

Longitudes (meridians) are half-circles joining the North and South Poles. The Prime Meridian (0°) passes through Greenwich, near London. There are 360 meridians: 180 east and 180 west. All meridians are of equal length, and they meet at the poles.

Time and longitude: The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours. So it turns 15° in 1 hour and 1° in 4 minutes. Places to the east are ahead in time; places to the west are behind.

Example: When it is 12 noon at Greenwich (0°), at 90°E it is 90 × 4 = 360 minutes = 6 hours ahead → 6 p.m.

Indian Standard Time (IST) is taken from 82°30'E, which passes near Mirzapur (Uttar Pradesh). 82.5 × 4 = 330 minutes, so IST is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of Greenwich Mean Time (GMT).

International Date Line (IDL): It runs roughly along the 180° meridian but zig-zags to avoid land. Crossing it changes the date by one day. Travelling eastward across it (Asia → America), the date goes back one day. Travelling westward (America → Asia), the date goes forward one day.

🧠 Pop quiz: How many minutes of time difference does 1° of longitude make? (4 minutes)
Which meridian is used for IST? (82°30'E)

Mnemonic for time: "15 per hour, 4 per degree — East is Early (ahead), West is Late (behind)."

4. Inside the Earth

The Earth has three main layers:

Crust — the thin outer layer. About 35 km thick under continents and only about 5 km under the ocean floor. Continental crust is called SIAL (silica + alumina); oceanic crust is called SIMA (silica + magnesium).

Mantle — below the crust, extending to a depth of about 2,900 km.

Core — the innermost layer, made mainly of nickel and iron, so it is called NIFE. Its radius is about 3,500 km.

Rocks are of three types:

Igneous (primary) rocks form when molten magma cools — granite (inside the Earth, intrusive) and basalt (on the surface, extrusive).

Sedimentary rocks form from layers of deposited particles — sandstone, shale, limestone. Fossils are found mainly in these rocks.

Metamorphic rocks form when rocks change under heat and pressure — limestone → marble, shale → slate, sandstone → quartzite, granite → gneiss.

🧠 Quick check: Which layer is called NIFE? (Core)
Marble is formed from which rock? (Limestone)

Mnemonic: "Lime turns to Marble, Shale goes Slate" — L→M, S→S.

5. Landforms: Mountains, Plateaus and Plains

Fold mountains are formed when layers of rock are pushed and folded. Young fold mountains: Himalayas (Asia), Alps (Europe), Andes (South America), Rockies (North America). Old fold mountains: Aravalli (India), Appalachians (USA), Urals (Russia).

Block mountains form when large blocks of land are raised; the sunk part forms a rift valley. Examples: Black Forest (Germany) and Vosges (France), with the Rhine valley between them.

Volcanic mountains are built by lava: Kilimanjaro (Africa), Mount Fuji (Japan).

The Andes are the longest mountain range on land. The Pamir Knot is called the "Roof of the World". The Tibet Plateau is the highest and largest high plateau.

Highest peaks of continents: Mount Everest (Asia, 8,848.86 m, height jointly announced by Nepal and China in December 2020), Aconcagua (South America), Denali (North America), Kilimanjaro (Africa), Elbrus (Europe), Vinson Massif (Antarctica), Kosciuszko (mainland Australia).

🧠 Pop quiz: Name one block mountain. (Black Forest or Vosges)
Which is the highest peak of Africa? (Kilimanjaro)

6. Continents: Seven Giants

There are seven continents. In order of area: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, Australia.

Mnemonic: "All Animals Need Space And Easy Air" — Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, Australia.

Asia — largest continent, about one-third of the Earth's land area. The Ural Mountains separate it from Europe.

Africa — second largest. The Equator runs almost through its middle, and both the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn pass through it. Home of the Sahara (largest hot desert) and the Nile (longest river).

North America — joined to South America by the narrow Isthmus of Panama.

South America — has the Andes and the Amazon, the largest river by volume of water.

Antarctica — lies entirely in the Southern Hemisphere with the South Pole near its centre; covered by thick ice sheets. India's research stations there include Maitri and Bharati (Dakshin Gangotri was the first).

Europe — the Arctic Circle passes through it; it is surrounded by water on three sides.

Australia — the smallest continent, lies entirely in the Southern Hemisphere and is called the "island continent".

🧠 Quick check: Which continent is crossed by the Equator and both Tropics? (Africa)
Which continent is called the "island continent"? (Australia)

7. Oceans: The Blue Majority

Five oceans in order of size: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern, Arctic.

Pacific — largest and deepest; almost circular. The Mariana Trench, the deepest known point on Earth (about 11 km deep), lies in it. The "Ring of Fire" of volcanoes surrounds it.

Atlantic — second largest; S-shaped. It is the busiest ocean for shipping.

Indian — the only ocean named after a country (India); almost triangular.

Arctic — smallest; around the North Pole; joined to the Pacific through the narrow Bering Strait.

Ocean floor relief: continental shelf → continental slope → deep sea plain → ocean deeps (trenches). Average salinity of ocean water is about 35 parts per thousand.

Ocean currents: warm currents flow from the Equator towards the poles; cold currents flow from the poles towards the Equator.

Warm: Gulf Stream (North Atlantic), Kuroshio (near Japan).

Cold: Labrador (near Canada), Humboldt or Peru (west coast of South America), Benguela (south-west Africa), California.

Where warm and cold currents meet, plankton grows and the best fishing grounds form — for example, near Newfoundland (Grand Banks, where the Gulf Stream meets the Labrador Current) and the seas around Japan.

Tides: The highest high tides (spring tides) occur on full moon and new moon days, when the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line. Tides of the smallest range (neap tides) occur at the first and last quarter of the Moon.

🧠 Pop quiz: Name the only ocean named after a country. (Indian Ocean)
Which current is cold — Gulf Stream or Labrador? (Labrador)

8. Atmosphere: Our Invisible Blanket

Air is mainly nitrogen (about 78%) and oxygen (about 21%); argon, carbon dioxide and other gases make up the rest.

Five layers, from the ground up:

Troposphere — average height about 13 km; almost all weather (clouds, rain, storms) happens here.

Stratosphere — up to about 50 km; nearly free of clouds, ideal for flying aeroplanes; contains the ozone layer that protects us from ultraviolet rays.

Mesosphere — up to about 80 km; meteorites burn up here.

Thermosphere — the ionosphere lies here; it reflects radio waves back to Earth.

Exosphere — the uppermost layer with very thin air.

Mnemonic: "The Strong Man Throws Eggs" — Troposphere, Stratosphere, Mesosphere, Thermosphere, Exosphere.

Winds: Air moves from high pressure to low pressure. Due to the Earth's rotation, winds bend to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere (Coriolis force; Ferrel's law).

Planetary winds: Trade winds (towards the Equator), Westerlies (towards the poles in mid-latitudes), Polar Easterlies.

Local winds: Loo (hot, dry, northern India), Chinook (warm wind of the Rockies, called "snow eater"), Foehn (warm, dry wind of the Alps), Sirocco (hot, dusty wind from the Sahara towards Italy), Harmattan (dry wind of West Africa, called "the doctor" because it brings relief from humid heat).

Tropical cyclones have different local names: Hurricanes (Caribbean / Atlantic), Typhoons (China Sea / West Pacific), Willy-willies (north-west Australia), Cyclones (Indian Ocean).

🧠 Quick check: In which layer is the ozone layer? (Stratosphere)
Which local wind is called the "snow eater"? (Chinook)

9. Grasslands and Deserts of the World

Temperate grasslands have different names in different lands:

Prairies — North America

Pampas — Argentina (South America)

Steppes — Eurasia (Central Asia)

Velds — South Africa

Downs — Australia

Canterbury — New Zealand

Tropical grasslands: Savanna (Africa), Llanos (Venezuela–Colombia), Campos (Brazil).

Mnemonic: "PaPa SteVe Down in Canterbury" — Prairies, Pampas, Steppes, Velds, Downs, Canterbury.

Deserts: Sahara (North Africa, largest hot desert), Kalahari (southern Africa), Atacama (Chile, among the driest places), Gobi (Mongolia–China, cold desert), Thar (India–Pakistan), Great Victoria (Australia). Antarctica, which gets extremely little precipitation, is the largest desert if cold (polar) deserts are counted.

🧠 Pop quiz: Pampas are found in which country? (Argentina)
Velds are in which continent? (Africa)

10. Rivers, Lakes and Record Holders

Nile — longest river in the world (flows north into the Mediterranean).

Amazon — largest river by volume of water.

Congo — crosses the Equator twice. Limpopo — crosses the Tropic of Capricorn twice.

Caspian Sea — largest lake in the world (salt water).

Lake Superior (North America) — largest freshwater lake by area.

Lake Baikal (Russia) — deepest lake.

Dead Sea — very salty; its shore is the lowest land point on Earth.

Angel Falls (Venezuela) — highest waterfall. Niagara Falls lies on the USA–Canada border; Victoria Falls is on the Zambezi (Zambia–Zimbabwe).

Greenland — largest island. Indonesia — largest archipelago (group of islands). Great Barrier Reef (off Queensland, Australia) — largest coral reef system.

Cities on rivers: London – Thames, Paris – Seine, Cairo – Nile, Baghdad – Tigris, Rome – Tiber, Vienna and Budapest – Danube, Washington DC – Potomac, New York – Hudson, Moscow – Moskva, Berlin – Spree.

🧠 Quick check: Which lake is the deepest? (Baikal)
Longest river vs largest river by volume? (Nile vs Amazon)

11. Straits, Canals and Boundary Lines

A strait is a narrow water passage joining two large water bodies.

Strait of Gibraltar — joins the Atlantic and the Mediterranean; separates Spain (Europe) from Morocco (Africa).

Bering Strait — joins the Pacific and the Arctic; separates Asia (Russia) from North America (Alaska).

Palk Strait — between India and Sri Lanka.

Strait of Malacca — between Malaysia and Sumatra (Indonesia); links the Andaman Sea (Indian Ocean) with the South China Sea (Pacific).

Strait of Hormuz — joins the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman.

Dover Strait — between England and France.

Canals: Suez Canal (Egypt) joins the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea; opened in 1869. Panama Canal (Panama) joins the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans; opened in 1914.

Boundary lines:

Radcliffe Line — India and Pakistan (also India–Bangladesh)

McMahon Line — India and China

Durand Line — Pakistan and Afghanistan

38th Parallel — North Korea and South Korea

49th Parallel — USA and Canada

Maginot Line — a line of defence forts built by France on its border with Germany

🧠 Pop quiz: Which canal joins the Mediterranean and the Red Sea? (Suez)
Which line separates Pakistan and Afghanistan? (Durand Line)

⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

Trap 1: The "nearest means hottest" trap

Type: लगभग सही कथन (Almost True Statement)

Trap statement: "Mercury is the hottest planet because it is nearest to the Sun."

Reality: Mercury is the nearest, but Venus is the hottest because its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat.

Smart move: Separate the two records — "nearest = Mercury, hottest = Venus".

Trap 2: Seasons and distance

Type: विपरीत कार्य-कारण (Reverse Causation)

Trap statement: "It is summer in India in June because the Earth is nearest to the Sun then."

Reality: In early July the Earth is actually farthest from the Sun (aphelion). Seasons are caused by the tilt of the axis during revolution, not by distance.

Smart move: Remember "January nearest, July farthest — tilt makes the seasons."

Trap 3: Largest, longest, deepest

Type: समान नाम/वर्ष (Similar Names / Similar Records)

Trap question: "The largest river in the world is the Nile" or "The largest freshwater lake is the Caspian Sea."

Reality: Nile is the longest; Amazon is the largest by volume. Caspian is the largest lake but salty; Superior is the largest freshwater lake by area; Baikal is the deepest.

Smart move: Read the adjective — longest, largest, deepest, highest — before matching.

Trap 4: Direction across the Date Line

Type: नकारात्मक वाक्यांश / उलटी दिशा (Negative Phrasing / Reversed Direction)

Trap statement: "A ship crossing the International Date Line from Japan towards the USA moves the date one day forward."

Reality: Going east (Asia → America) the date goes back one day; going west it goes forward.

Smart move: "East — repeat a day; West — skip a day."

🔗 Static-Dynamic Link: From Textbook to Today

Mount Everest's height: For decades books printed 8,848 m. In December 2020, Nepal and China jointly announced a new height of 8,848.86 m. If an option shows 8,848.86 m, it is the updated official figure.

Pluto: Older books list nine planets. Since the IAU decision of 2006, Pluto is a dwarf planet and the solar system has eight planets.

Strait of Hormuz and Suez Canal: These narrow waterways are in the news whenever shipping is disrupted, because a large share of world oil and trade moves through them. A static fact (which seas they join) becomes a current-affairs question.
