Why do we have summer and winter? Most people answer: "In summer the Earth is closer to the Sun." It sounds logical — stand close to a fire and you feel hot. But it is wrong, and examiners know that many students believe it. So this is the concept to clear up today.

Start with a surprising fact. Every year, around 3 January, the Earth is at its nearest point to the Sun (perihelion). Around 4 July, it is at its farthest point (aphelion). Look at those dates again. In January, India is having winter — while the Earth is nearest to the Sun! In July, India is hot — while the Earth is farthest. So distance cannot be the reason. (The difference in distance is also quite small compared with the total distance.)

The real reason is the tilt.

The daily-life example: the torch and the wall
Take a torch in a dark room. Shine it straight at the wall. You get a small, bright, hot circle of light. Now tilt the torch so the light falls slanting on the wall. The same light spreads over a big, pale patch. Same torch, same distance — but the slanting light is weaker on each spot.

The Sun is the torch. The Earth's axis is tilted (it makes 66½° with its path around the Sun, which is 23½° from upright). As the Earth goes round the Sun, this tilt always points the same way in space. So:

Around 21 June, the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun. The Sun's rays fall straight (overhead) on the Tropic of Cancer. Rays are strong, days are long → summer in the north (India, Europe, USA).

Around 22 December, the Northern Hemisphere leans away. The Sun is overhead on the Tropic of Capricorn. Rays fall slanting on India, days are short → winter in the north, but summer in Australia and South Africa.

On 21 March and 23 September, neither hemisphere leans towards the Sun. The Sun is overhead on the Equator, and day and night are equal everywhere (equinox).

So two things make summer: straight rays and long days. Both come from the tilt.

A quick proof you already know: when India has winter in December, Australians celebrate Christmas on the beach in summer. If distance were the reason, the whole Earth would have the same season at the same time. Opposite seasons in the two hemispheres prove it is the tilt.

The unforgettable trick
"Tilt makes the season, distance is no reason."
And for the dates: "June — jump up north (Cancer), December — dive down south (Capricorn)."
And for perihelion: "January — the Earth gives the Sun a hug; July — it walks away." Hug in winter, walk away in summer — funny, but true for the north.

One more trap to watch: rotation causes day and night; revolution with the tilt causes seasons. If an option says "revolution causes day and night", it is wrong.
