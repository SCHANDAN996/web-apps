🌄 The Earth’s Epic Diary: Physical Geography of India

*“In 1815, a British surveyor looked at a 15-ton rock resting on a mountain slope in Mahabalipuram and realized it had been dragged there by a glacier—proof that the Indian landmass had once been part of a frozen supercontinent. That rock is still there, a silent witness to India’s 4-billion-year journey from Gondwana to its present form.”*

India’s physical geography is not a list of rivers and mountains. It is the autobiography of a land that broke away from Africa, raced across an ocean, smashed into Asia, and is still pushing the Himalayas skyward by 5 mm every year. By the end of this chapter, you will not only know the facts—you will feel the story beneath your feet.

1. The Giant Frame: India’s Geological Backbone

India stands on four giant rock systems:

Archaean Rocks (4+ billion years old): The core of the Peninsular Plateau—granite, gneiss, and schist. They contain no fossils.

Gondwana Rocks: Sedimentary layers that hold 98% of India’s coal (Damodar, Mahanadi, Godavari valleys).

Deccan Trap (Cretaceous volcanism): Basaltic lava covering Maharashtra, MP, Gujarat—source of the famous black cotton soil.

Tertiary Sediments: The youngest rocks; they built the Himalayas, the Indo-Gangetic plains, and the coastal margins.

3. The Great Himalayas: Three Steps to the Sky

The Himalayas are a system of three parallel ranges:

Range	Hindi Name	Average Elevation	Key Features
Outer Himalayas	Shivalik	900–1200 m	Youngest; Duns (Dehradun, Patlidun)
Middle Himalayas	Himachal	3500–4500 m	Pir Panjal, Dhauladhar; hill stations
Greater Himalayas	Himadri	6000 m+	Mt. Everest (8848 m), Kanchenjunga (8586 m, India’s highest)

Exam Trap Alert: The Shivalik is the outermost range, not the lowest in terms of geological age. The northernmost is the Greater Himalaya, but physically it lies north of the Middle Himalayas. Don’t mix up location vs age.

4. The Mighty Northern Plains: The Gift of Three Rivers

These 7-lakh-sq-km plains were built by the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra. Their fine alluvium makes this one of the world’s most densely populated regions.

The Four Sub-Regions You Must Differentiate:

Bhabar: Narrow belt just south of the Shivalik. Coarse pebbles and boulders; rivers disappear underground here.

Terai: South of Bhabar. The underground streams reappear as marshy, swampy ground; dense forests and wildlife.

Bhangar (Bangar): Older alluvium, slightly elevated, often contains calcareous concretions (kankar). Forms the river terraces.

Khadar: Newer alluvium deposited by annual floods; extremely fertile, lower level.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: In which region do rivers go underground? In which do they re-emerge? (Answers: Bhabar – disappear; Terai – reappear.) Examiners love swapping these two.

Mnemonic for sequence B→T→B→K:
“Babbar Took Bhang Khadi” → Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar.

5. The Ancient Peninsular Plateau: India’s Mineral Vault

The Peninsular Plateau is the oldest and most stable part of India, rich in minerals. It is divided by the Narmada-Son rift valley into:

Central Highlands: Aravallis (Gurushikhar, 1722 m), Malwa, Bundelkhand.

Deccan Plateau: Bordered by Western Ghats (continuous, Sahyadri) and Eastern Ghats (discontinuous, Mahadeo, Mahendragiri 1501 m). The highest peak of the Ghats is Anaimudi (2695 m) in Kerala, located in the Anaimalai Hills, part of the Western Ghats.

Key plateau clusters: Chotanagpur (mineral treasure), Telangana, Karnataka (Bababudan hills), Meghalaya (Garo, Khasi, Jaintia).

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which mountain range separates the Deccan Plateau from the coastal plain on the west? What is its local name? (Western Ghats / Sahyadri.)

6. The Thar Desert: Where Rivers Ran Dry

This 2-lakh-sq-km desert in Rajasthan and Gujarat is a rain-shadow region. The Luni river is the only significant inland drainage river, ending in the Rann of Kutch. Satellite imagery shows buried channels of the ancient Saraswati river, now the dry Ghaggar-Hakra system. Indira Gandhi Canal has turned parts of the desert green.

7. Coastal Edges and Island Jewels

West Coast: Narrow (50–100 km), submerged coast, full of estuaries and lagoons (Chilika, Pulicat, Vembanad). Divided into Konkan, Kannad, Malabar.
East Coast: Wide (100–130 km), emergent coast, large deltas (Ganga, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri). Called Coromandel coast in Tamil Nadu.

Islands:

Andaman & Nicobar: Volcanic origin. Barren Island is India’s only active volcano. Saddle Peak (North Andaman) is the highest point.

Lakshadweep: Coral origin; 36 islands. Minicoy is separated from the main group by the 9° Channel.

Critical Channels (Exam favourite):

10° Channel: Andaman ↔ Nicobar

9° Channel: Lakshadweep (Minicoy) ↔ rest of Lakshadweep

8° Channel: Lakshadweep (Minicoy) ↔ Maldives

Duncan Passage: South Andaman ↔ Little Andaman

🧠 Active Recall Pause: The body of water separating Minicoy from Maldives is the ____ channel. (Answer: 8° Channel.)

8. River Systems: Himalayan vs. Peninsular
Feature	Himalayan Rivers	Peninsular Rivers
Water source	Glaciers + rain → perennial	Rain only → seasonal
Course length	Long, meandering, deep gorges	Shorter, straight, shallow valleys
Mouth	Form deltas (Ganga-Brahmaputra)	East-flowing form deltas; west-flowing form estuaries (Narmada, Tapi)
Drainage pattern	Antecedent (older than mountains)	Superimposed

Ganga System: Bhagirathi from Gangotri glacier + Alaknanda → Ganga at Devprayag. Left bank tributaries: Ramganga, Gomti, Ghaghra, Gandak, Kosi. Right bank: Yamuna (and its Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken), Son.

West-flowing rivers: Narmada (flows through rift valley between Vindhyas and Satpuras; forms estuary), Tapi (between Satpuras and Satmala), Mahi, Sabarmati. All form estuaries, not deltas.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which peninsular river forms a delta, Godavari or Narmada? (Godavari.) And why? (Because it flows east, shallow gradient; Narmada flows west in rift valley, sudden drop.)

9. The Rhythms of Climate and Soil

India’s climate is tropical monsoon. Four seasons: Cold (Jan), Hot (Mar–May), South-West Monsoon (Jun–Sep), Retreating/NE Monsoon (Oct–Dec). The Western Ghats receive heavy orographic rainfall; the Thar lies in the rain-shadow. Mawsynram (Meghalaya) holds the world record for highest average annual rainfall (~1187 cm). Winter rainfall in North India comes from Western Disturbances.

Soil High-Five:

Alluvial: 40% of India, most fertile, Ganga plains.

Black (Regur): Deccan Trap, cotton soil; moisture retentive.

Red: Peninsular India, iron-rich.

Laterite: High rainfall, leached, tea, coffee, cashew.

Desert: Sandy, low organic matter.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which soil is called regur, and for which crop is it ideal? (Black soil, cotton.)

⚠️ Caution: Examiner’s Trap
Trap	The Illusion	The Truth	Trap Type
Rivers disappearing in Terai	“In the Terai belt, rivers disappear due to high porosity.”	Rivers disappear in Bhabar, not Terai. In Terai, they re-emerge.	Almost Correct Statement (90% correct, one swapped term)
Peninsular west-flowing rivers form deltas	“Narmada and Tapi are west-flowing peninsular rivers that have built large deltas.”	West-flowing peninsular rivers form estuaries, not deltas. Only east-flowing ones like Godavari, Krishna form deltas.	Reverse Causation (expecting delta because of sediment load)

🔗 Static-Dynamic Link

In January 2023, the town of Joshimath in Uttarakhand experienced severe land subsidence, cracking hundreds of buildings. This tragedy is a direct consequence of the town being located on the young, fragile Shivalik/Lesser Himalayan rock debris—a classic example of how India’s physical geography impacts current events. The area lies in a seismic zone, and unplanned construction on unstable moraine deposits triggered disaster. Expect questions linking Himalayan geology to such incidents.
