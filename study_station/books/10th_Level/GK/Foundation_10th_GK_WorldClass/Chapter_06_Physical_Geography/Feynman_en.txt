🧒 The Secret of the Vanishing Rivers – Feynman Style

Alright, imagine you're holding a bucket full of water. You pour it onto two different surfaces.

First surface: a big pile of marbles and stones.
What happens? The water just vanishes immediately into the gaps between the marbles. The surface stays dry. You can't see any water flowing on top.

Second surface: a flat, soaked kitchen sponge.
When you pour water on it, the sponge can't hold any more—the water oozes back out and makes a puddle on top.

This is exactly what happens at the foot of the Himalayas, and it's the most confusing trap in exams.

When rivers rush down from the steep mountains, they first hit a narrow belt called Bhabar. The ground here is made of big boulders and pebbles—like our pile of marbles. The rivers hit this and go "bloop"—they sink underground! The water disappears. So Bhabar is dry on the surface.

But that water hasn't gone forever. It keeps flowing slowly underground, like a secret tunnel. A little further south, the land changes. The ground becomes fine clay and silt—like our soaked sponge. The underground water gets squeezed up, and rivers re-emerge as springs and swamps. This wet, soggy belt is the Terai. It's full of marshes, tall grass, and tigers.

So:

Bhabar = rivers say bye-bye to the surface.

Terai = rivers reappear there again.

The unforgettable trick:
"Bhabar mein bhaag gayi nadi, Terai mein phir se dikhi." (In Bhabar the river ran away, in Terai it showed up again.)

Examiners love to swap this: they write "In Terai, rivers disappear due to high porosity"—and you catch them because you know the sponge and marbles story.
