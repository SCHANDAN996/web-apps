🌄 India's Land, Chapter by Chapter: Physical Geography of India

Pour a bucket of water on a heap of pebbles and it vanishes. Pour it on a soaked sponge and it comes back up as a puddle. That simple picture explains one of the most-asked traps of this chapter — why streams disappear in the Bhabar and reappear in the Terai. Physical geography is full of such "why" stories. Learn the story and the facts stay with you.

1. The Big Picture: Old Plateau, Young Mountains

India has practically all major physical features of the earth — mountains, plains, deserts, plateaus and islands.

The Peninsular Plateau is one of the oldest and most stable landmasses on the earth. It is made of old crystalline, igneous and metamorphic rocks and was formed when the Gondwana land broke and drifted.

The Himalayas and the Northern Plains are the most recent landforms. The Himalayas are young fold mountains, an unstable zone with high peaks, deep valleys and fast-flowing rivers. They rose when the northward-moving Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate.

The Northern Plains are made of alluvial deposits brought down by rivers.

3. The Himalayas: Three Parallel Ranges

The Himalayas run west–east from the Indus to the Brahmaputra in an arc of about 2,400 km. Their width is about 400 km in Kashmir and 150 km in Arunachal Pradesh.

Range	Other Name	Height	Key Features
Great Himalaya	Himadri (Inner)	Average 6,000 m	Northernmost and most continuous; loftiest peaks; core of granite; perennially snow-bound; glaciers
Lesser Himalaya	Himachal	3,700–4,500 m	Most rugged; Pir Panjal (longest and most important), Dhaula Dhar, Mahabharat ranges; Kashmir, Kangra and Kullu valleys; hill stations
Outer Himalaya	Shiwaliks	900–1,100 m	Outermost; 10–50 km wide; unconsolidated sediments brought by rivers

Duns are longitudinal valleys between the Lesser Himalaya and the Shiwaliks — Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun.

Divisions from west to east (marked by river valleys):

Punjab Himalaya — between the Indus and the Satluj (Kashmir and Himachal Himalaya)

Kumaon Himalaya — between the Satluj and the Kali

Nepal Himalaya — between the Kali and the Teesta

Assam Himalaya — between the Teesta and the Dihang

Beyond the Dihang gorge the mountains bend south along India's eastern border as the Purvachal — the Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo hills — mostly made of strong sandstones (sedimentary rocks).

Exam Trap Alert: "Outermost" is not "oldest". The Shiwaliks are the outermost range, made of loose river sediments; they are not the oldest range.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Name the valley type found between the Lesser Himalaya and the Shiwaliks. (Duns.)

4. The Northern Plains

Formed by the Indus, the Ganga and the Brahmaputra with their tributaries, the plain covers about 7 lakh sq km; it is about 2,400 km long and 240–320 km broad. It is densely populated and agriculturally productive.

Three sections:

Punjab Plains (west) — formed by the Indus and its tributaries; larger part in Pakistan; dominated by doabs ('do' = two, 'ab' = water).

Ganga Plain — between the Ghaggar and the Teesta.

Brahmaputra Plain — in Assam. Majuli, in the Brahmaputra, is the largest inhabited riverine island in the world.

Four relief belts (from the Shiwalik foothills southwards):

Bhabar — a narrow belt of pebbles, about 8–16 km wide, parallel to the Shiwalik slopes. All streams disappear here.

Terai — south of the Bhabar; streams re-emerge and make a wet, swampy, marshy region. Once thickly forested and full of wildlife (Dudhwa National Park lies here).

Bhangar — the largest part of the plain; older alluvium lying above the floodplains like terraces; contains calcareous deposits called kankar.

Khadar — newer alluvium of the floodplains, renewed almost every year, fertile and ideal for intensive agriculture.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Where do streams disappear and where do they re-emerge? (Disappear in Bhabar; re-emerge in Terai.)

5. The Peninsular Plateau

A tableland with broad, shallow valleys and rounded hills. Two divisions:

Central Highlands — north of the Narmada; cover a major area of the Malwa plateau; bounded by the Aravalis on the north-west. Wider in the west, narrower in the east; eastward extensions are Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand. The Chotanagpur plateau, drained by the Damodar, marks the further eastward extension. Rivers here (Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken) flow from south-west to north-east.

Deccan Plateau — a triangular landmass south of the Narmada; the Satpura range flanks its broad base in the north; the Mahadev, Kaimur and Maikal ranges are its eastern extensions. It is higher in the west and slopes gently eastwards. Its north-eastern extension is the Meghalaya, Karbi-Anglong plateau and North Cachar hills, separated from the Chotanagpur plateau by a fault (the Malda fault). The Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills run from west to east.

Western Ghats vs Eastern Ghats:

Feature	Western Ghats	Eastern Ghats
Continuity	Continuous; crossed only through passes (Thal, Bhor, Pal Ghats)	Discontinuous, irregular, cut by rivers flowing to the Bay of Bengal
Average height	900–1,600 m; height increases from north to south	About 600 m
Highest peaks	Anai Mudi (2,695 m), Doda Betta (2,637 m)	Mahendragiri (1,501 m)
Local names	Sahyadri (Maharashtra), Nilgiri, Anaimalai, Cardamom hills	Stretch from the Mahanadi valley to the Nilgiris
Rain	Cause orographic rain on their western slopes	—

The Shevroy and Javadi hills lie to the south-east of the Eastern Ghats. Ooty (Udagamandalam) and Kodaikanal are well-known hill stations.

Deccan Trap: the black soil area of the plateau, of volcanic origin; its igneous rocks have denuded to form black soil.

Aravali Hills: on the western and north-western margins of the plateau; highly eroded, broken hills extending from Gujarat to Delhi in a south-west to north-east direction. Guru Shikhar (Mount Abu) is the highest peak.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which peak is the highest of the Eastern Ghats? (Mahendragiri.)

6. The Indian Desert

Lies towards the western margins of the Aravali hills. An undulating sandy plain with sand dunes; rainfall below 150 mm per year; arid climate with little vegetation. Streams appear in the rainy season and soon disappear into the sand. Barchans (crescent-shaped dunes) cover large areas; longitudinal dunes are more prominent near the India–Pakistan border. The Luni is the only large river; it originates near Pushkar and ends in the Rann of Kachchh.

7. Coastal Plains and Islands

Western coastal plain — narrow, between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea; a submerged coast. North to south: Konkan (Mumbai–Goa), Kannad Plain, Malabar coast. The Kerala coast has Kayals (backwaters) such as Vembanad.

Eastern coastal plain — wide and level, along the Bay of Bengal; an emergent coast. North part: Northern Circar; south part: Coromandel Coast. The Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri form extensive deltas here. Chilika Lake (Odisha), south of the Mahanadi delta, is the largest salt water lake in India.

Islands:

Andaman and Nicobar (Bay of Bengal) — bigger, more numerous; believed to be an elevated portion of submarine mountains; India's only active volcano is on Barren Island; Saddle Peak (North Andaman) is the highest peak.

Lakshadweep (Arabian Sea, near the Malabar coast) — small coral islands; earlier called Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindive; named Lakshadweep in 1973; Kavaratti is the administrative headquarters; Pitti island (uninhabited) has a bird sanctuary.

Channels (exam favourite):

10° Channel — Andaman ↔ Nicobar

9° Channel — divides the Lakshadweep group; Minicoy lies to its south

8° Channel — Minicoy ↔ Maldives

Duncan Passage — South Andaman ↔ Little Andaman

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which channel lies between Minicoy and the Maldives? (8° Channel.)

8. Drainage: Himalayan vs Peninsular Rivers

Feature	Himalayan Rivers	Peninsular Rivers
Water	Rain + melting snow; mostly perennial	Rain only; many are seasonal
Course	Long; cut gorges; heavy erosion and silt	Shorter and shallower
Mouth	Well-developed deltas	East-flowing: deltas; Narmada and Tapi (west-flowing): estuaries
Examples	Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra	Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Narmada, Tapi

Indus: rises in Tibet near Lake Mansarovar, enters India in Ladakh; the Satluj, Beas, Ravi, Chenab and Jhelum join it near Mithankot in Pakistan; total length 2,900 km. Under the Indus Water Treaty (1960), India can use only 20 per cent of its water.

Ganga: the Bhagirathi (fed by the Gangotri Glacier) meets the Alaknanda at Devprayag; the Ganga enters the plains at Haridwar. The Yamuna (from Yamunotri Glacier) joins it at Prayagraj (Allahabad). The Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi come from the Nepal Himalaya; the Chambal, Betwa and Son from the peninsular uplands. At Farakka the river bifurcates — the Bhagirathi-Hooghly flows south through West Bengal. Length: over 2,500 km. Ambala lies on the water divide between the Indus and Ganga systems.

Brahmaputra: rises in Tibet east of Mansarovar (called Tsang Po there), takes a 'U' turn at Namcha Barwa, enters Arunachal Pradesh as the Dihang, and is called the Jamuna in Bangladesh. The Ganga–Brahmaputra waters form the Sundarban Delta, named after the Sundari tree.

Peninsular rivers: the Western Ghats form the main water divide.

Narmada — rises in the Amarkantak hills (MP); flows west in a rift valley; Marble rocks and Dhuadhar falls near Jabalpur.

Tapi — rises in the Satpura ranges, Betul district (MP); flows in a rift valley parallel to the Narmada.

Godavari — largest Peninsular river (about 1,500 km); rises in Nasik district (Maharashtra); largest Peninsular basin; called Dakshin Ganga.

Mahanadi — rises in the highlands of Chhattisgarh; about 860 km.

Krishna — rises near Mahabaleshwar; about 1,400 km; tributaries Tungabhadra, Koyna, Ghatprabha, Musi, Bhima.

Kaveri — rises in the Brahmagiri range; about 760 km; makes the Shivasamudram Falls.

Lakes: Wular (J&K) — largest freshwater lake, tectonic origin. Sambhar (Rajasthan) — salt water, used for salt. Chilika, Pulicat, Kolleru — lagoons formed by spits and bars.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which makes a delta — the Godavari or the Narmada? (Godavari.)

9. Climate and Soils

Seasons: cold weather season, hot weather season, advancing monsoon (rainy season) and retreating monsoon.

Loo — strong, gusty, hot, dry day-time winds over north and north-west India in summer.

Kaal Baisakhi — violent evening thunderstorms of West Bengal in summer.

Mango showers — pre-monsoon showers in Kerala and Karnataka that help mangoes ripen early.

Burst of the monsoon — the sudden, continuous rain for several days at its onset. The monsoon reaches the southern tip of the peninsula generally by the first week of June.

Mawsynram, in the southern ranges of the Khasi Hills, receives the highest average rainfall in the world.

October heat — high temperature with humidity after the monsoon retreats.

Western disturbances — winter low-pressure systems from the Mediterranean Sea and western Asia; they bring winter rain (mahawat) to the plains, important for rabi crops.

The Tamil Nadu coast gets a large portion of its rain in October and November.

Soils:

Alluvial — most widespread; Bangar (old) has more kankar; Khadar (new) is finer and more fertile.

Black (regur) — Deccan Trap (basalt) region; very fine clayey; holds moisture; rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime; poor in phosphoric content; deep cracks in hot weather; ideal for cotton.

Red and yellow — on crystalline igneous rocks in low-rainfall parts of the eastern and southern Deccan; red due to diffusion of iron; yellow when hydrated.

Laterite — from Latin 'later' (brick); intense leaching by heavy rain; with soil conservation useful for tea and coffee in the hills of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

Arid — sandy, saline; kankar in lower layers because calcium increases downwards.

Bad land in the Chambal basin, cut by deep gullies, is called ravines.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Which soil is called regur, and which crop suits it? (Black soil; cotton.)
