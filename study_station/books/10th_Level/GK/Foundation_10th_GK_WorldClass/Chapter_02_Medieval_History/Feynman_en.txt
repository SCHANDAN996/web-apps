Din-i-Ilahi — the name sounds grand, but what exactly was it? Let’s clear the fog.

Imagine your school principal starts a special club called the “Circle of Light.” The rules are simple: always tell the truth, respect all faiths, help the poor. Only a few close friends join. There is no new holy book, no new temple, no priests, and no mass conversion of ordinary people. That is very close to what Akbar did in 1582 when he promulgated Din-i-Ilahi (Divine Faith).

Now here is the key point students get wrong in both directions. NCERT textbooks — the main source for SSC, Railway and Bank exams — describe Din-i-Ilahi as a **new religion** (or new faith) introduced by Akbar in 1582. So if an exam option says “Akbar started a new religion called Din-i-Ilahi,” that is the correct statement. But the exam trap works the other way too: questions try to make you believe it spread widely or replaced Islam. It did not. Din-i-Ilahi had no scripture, no places of worship, no priesthood, and only a small circle of followers — courtiers like Birbal were among the few who accepted it. It never became a mass movement and faded away after Akbar’s death in 1605.

So remember it like this: **a new religion by name, but a tiny moral circle in practice.** It grew out of the discussions at the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) at Fatehpur Sikri, where Akbar invited scholars of many religions — and it reflected his policy of sulh-i-kul (universal peace).

Unforgettable Trick: Picture Akbar holding a small, thin book labelled “Din-i-Ilahi, 1582” with a sign: “New faith — members: only a few.” New religion? Yes (that’s what NCERT says). Mass following, holy book, temples? No, no, no.
