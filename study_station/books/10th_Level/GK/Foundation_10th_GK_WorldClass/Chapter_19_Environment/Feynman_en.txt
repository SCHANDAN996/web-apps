🧒 Feynman Corner: Ozone hole and global warming — are they the same problem?

This is the fact that confuses students most in this chapter. Both are about gases in the sky, both are "bad news", and CFCs appear in both stories. So many students write "the ozone hole causes global warming" — and lose the mark. Let's make it simple.

The daily-life example: an umbrella and a blanket

Imagine you are lying in the sun on a summer afternoon. You have two things with you: an umbrella and a blanket.

The umbrella is the ozone layer. It sits high up (in the stratosphere) and blocks the harmful ultraviolet (UV) rays of the Sun, the rays that burn skin and can cause skin cancer and cataract. When CFCs from old refrigerators and sprays reach that height, sunlight breaks them and sets chlorine free. Chlorine pokes holes in the umbrella. Now more UV comes in. That is ozone depletion — a torn umbrella. The biggest tear is over Antarctica.

The blanket is the layer of greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapour. The warm Earth gives out heat, and the blanket keeps some of it in. A thin blanket is good — without it the Earth would be freezing. But when we burn coal, petrol and diesel, we keep adding layers. A blanket that is too thick makes us sweat. That is global warming — a blanket that is too thick.

So: torn umbrella → more UV gets in. Thick blanket → more heat stays in. Two different problems, two different fixes.

Where do CFCs fit? CFCs do both bad things: they tear the umbrella and they also add to the blanket. That is why they appear in both stories. But the main cause of global warming is carbon dioxide.

And the treaties follow the same split: the umbrella has the Montreal Protocol (1987); the blanket has the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015).

The unforgettable trick

"Umbrella torn — UV is born. Blanket thick — Earth gets sick."
Umbrella = ozone layer (Montreal). Blanket = greenhouse gases (Kyoto, Paris).

Blurting Sheet

Now, without looking up, write down everything you remember about this concept: where the ozone layer is, what it blocks, which chemical tears it and which atom does the damage, where the "hole" is biggest, the unit used to measure ozone, the main greenhouse gases, which gas contributes most to warming, two effects of global warming, and which treaty belongs to which problem.

Then check: Did you write "stratosphere"? Did you write "chlorine"? Did you put Montreal with ozone and Kyoto with greenhouse gases?

Question for you: Ozone near the ground is called a pollutant, yet ozone high up is called a protector. Using the umbrella idea, why is the same gas good in one place and bad in the other?
