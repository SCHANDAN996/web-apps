🧒 Feynman Corner: Why is a "light-year" a unit of distance, not time?

This is the fact that trips students most in this chapter. The word "year" shouts TIME, so the brain jumps to the wrong answer. Let's make it simple.

The daily-life example: "My school is 10 minutes away"

When a friend asks how far your school is, you may say, "It's 10 minutes away." You have not given a time — you have given a distance, described by how long it takes to cover it at your usual walking speed. Everyone understands that "10 minutes away" means "the distance I walk in 10 minutes."

A light-year works exactly the same way. Light is the fastest thing we know — about 3 lakh kilometres every second (3 × 10^8 m/s). A light-year is the distance light covers in one whole year. That is about 9.46 × 10^15 metres — roughly 9.46 lakh crore kilometres. So when someone says "that star is 4 light-years away", they are saying "light, the fastest walker of all, would need 4 years to walk there." It is a distance.

Why not simply use kilometres?

Because the numbers become too big to handle. Saying "the star is about 4 light-years away" is much easier than writing a number with more than a dozen digits. In the same way, we say "Delhi to Agra is about 3 hours by car" instead of counting every metre.

Two cousins of the light-year

Parsec — another, bigger unit of distance used by astronomers.
Angstrom — the opposite end: a very tiny length (10^-10 m), used for things like wavelengths.
Both are units of LENGTH, just like the light-year.

The unforgettable trick

"Light-year has 'year' in its name, but it walks a road, not a clock."
Whenever a unit has "light" in front, think of a distance that light walks.
