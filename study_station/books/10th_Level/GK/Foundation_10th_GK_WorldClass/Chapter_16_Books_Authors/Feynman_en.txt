🧒 Feynman Corner: "Written BY him" or "written ABOUT him"? — Harshacharita, Akbarnama and Baburnama

This is the fact that confuses students most in this chapter. The book is called "Harsha-charita", so surely Harsha wrote it? The book is called "Akbar-nama", so surely Akbar wrote it? No! But "Babur-nama" really was written by Babur. How do we keep this straight?

The daily-life example: your school report card

Your report card has your name printed on top in big letters. But did you write it? No — your class teacher wrote it about you. Now think of your personal diary. It also has your name on it, and this time you did write it yourself.

A book with a king's name in its title can be either of these two things:

A report card — someone else writes about the king. Harshacharita ("the deeds of Harsha") was written by Banabhatta, the court poet. Akbarnama ("the book of Akbar") was written by Abul Fazl, Akbar's friend and minister. The Ain-i-Akbari, its third volume, is also by Abul Fazl. Padshahnama ("the book of the emperor", Shah Jahan) was written by Abdul Hamid Lahori. Humayun-nama was written by Gulbadan Begum, Humayun's sister.

A diary — the king writes about himself. Baburnama was written by Babur himself, in Turkish. Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri was written by Jahangir himself.

And one twist: King Harsha did write books too — but they are plays, not about himself: Ratnavali, Nagananda and Priyadarshika.

The unforgettable trick

"Only B and J kept a diary." — Among the books in this chapter, Babur and Jahangir wrote their own life story. Every other "-nama" or "-charita" here with a king's name was written by a court writer.

So when you see a king's name in a book title, ask: is this his diary or his report card? If the king is not Babur or Jahangir, look first for the court writer.
