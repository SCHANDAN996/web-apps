Memory Boosters & Skip Strategies

🎩 Outrageous Mnemonics for Alphabet Questions

1. EJOTY – The Forward Anchors
"Elephants Jump Over Three Yaks"
Imagine five giant animals standing in a line: an Elephant (E=5), a Jumping Jaguar (J=10), an Owl (O=15), a Tiger (T=20), and a Yak (Y=25). Their weight in tons equals their positions! To find R, just think "Owl (15) plus 3 snacks makes R (18)."

2. 27 – The Magic Kiss
"A and Z are lovers. Every night at 9:00 PM (21:00), they kiss exactly 27 times."
Whenever you need an opposite letter, remember the magic kiss number 27. Opposite letter = 27 – position. So A (1) + Z (26) = 27, B (2) + Y (25) = 27... it’s a perfect marriage. Picture the alphabet folding like a love letter, A meeting Z.

3. Reverse Alphabet Anchors – VQLGB
"Very Quiet Lions Growl Boldly" (V=5, Q=10, L=15, G=20, B=25 in reverse counting)
To memorise, imagine a silent lion (Very Quiet Lion) in a library, and then it suddenly growls loudly (Boldly) at a Giraffe. The scene is so funny that you'll never forget the sequence: V, Q, L, G, B. Reverse positions are anchored here, just like EJOTY for forward.

4. Series Pattern – The Ladder Walk
"The drunk man climbs a ladder, sometimes 2 steps, sometimes 3, sometimes more."
When you see A, C, F, J, ?, picture a man who first steps 2 rungs (A to C), then 3 (C to F), then 4 (F to J). Next he'll step 5, so he'll reach the 15th rung (O). Always check if his step size is increasing, decreasing, or constant – the ladder reveals all.

5. Opposite Pair Pockets
"Every Villain Quietly Laughs; Good Boys Yell."
E↔V, J↔Q, O↔L, T↔G, Y↔B. Visualise a villain (V) chasing an elf (E) – they're opposites. A queen (Q) and a jack (J) are card opposites. An owl (O) and a lion (L) are forest rivals. A tiger (T) and a giraffe (G) are zoo neighbours. A yak (Y) and a bee (B) – one big, one small. These five opposite pairs are your pocket rockets; memorise them as if they are Pokémon cards.

6. Coding-Decoding – The Kitchen Rule
"First chop, then fry, then serve – the order is everything."
If the code is opposite then +2, imagine you take a letter (raw vegetable), first you flip it (opposite), then add 2 spices (shift). To decode, reverse the order: first remove 2 spices, then flip back. Like un-frying an egg – impossible if you mess the order. So always write the operation sequence and reverse it for decoding.

7. Analogy – The Copycat Monkey
"A monkey sees, monkey does – exactly the same moves."
In letter analogy (ABC : DEF :: GHI : ?), the monkey watches the first pair’s dance (A moves +3 to D, B moves +3 to E, C moves +3 to F). Then it imitates the exact same dance for the second pair (G+3=J, H+3=K, I+3=L). Never let the monkey invent new steps; it must copy exactly.

8. Classification – The Odd Sock Drawer
"In a drawer full of striped socks, one is polka-dotted – that's the odd one."
When finding the odd letter out, always look for the most absurd difference: vowel vs consonant, even position vs odd, prime vs composite. If you see A, E, I, O, and T, T is the only consonant – it's the polka-dot sock! Don't get distracted by positions; check simple categories first.

9. Half-Reversal – The Book Fold
"If you fold a book, the first half goes upside down."
When the first half of the alphabet is reversed, imagine taking pages A to M, flipping them, so M becomes first, then L, K, J... until A. The second half (N to Z) stays right-side up. Picture a teacher folding a test paper – the top half is inverted. To answer arrangement questions, quickly sketch this folded line and count.

10. Gap Series – The Stretching Rubber Band
"A rubber band stretches by 2 cm, then 3 cm, then 4 cm... soon it snaps."
In series like AC, EG, IK, ? the gap between letters is constant (+2 in positions). But if gaps increase (A, C, F, J), it's like a rubber band being pulled unevenly. Always measure the stretch – if it's uniform, it's a simple band; if it accelerates, the band will snap into a predictable pattern. Use finger counting.

11. The 27-Second Magic Trick
"Imagine a magician shouting '27!' and the alphabet instantly flips."
Whenever you forget the opposite of a letter, quickly subtract its position from 27. M (13) → 27-13=14=N. It's like the magician pulling a rabbit: 27 is the hat, and the difference is the rabbit. Practice until you can do it in under 2 seconds – it's pure speed magic.

12. Mixed Series – The Salad
"A mixed letter-number series is like a fruit salad – separate the apples from the oranges."
A2, C4, E6, ? – Don't eat it all at once! Pick out the letters (A, C, E → +2 → G) and numbers (2, 4, 6 → +2 → 8), then combine (G8). If you mix them, you'll choke. Always handle letters and numbers in separate mental bowls.

13. Double-Layer Trap – The Unpeeling Onion
"Multi-layer coding is an onion; cry if you must, but peel one layer at a time."
If the code is first opposite, then +1, then reverse, imagine three layers of onion skin. To decode, you must peel backwards: reverse first, then -1, then opposite. Write the layers on your finger – peeling in wrong order makes you cry more. So stay calm and unpeel systematically.

14. The Bouncing Ball Trick (for letter pairs)
"Think of a ball bouncing between two letters: if it bounces equally, they are a pair."
In "how many such pairs in a word?" questions, imagine a rubber ball hopping from one letter to another in the alphabet, counting bounces. If the number of bounces (letters between) matches the number of letters between them in the word, it's a valid pair. Visualise the ball bouncing to keep count.

⚡ Skip Strategy – When to Abandon a Question

The 15-Second Gut Check: If you cannot identify the pattern (series, code, analogy) within 15 seconds, mark it, skip it, and move on. Alphabet questions often have hidden traps, and staring longer usually won't crack them – you'll just waste time.

The 45-Second Execution Limit: Even if you spot the pattern, if solving (writing positions, counting, checking all options) takes more than 45 seconds, skip and return later. Some coding-decoding questions with multi-step operations are designed to consume time. Attempt them only if you have a clear mind and spare time at the end.

Skip Multi-Layer Coding on Sight: If a coding question involves more than two operations (e.g., opposite, then shift, then reverse) or a word longer than 5 letters, it's a potential time sink. Skip immediately; these are the examiner's traps to slow you down. Only attempt if you have a solid plan and extra minutes.
