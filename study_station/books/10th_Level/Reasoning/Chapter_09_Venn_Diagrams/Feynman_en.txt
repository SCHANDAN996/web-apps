🧒 Feynman Technique: The Venn Diagram Detective

Let me tell you a story that will make you see Venn diagrams like a real detective.

Imagine your school is having a grand picnic. Every kid can join three clubs: Soccer, Art, and Music. The teacher gives you two clue-cards:

Card 1: All Soccer kids are also in Art.

Card 2: Some Art kids are in Music.

Your friend whispers, “So, some Soccer kids must be in Music, right?” You are the detective. You must find out if that is definitely true, or just a guess.

You take a paper and draw three circles that can overlap. Card 1 forces you to put the whole Soccer circle inside the Art circle. Good. Card 2 says Art and Music must share some part — you mark a star in the overlap and imagine a kid sitting there who does both.

Now where can the Soccer circle sit inside the big Art circle? It could sit in the part that does not touch Music at all. The star-kid is somewhere in the overlap, but Soccer kids are free to stay far away from that star. So your friend's conclusion “some Soccer kids must be in Music” is not forced. The detective says: “Maybe, but not definitely.”

That is the hardest idea in Venn diagrams: “Some” does not mean a picture with a fixed overlap; it means you cannot erase the possibility of that overlap. When you test a conclusion, you must build the minimum diagram — the one with the least commitments — and see if the conclusion still holds.

Unforgettable Trick: The Chewing-Gum Game

Take a wad of chewing gum (it represents one person). Your job is to stick that gum on the diagram only where the statements force it.

“All A are B” forces the gum to be stuck inside B whenever it’s stuck in A.

“No A is B” builds a wall; gum can never be in both.

“Some A are B” says: stick one gum somewhere in both A and B. But it doesn’t say all A’s are in B. So other gums are free.

To check a conclusion like “Some Soccer are Music,” you try to place your Soccer gum in a way that avoids Music. If you can, the conclusion is not definite. If the statements cage you so that every possible place for Soccer touches Music, then it’s definite.

This trick works for every syllogism question in exams. Be the kid with the gum — place it, move it, and see what is truly forced.
