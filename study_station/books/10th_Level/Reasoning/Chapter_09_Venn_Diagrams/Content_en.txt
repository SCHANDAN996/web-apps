Venn Diagrams: Unlock the Logic Behind the Overlaps
Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Interpret relationships among classes of objects, persons, or ideas.

Choose the correct Venn Diagram for three given words in under 10 seconds.

Count exact numbers from a complex Venn diagram without making silly mistakes.

Apply Venn Diagrams to test Syllogism conclusions reliably.

Identify and avoid the examiner’s favourite traps.

A Hook That Sticks

Imagine you are a detective. Three gangs operate in the city – the Ciphers, the Mask, and the Serpents. Some members belong to only one gang, some to two, and a few dangerous ones are in all three. Your informant gives you a hand‑drawn map of overlapping circles with numbers scribbled inside. Your job? Find out exactly how many criminals are loyal to at least two gangs so that the SWAT team is neither under‑prepared nor over‑stretched. What you are holding is nothing but a Venn Diagram – the simplest and most powerful reasoning tool you will ever learn.

If you can solve the gang‑war puzzle, you can solve any Venn Diagram question that SSC, Banking, Railway, or State SSC (GD/MTS/Police) throws at you.

Why Do Students Stumble?

Most of us first saw Venn Diagrams in primary school as “two circles that overlap”. The confusion begins later when three circles, cryptic words, and statements like “Some A are not B” hit the paper. Suddenly the same tool feels like a maze. The real problem is not the concept; it is that we try to memorise diagrams instead of visualising relationships. This chapter will make you see the logic behind every curve.

Chunk 1: The Three Big Relationships

Whenever you see three words in a Logical Venn Diagram question, they can relate in only three fundamental ways.

Complete Overlap (One inside another)
Example: Animal, Dog, Puppy.
All puppies are dogs, and all dogs are animals. The circles nest like Russian dolls.

Partial Overlap (Intersecting circles)
Example: Athlete, Singer, Doctor.
A person can be any two, or even all three. Circles cut into one another, creating a common centre where the three intersect.

No Overlap (Separate circles)
Example: Table, River, Moon.
These have no logical connection. They sit as three separate islands.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt:
Close your eyes and draw the three types with your finger. Which type best fits Professor, Principal, School?

Chunk 2: The Magic of “Some” and “Only”

Only: means “exactly this much and nothing more.” In a diagram, “only A” is the part of the A circle that does not touch any other circle.

At least two: includes people who belong to exactly two plus those who belong to all three.

At most two: excludes the central triangular zone (the “all three” region).

✅ Trick to remember:
Think of a pizza with three toppings.

“Only pepperoni” – pepperoni and nothing else.

“At least two toppings” – double cheese + pepperoni, triple delight everything.

“At most two toppings” – you never got the triple‑layer slice.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt:
If a Venn diagram has three circles labelled A, B, C, how many total regions are there? (Answer: 7 regions – one for each single, three for pairs, and one for the triple intersection.)

Hindi only = 5

English only = (the English circle’s portion that doesn’t touch any other) – here not labelled, but you get the logic.

Hindi & English only (but not Sanskrit) = 7

All three = 3

🧠 Active Recall Prompt:
Which number represents a person who speaks Hindi and Sanskrit but not English? (It would be the intersection of Hindi and Sanskrit minus the triple zone.)

Chunk 4: Syllogism Simplified with Venn Diagrams

Statement 1: All cats are mammals.
Statement 2: Some mammals are wild.

We draw:

Cat circle completely inside Mammal circle.

Another circle ‘Wild’ overlapping the Mammal circle partially. The Cat circle may or may not touch Wild. That uncertainty is your weapon.

Conclusion: “Some cats are wild.” Does it follow? Not necessarily – the cat circle could be entirely outside the wild region. So the conclusion is false.

Mnemonics for syllogisms:

SOME = Maybe All → When you hear “Some”, never lock a partial overlap; possibility of “All” is always there unless another statement forbids it.

NO = Permanent Wall → “No A is B” means circles never meet; draw them far apart.

ALL = In‑Out Rule → “All A are B” puts A fully inside B.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt:
If “All pens are books” and “No book is a table”, can “Some pens are tables” be true? (Think using the In‑Out Rule + Permanent Wall.)

Chunk 5: The 15‑Second Word‑to‑Diagram Trick

When faced with a “pick the correct Venn” question, do not start drawing. Mentally ask:

Is one word a subset of another? → Nested circles.

Are all three completely independent? → Three separate circles.

Is there at least one possible intersection? → Overlapping circles.

Use the PIG method – Part, Intersect, Gap.

Part: check subset relation.

Intersect: check if any two can share members.

Gap: check if any pair must be separate.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt:
Words: Doctor, Surgeon, Lawyer. Apply PIG: Surgeon is part of Doctor (subset). Lawyer can intersect with Doctor. So answer is a big circle “Doctor” containing “Surgeon” and partially overlapping with “Lawyer.” Visualise it.

⚠️ Caution: Examiner’s Trap

Trap 1: The “Some Not” Switcheroo
Statement: “Some students are not athletes.”
Students often wrongly conclude “Therefore, some students are athletes.” This is a classic trap. “Some not” simply means there exists at least one student outside the athlete circle; it says nothing about the existence of a student inside it. Always verify both ways.

Trap 2: Hidden Double Relationships
Question: Paper, Book, Printer.
Many assume “Printer” is a machine that prints books, so it must overlap with Book. But a Printer is not a type of Book or Paper; it is a separate device. The correct diagram: Paper and Book (paper is part of book) show subset, while Printer is an independent circle. The examiner plays on your worldly association, not the class logic.

Trap 3: Too‑Tempting Triple Overlap
In counting questions, the phrase “at least two” includes the central “all three” region. Options will have one number that excludes the centre and another that includes it. Students in a hurry pick the wrong one. Underline the words “at least” or “only” before you start adding numbers.

When you see these traps, smile. Because now you know exactly what the examiner doesn’t want you to know.
