🧒 Feynman Technique: The Mystery of the Red Marbles

Imagine you have a best friend, Aryan. One day, Aryan puts a big, covered box on the table and says, "I put some marbles in this box. And here's a true fact: Some of the marbles are red. That's all I'm telling you."

Now, he asks you a question: "Can you be absolutely, 100% sure that some marbles in the box are NOT red?"

You think for a moment. Your brain wants to shout, "Well, if some are red, then the others must be something else, so yes, some are not red!" But hold on — is that really true? What if Aryan secretly put only red marbles in the box? He said "some marbles are red." If all marbles are red, is it still true that "some are red"? Yes! Because "some" means at least one, but it doesn't say anything about the rest. It could be all, or it could be half. So you can't be sure that some are NOT red. That's the hardest, most sneaky trap in Statement & Conclusion.

Let's see another example. Aryan says, "Some students in class have a pet dog." Can you conclude that "Some students in class do not have a pet dog"? Nope. Maybe every single student has a dog, and then "some have a pet dog" is still perfectly true. So the conclusion "some do not have a dog" is not guaranteed. The examiner loves to make you fall into this ditch.

The Unforgettable Trick: Robot Brain 🧠🤖
Whenever you see a statement, turn yourself into a robot. A robot only knows what is typed into its memory. It cannot guess, imagine, or bring in things from the real world. So, if the robot reads "Some marbles are red," its internal screen only shows: "At least one red marble exists. All? Don't know. None? Not possible." So, when asked, "Are some marbles not red?" the robot replies: "I don't have that data. Cannot be determined." That's the only safe answer.

How to use this in exams:

Read the statement like a robot. Delete everything you know about the real world.

Ask: "If this statement is true, is this conclusion always, forcedly true?" If there's even one imaginary situation where the statement is true but the conclusion fails, then the conclusion is wrong.

For "some" statements, never jump to "some not". It's the Golden Trap.
