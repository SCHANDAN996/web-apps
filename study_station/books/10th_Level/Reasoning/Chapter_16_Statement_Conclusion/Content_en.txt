# Statement & Conclusion (Basic)

## Chapter Outline & Learning Objectives

In this chapter, you will learn:
- The fundamental meaning of Statement and Conclusion without confusion.
- The "Minimum Brain / Robot Brain" rule for reasoning.
- How to determine whether a conclusion is definitely true or merely possible.
- The exam-ready mindset to solve questions rapidly.
- How to identify and avoid the examiner's most dangerous traps.

Imagine wearing a pair of magical glasses that only show you what is explicitly written in front of you—nothing more, nothing less. This chapter equips you with that exact analytical lens.

Consider this question:
- **Statement:** "All pencils are books."
- **Conclusion:** "Some books are pencils."
Does this conclusion follow? If you said YES, excellent! If you felt even a slight hesitation, this chapter will dissolve that hesitation forever.

Statement and conclusion questions appear deceptive and simple, but this is precisely where students stumble. Why? The human brain naturally mixes real-world common sense with statements. Today, we will learn how to isolate given statements completely.

---

## Every Student's Dilemma
Most candidates get trapped in two pitfalls:
1. **Injecting External Knowledge:** Assuming facts that are true in the real world must apply to the statement.
2. **Confusing "Possible" with "Definite":** Assuming something that might happen is guaranteed to happen.

**Good News:** Starting today, you will think strictly like a robot. A robot possesses only the data fed into its memory—it has zero external real-world knowledge.

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** Without reading further: From the statement "Some clouds are white", can we say with 100% certainty that "Some clouds are NOT white"? (Yes / No / Cannot say)

## The Core Rule of Statement & Conclusion

- **Statement:** A piece of information that we accept as 100% true without questioning or debate.
- **Conclusion:** A proposition that directly, logically, and necessarily follows from the given statement without requiring any imaginative leaps.

### The Golden Formula:
> *"Accept a conclusion ONLY if the statement makes it impossible for it to be false."*

At the basic level, the rule is even simpler: If a conclusion forces you to step even one millimeter outside the boundary of the statement, it is **INVALID**.

---

## Chunk 1: The Three Fundamental Relationships
At the foundation level, questions revolve around three central relationships:

1. **All A are B (Universal Affirmative):**
   - Example: All pencils are stationery.
   - Deduction: "Some B are A" always follows.
   - Trap: "All B are A" does NOT necessarily follow.

2. **Some A are B (Particular Affirmative):**
   - Example: Some flowers are red.
   - Deduction: "Some B are A" always follows.
   - Trap: "Some A are not B" does NOT definitely follow.

3. **No A is B (Universal Negative):**
   - Example: No dog is a cat.
   - Deduction: "No B is A" always follows.
   - Trap: Any positive connection between A and B is completely FALSE.

### Core Logic:
- "Some" is always hidden inside "All".
- "Some" allows for the possibility of "All", but does not guarantee it.
- "No" completely separates the two sets.

> 🧠 **Active Recall:** From "All water is liquid", which conclusion definitely follows: "Some liquid is water" or "All liquid is water"?

## Chunk 2: Venn Diagrams — The Two-Circle Logic
You don't need elaborate drawings in your rough sheet. Simply visualize two overlapping or nested circles:
- Does the conclusion hold true in **every possible diagram**?
- If there is even **one valid scenario** where the conclusion fails, reject it immediately.

### Example:
- **Statement:** All crows are birds.
- **Conclusion I:** Some birds are crows. ✅ (Follows because "Some" is part of "All")
- **Conclusion II:** All birds are crows. ❌ (Invalid: Other birds like peacocks also exist)

### Rules of Conversion:
- **"All A are B"** converts to **"Some B are A"** (Always valid)
- **"Some A are B"** converts to **"Some B are A"** (Always valid)
- **"No A is B"** converts to **"No B is A"** (Always valid)
- **CRITICAL:** "Some A are B" does NOT imply "Some A are not B".

---

## Chunk 3: The Exam Strategy — The T.E.A. Test
Whenever you evaluate a conclusion, apply the **T.E.A. Test**:
- **T – True as per statement only:** Does the statement directly justify it?
- **E – Eliminate outside knowledge:** Discard real-world knowledge and personal biases.
- **A – Avoid "Some not" from "Some":** Never jump from "Some are" to "Some are not".

### Memory Hook: The Yellow Elephant 🐘
Think of a yellow elephant who only looks at the patch of grass directly beneath his feet and ignores the rest of the jungle. Similarly, an exam candidate must only focus on the statement in front of them, shutting out the rest of the world.

## ⚠️ Examiner's Traps

### Trap 1: The "Some Not" Allurement
The examiner will tempt you to conclude "Some A are not B" from "Some A are B". In everyday language, people assume "some" means "not all". But in formal logic, "some" means "at least one", and it may very well be all. Hence, "Some not" is never certain.

### Trap 2: Vocabulary Overload
Simple logical relations are often masked with complex, intimidating phrasing—such as "individuals who are not offenders" instead of "honest people". Do not be distracted by heavy vocabulary; simply identify the underlying relation (A ⊂ B).

### Trap 3: Multiple Possible Scenarios
If a conclusion holds true in one arrangement but fails in another conceivable arrangement that satisfies the statement, the conclusion is **FALSE**. A valid conclusion must hold true under all valid arrangements.
