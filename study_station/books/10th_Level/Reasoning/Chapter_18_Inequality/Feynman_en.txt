🧒 Feynman (फेनमैन तकनीक + ब्लर्टिंग शीट)

Imagine you and your friend Rohan are standing against the wall, and someone draws a mark above both your heads. You are told, "Rohan's height is less than or equal to your height." That's a weird statement. It means Rohan could be shorter than you, or exactly the same height. You look at the marks, but they're smudged! You can't see who is taller. Now someone asks:

"Can you prove Rohan is shorter than you?"
— No, because maybe you're both exactly the same.
"Can you prove you are exactly the same height?"
— No, because maybe Rohan is a bit shorter.
You're stuck. Both answers could be true, but neither is guaranteed.

This is the hardest idea in Inequality: the "Either-Or" case. It's like when you know the truth is sitting inside a mystery box with two keys, but you don't know which key opens it. The statement gives you two possibilities, and you cannot pick one as certain. So you say, "Either this, or that."

That's exactly what happens with A ≥ B. The statement is a box containing two toys: one with A > B and one with A = B. The examiner asks, "Which toy is definitely in my hand?" You can't say. But if they ask, "Is one of these two toys definitely in my hand?" you shout, "YES!"

So, the Either-Or trick is simply this: When you see a statement that leaves two possibilities open, and two conclusions each claim one of those possibilities, and neither is forced by other clues, then they are like best friends who walk together. You can't separate them. The answer becomes "Either I or II follows."

Unforgettable trick — "The Smudged Line":
Next time you see A ≥ B, imagine a line drawn between A and B, but it's smudged. You can't tell if it's a > or an = sign. So, if a conclusion says A > B, you say, "Maybe, but the smudge hides the =." If another says A = B, you say, "Maybe, but the smudge hides the >." When both conclusions appear together, you simply say, "The smudge covers exactly these two — so one of them has to be right!" That's your signal for Either-Or.
