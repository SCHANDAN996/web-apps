🧒 Feynman's Way: The Trick That Never Fails

Imagine you are trying to open a stubborn lock. You have a bunch of keys that look almost the same. You try one—it doesn’t turn. Another—almost. Frustrating, right? Analogy questions in exams feel exactly like that. The options look so similar, your brain starts spinning. But what if I told you that you don’t need to try every key randomly? You just need to look at the shape of the keyhole first.

That’s what Richard Feynman would have done. He’d say, “Stop memorising words. Start seeing the shape of the relationship.”

🍎 A Story About Apples and Oranges

Little Riya wanted to win the “Perfect Match” game in her class. The teacher held up two pictures: an apple and an orange. Then she showed a rose and asked, “Which one goes with the rose? A mango or a sunflower?”

Riya’s friends shouted “mango” because mango and apple are both fruits. But Riya paused. She thought: What’s the real connection between apple and orange? Both are fruits, yes. But the teacher had paired apple and orange, not apple and banana. Why? Because apple and orange are both round and grow on trees? That’s not unique. Then it struck her—the teacher was showing things that come in different colours but belong to the same family. Apple (red/green) and orange (orange) are both fruits, but the rose is not a fruit. Wait, that’s confusing.

Let’s try again with the classic analogy: Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ? The options: School, Student, Book. A child might say “Student” because a doctor treats patients and a teacher teaches students. That’s a relationship, but it’s the wrong shape of the keyhole. The correct relationship is workplace. A doctor works in a hospital; a teacher works in a school. The keyhole is “works in,” not “deals with.” See the difference? That tiny distinction is what makes the hardest analogy questions feel impossible—until you know the trick.

🧠 The Unforgettable Trick: The “Because-Therefore” Test

The hardest concept in analogy isn’t about knowing big words. It’s about choosing between two options that both seem correct. Here’s how a 12-year-old can always get it right.

Turn the given pair into a “Because… Therefore…” sentence.

For example: Fish : Water
Step 1: Ask why is fish related to water? “Because a fish lives in water.”
Step 2: Now take the first word of the next pair and put it in the same sentence. Bird : ?
“Because a bird lives in ?” — Sky, nest, tree… many answers? Uh oh.

We need to make the relationship tighter. The real trick is to use the “Therefore” half: “Because a fish lives in water, therefore water is the natural home of a fish.” Now do the same for bird: “Because a bird lives in ___, therefore ___ is the natural home of a bird.” “Nest” is a home, but not the natural environment. The tightest match is Sky (or Air). That’s how you pick the most precise key.

Now, when two options both seem to fit, tighten your because-therefore sentence. Instead of “A doctor works in a hospital,” say “A doctor treats patients in a hospital.” For teacher: “A teacher teaches students in a school.” Options “classroom” and “school”? Classroom is a part of school; school is the workplace like hospital is. So “school” wins.

The Golden Rule: If more than one option fits, your relationship isn’t specific enough. Add one more detail until only one key fits the lock.
