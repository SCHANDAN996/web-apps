🔥 A Powerful Start — The Detective's Story

A detective arrives at two completely different crime scenes. At the first scene, he finds a single red rose. At the second scene, a red silk handkerchief. There are no other clues. While other officers feel stuck, a spark goes off in the detective's mind: "Red color... this is not a coincidence, it is a pattern." He discovered a hidden link between two seemingly unrelated items. That is exactly what Analogy is all about.

Now think about this: "Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ?" Your mind instantly answers — "School". Without even realizing it, you just solved an analogy problem! So why does anxiety kick in during an exam? Because students often get distracted by complex words and lose sight of the relationship connecting them. This chapter will eliminate that fear once and for all.

😣 Why Do Students Struggle with Analogy?

Most students mistakenly assume that analogy is purely a test of vocabulary. That is a major misconception. The real core is identifying the exact Relationship. Examiners deliberately include options that appear superficially similar. However, if you pinpoint the precise connection, even unfamiliar vocabulary cannot stop you. Today, we will master that skill.

🧠 Active Recall: What does the symbol "::" mean in an analogy question? (Think before reading ahead — "A : B :: C : D" means "A is related to B in the exact same way that C is related to D".)

🧩 What is Analogy? — Breaking It Down

Analogy = A relationship of similarity. In any given pair of words, numbers, or letters, there is a hidden rule or relationship. We must apply that identical relationship to the second pair to find the missing term.

Format: A : B :: C : ?
Step 1: Identify the relationship connecting A and B.
Step 2: Apply the exact same relationship to C to find the answer.

Example:
Fish : Water :: Bird : ?
A fish lives in water, so where does a bird live? Air / Sky. Answer: Sky.

🧠 Active Recall: If the relationship in A : B is "A is the offspring of B", and C is "Cow", what is D? (Answer: Calf)

🪄 The Magic Mnemonic: Identify Questions Instantly with WING

The moment you see a question, classify it immediately using the WING formula:

W – Word Analogy: Involves word meanings, synonyms, antonyms, part-to-whole, and cause-and-effect.
I – Integer / Number Analogy: Involves mathematical operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, squares, cubes, and digit operations.
N – aN alphabet (Letter Analogy): Involves alphabetical positions, forward/backward shifts, and opposite letter pairs.
G – General Knowledge Analogy: Involves facts such as country-capital, author-book, units, instruments, and inventions.

WING gives you the right direction within seconds.

🧱 Type 1: Word Analogy & The "Sentence Bridge" Technique

This is the most common and accessible type. Words are connected through logical, semantic relationships.
Sentence Bridge Method: Mentally form a short, clear, and complete sentence connecting A and B. Then take word C and insert it into that identical sentence structure. Whichever option fits naturally and logically is your answer.

Example 1:
Cow : Milk :: Bee : ?
Sentence: "A cow provides us with milk."
Now apply to Bee: "A bee provides us with..." Honey. Answer: Honey.

Example 2:
Happy : Joyful :: Sad : ? (Relationship: Synonyms)
Sentence: "Joyful is a synonym for happy."
Now apply to Sad: "A synonym for sad is..." Sorrowful / Gloomy. Answer: Sorrowful.

🧠 Active Recall: Build a sentence bridge for "Tree : Seed :: Human : ?" (A tree grows from a seed; a human develops from an infant/baby. Answer: Infant/Baby.)

🔢 Type 2: Number Analogy

Here, numbers are connected through mathematical logic. Look closely at numerical relationships:
Common patterns:
• Basic arithmetic: Addition, subtraction, multiplication, division
• Squares and Cubes: n², n³, n² ± 1, n³ ± 1
• Operations on digits: Sum of digits, product of digits
• Combined operations: e.g., (×2 + 1), (×3 - 2)

Example:
4 : 16 :: 5 : ?
The square of 4 is 16 (4² = 16), therefore the square of 5 is 25 (5² = 25). Answer: 25.

Caution: Sometimes multiple operations seem plausible. Always check available options to verify which rule the examiner applied.

🧠 Active Recall: 8 : 27 :: 64 : ? (Hint: Cubes) — 8 = 2³, 27 = 3³, 64 = 4³, so the next term is 5³ = 125. Answer: 125.

🔤 Type 3: Letter Analogy

Remember alphabetical positions: A = 1, B = 2 ... Z = 26. Look for logical letter transformations:
Key rules:
• Forward and Backward shifts: +1, +2, -2, etc.
• Opposite Letters: A pairs with Z, B pairs with Y. (The sum of opposite letter ranks is always 27: A(1) + Z(26) = 27, B(2) + Y(25) = 27).
• Reverse order / Mirror reflection of letters within a word or cluster.

Example:
ABC : ZYX :: PQR : ?
ABC is paired with its exact opposite letters ZYX.
For PQR: P(16) opposite is 27 - 16 = 11 (K); Q(17) opposite is 27 - 17 = 10 (J); R(18) opposite is 27 - 18 = 9 (I).
Result: KJI. Answer: KJI.

🧠 Active Recall: AB : YZ :: CD : ? (AB from the start pairs with YZ from the end; C=3 gives 27 - 3 = 24 (X), D=4 gives 27 - 4 = 23 (W)). Answer: XW.

🌍 Type 4: General Knowledge (GK) Analogy

These questions test static GK through associative logic. Key categories:
• Country : Capital (e.g., France : Paris)
• Country : Currency (e.g., Japan : Yen)
• Instrument : Measurement (e.g., Barometer : Pressure)
• Worker : Tool (e.g., Carpenter : Saw)
• State : Folk Dance (e.g., Punjab : Bhangra)

Example:
India : New Delhi :: Japan : ?
Sentence Bridge: "New Delhi is the capital of India."
Therefore: "The capital of Japan is..." Tokyo. Answer: Tokyo.

🧠 Active Recall: "Pen : Write :: Knife : ?" (A pen is an instrument used to write; a knife is an instrument used to cut. Answer: Cut.)

⚡ The 30-Second Strategy in the Exam Hall

1. Identify with WING: Immediately determine whether the question is Word, Number, Letter, or GK based.
2. Build the Sentence Bridge: Formulate a precise, concise sentence connecting the first pair.
3. Test the Options: Substitute each option into your sentence for term D. The option that creates the most logical match is correct.
4. If Two Options Remain: Tighten the sentence bridge with more specific constraints (e.g., not just "a workplace", but "the specific indoor room where an expert operates").

🧠 Active Recall: If two options seem to fit your sentence bridge, what should be your immediate next step? (Tighten the definition by adding specific functional or relational details.)

🧪 Testing the Sentence Bridge on a Tricky Question

Question: "Doctor : Stethoscope :: Musician : ?"
Options: (a) Instrument (b) Music (c) Hearing (d) Rhythm

Sentence Bridge: "A doctor uses a stethoscope as a physical tool to perform professional work."
Now test the musician: A musician uses an instrument as a physical tool to perform professional work.
Option (a) "Instrument" matches perfectly.
Why not (b) "Music"? Music is the artistic product/output, not the tool used to produce it. The sentence bridge saves you from the trap!

⚠️ Examiner's Traps & How to Avoid Them

1. Two Near-Miss Options
Examiners often include two choices that both loosely relate to the prompt. For instance: "Painter : Brush :: Writer : ?" Options: (a) Pen (b) Words.
Sentence: "A painter uses a brush as a physical tool to create art."
A writer uses a pen as a physical tool to write. "Words" are the medium or language, not the instrument. Always focus on the exact tool-user relationship.

2. Intimidating Vocabulary
Sometimes questions use formal or archaic words: "Oasis : Desert :: Island : ?" (Water/Ocean). Do not panic when facing uncommon words. Look at the relationship and use the surrounding options as clues to deduce the core connection.

3. Double Relationships (Broad vs. Specific)
Consider: "Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ?" If options include both "Classroom" and "School", choose "School". Hospital is the overall institution where a doctor works, so School (the overall institution) is the exact parallel, whereas classroom is merely one sub-unit. Always match the scope of the relationship.
