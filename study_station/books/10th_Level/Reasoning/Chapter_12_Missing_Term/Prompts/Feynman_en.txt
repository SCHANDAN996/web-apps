🧒 Feynman Technique: The Trick of the Hidden Twins

Imagine you're at a party. There are two DJs, DJ Even and DJ Odd. DJ Even plays a beat: boom... boom... boom... – every beat is louder by 2 decibels: 10, 12, 14, 16. DJ Odd plays a different tune: clap... clap... clap... – every clap gets 3 decibels louder: 5, 8, 11, 14. They take turns. The sound you hear is: 10, 5, 12, 8, 14, 11, 16, 14.

If I ask you, “What comes next?” you might get confused because you’re hearing two songs mixed together. But once you realise that one singer only sings at odd positions (1st, 3rd, 5th) and the other only at even positions (2nd, 4th, 6th), you simply separate them in your mind. Then you find each song’s own simple pattern. That’s the “double series” or “interleaved series” — the hardest concept in Missing Term made child’s play.

The Unforgettable Trick: “Split and High-Five”
Whenever you see a series that doesn’t make sense as a whole, give it a high-five:

Split the sequence into two hands: left hand (odd positions) and right hand (even positions).

Write them down separately.

Now find the pattern for each hand independently — they’re almost always simple addition/subtraction or multiplication.

Example: 3, 21, 5, 18, 7, 15, ?, ?
Left hand (1st, 3rd, 5th): 3, 5, 7 → +2 each time → next is 9.
Right hand (2nd, 4th, 6th): 21, 18, 15 → –3 each time → next is 12.
Answer: 9, 12.

That’s it. No magic, just separating the twin series. The examiners love to wrap simple patterns in a tangled ribbon, and you’re the one who untangles them.

So, why is this the hardest? Because your brain tries to connect every number to its neighbour, and when 3 jumps to 21 (+18), then 21 to 5 (–16), it looks random. But you’ve now learned that some neighbours don’t belong to the same family. They’re just politely taking turns.

📝 Blurting Sheet Instruction

Now close the book. Take a blank sheet of paper. Without peeking, write down:

How to recognise a double series.

After you’ve written everything you remember, open the chapter and fill in what you missed with a different coloured pen. That’s how Feynman mastered hard topics — by teaching them to an empty chair.

Final Question: What if a triple series appears — three patterns taking turns? Can you extend the Split and High-Five trick to three hands? How would you find the next term in 1, 10, 100, 2, 20, 200, 3, 30, ? Think about it.
