📊 PYQ Analysis: Missing Term — Pattern & Trend Analysis

Note: This section analyses how exams test Missing Term questions — which sub-topics recur, what traps examiners set, and how to manage time. We do not quote year-wise question counts, as verified official data of that kind is not available.

1. How Different Exams Test This Topic

- Banking exams (IBPS/SBI PO & Clerk Prelims): Number series is a regular, high-weightage feature of the reasoning/quant section. Mixed-operation series (×2+1, ×3−2), decimal multipliers (×0.5, ×1.5, ×2.5…) and double (interleaved) series are favourites. Expect several series questions in a single paper.
- SSC (CGL, CHSL): Usually a small but consistent number of series questions per paper. SSC favours squares/cubes-based series, second-order differences, and occasionally letter series and matrix-based missing numbers.
- Railway (NTPC, Group D): Series questions appear intermittently; simple number series and alphabet series dominate, with moderate difficulty.
- Delhi Police / State exams: Occasional series questions, often logic-heavy — primes, alternating sequences, and sometimes date-based series.

Overall: the topic's importance is stable across exams. Banking demands speed on number series; SSC demands breadth across number, letter and matrix formats.

2. Sub-Topics That Recur Most

- Number series with simple or second-order differences (all exams; usually easy)
- Number series based on squares, cubes, or n²/n³ ± constant (SSC favourite)
- Mixed-operation series: ×a + b with changing a, b (Banking favourite)
- Double (interleaved) series — two alternating patterns (Banking, higher difficulty)
- Alphabet series with position-wise increments (SSC, Railway)
- Alphanumeric series — parallel number + letter + number patterns (Railway, SSC)
- Matrix-based missing number — row or column logic (SSC, occasional)

Key insight: In Banking, master mixed operations and decimal multipliers first. For SSC, prioritise squares/cubes and second-order differences, then letter series. For Railway, simple number and alphabet series give the best return.

3. Ten High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

Q1. 0, 7, 26, 63, ?

Solution: Each term is n³ – 1. 1³–1=0, 2³–1=7, 3³–1=26, 4³–1=63, so next is 5³–1=124.
Answer: 124
Trap: The numbers are one less than perfect cubes, but 0 also fits n²–1 (n=1), anchoring candidates to squares. Explore cubes when squares fail.
Skip decision (45-sec rule): Solve immediately — the cube pattern is recognisable in under 15 seconds.
Source: PYQ-style

Q2. Matrix: In each column, a² + c = b². Given 1² + 120 = 121 = 11² and 2² + 45 = 49 = 7², find x where 3² + x = 5².

Solution: x = 25 – 9 = 16.
Answer: 16
Trap: Matrix data is often displayed to suggest row-wise reading while the logic works column-wise — a "direction bias" trap. Candidates waste time checking rows.
Skip decision: Solve if column logic is spotted quickly; otherwise mark and return after two attempts.
Source: PYQ-style

Q3. HJRN, JMTQ, LPVT, NSXW, ?

Solution: First letter: H→J→L→N→P (+2). Second: J→M→P→S→V (+3). Third: R→T→V→X→Z (+2). Fourth: N→Q→T→W→Z (+3).
Answer: PVZZ
Trap: Four letters with two alternating increments (+2, +3) overload working memory. Decoy options match only one or two letters.
Skip decision (15-sec rule): If the split pattern isn't detected in 15 seconds, skip and return.
Source: PYQ-style

Q4. 56, 28, 42, 105, ?, 1653.75

Solution: Multipliers are ×0.5, ×1.5, ×2.5, ×3.5, ×4.5. So 105 × 3.5 = 367.5 (check: 367.5 × 4.5 = 1653.75 ✓).
Answer: 367.5
Trap: Decimal multipliers induce anxiety — candidates expect integers. The large jump to 1653.75 tests whether you trust the pattern with extreme values.
Skip decision: A classic banking pattern; solve if the multiplier sequence is recognised, else skip after 45 seconds.
Source: PYQ-style

Q5. 2, 6, 30, 210, ?, 30030

Solution: Multipliers are consecutive primes: ×3, ×5, ×7, ×11, ×13. So 210 × 11 = 2310 (check: 2310 × 13 = 30030 ✓).
Answer: 2310
Trap: Rapid growth tempts candidates toward ×2, ×3, ×4… (factorial-like). The examiner exploits "familiar pattern bias" — check primes when integer multipliers fail.
Skip decision: Solve within 30–40 seconds if prime multiplication occurs to you; otherwise skip.
Source: PYQ-style

Q6. 132, 129, 124, 117, 106, 93, ?

Solution: Differences: –3, –5, –7, –9, –11, so next is –13. 93 – 13 = 80.
Answer: 80
Trap: The differences look like primes (–3, –5, –7…) but –9 is not prime — a "near-prime" decoy for those who don't verify every term.
Skip decision (15-sec rule): Recognisable second-order pattern — solve in 15–20 seconds.
Source: PYQ-style

Q7. Date series: 4/12/95, 1/1/96, 29/1/96, 26/2/96, ?

Solution: Each date is 28 days after the previous. 26/2/96 + 28 days = 25/3/96 (1996 is a leap year, so February has 29 days).
Answer: 25/3/96
Trap: A "domain shift" trap — the pattern leaves pure arithmetic and enters calendar logic. The leap year is a subtle distractor that tests date awareness.
Skip decision: If the 28-day cycle isn't spotted quickly, skip — date series are rare but high-trap.
Source: PYQ-style

Q8. 2A11, 4D13, 12G17, ?, 240M31

Solution: Prefix numbers: ×2, ×3, ×4, ×5 → 12×4 = 48 (check: 48×5 = 240 ✓). Letters: A→D→G→J→M (+3). Suffix: primes 11, 13, 17, 23, 31 (gaps +2, +4, +6, +8).
Answer: 48J23
Trap: Three parallel patterns overload working memory. The suffix skips 19 — a "missing step" trap for those who assume consecutive primes.
Skip decision: High-difficulty alphanumeric; skip if not solved in 45 seconds.
Source: PYQ-style

Q9. ZE8, YJ6, VO4, ?, JY0

Solution: First letter: Z→Y(–1), Y→V(–3), V→Q(–5), Q→J(–7). Second letter: E→J→O→T→Y (+5). Number: 8→6→4→2→0 (–2).
Answer: QT2
Trap: Three different operations on three elements demand divided attention. The examiner lures candidates into assuming one uniform operation.
Skip decision: Time-consuming multi-element series — mark and return after 45 seconds.
Source: PYQ-style

Q10. 12, 17, 24, 33, 44, ?

Solution: Differences: +5, +7, +9, +11, so next is +13. 44 + 13 = 57.
Answer: 57
Trap: A simple second-order difference looks too easy, provoking overthinking — candidates hunt for hidden squares or primes and miss the obvious. The trap is distrust of simplicity.
Skip decision (15-sec rule): Solve immediately — a textbook 10-second question.
Source: PYQ-style

4. Psychological Trap Summary

Trap Category | Description | Mitigation
Direction Bias | Matrix logic works column-wise but data appears row-wise. | Test both row and column patterns within the first 15 seconds.
Working Memory Overload | Multi-letter or alphanumeric series with different increments per element. | Isolate each position and solve independently.
Familiar Pattern Bias | Candidate locks onto a familiar pattern (e.g., squares) and ignores alternatives (e.g., cubes). | Keep a mental checklist; do not commit until 3 terms confirm.
Decimal Anxiety | Non-integer multipliers induce panic. | Treat decimals as normal arithmetic; the logic is identical.
Premature Closure | Candidate selects an answer after matching only 2 terms. | Verify the rule on at least 3 consecutive pairs.
Domain Shift | Pattern shifts from numbers to calendar logic or letter rules. | Stay mentally flexible; not all series are arithmetic.
Simplicity Distrust | Easy patterns are doubted, leading to overthinking. | If a simple rule fits all terms, it is correct — do not overcomplicate.

Final Word: Missing Term is a high-accuracy, high-speed topic. The key is pattern-recognition speed, which comes only from practising diverse question types. Use the 15/45-second rule ruthlessly; never let one question consume your entire reasoning section.
