🧒 Feynman Corner: The Secret of the Shape Dance

Alright, imagine you're at a park. Two best friends, Riya and Raj, are playing a strange game with a piece of chalk and a stone. Riya draws a smiley face on the ground. Then Raj steps up. He doesn't draw a new face—he takes the same stone and flips it over, creating a mirror image of Riya's smiley. Then Riya steps up again. She grabs the stone and spins it a quarter turn. Then Raj flips it again. They keep taking turns: Riya spins, Raj flips.

Now, you're watching from a bench. After a few turns, you close your eyes for a second. When you open them, you see the stone in a new position. Can you guess what the next move will be? The key is knowing whose turn it is. If it's Riya's turn, a spin is coming. If it's Raj's turn, a mirror flip is coming.

Figure Series sometimes plays exactly this game with your brain. You see a sequence of shapes: first one, second one, third, fourth. You look for a rule: "Oh, it's rotating!" But then the next step breaks that rule. Confusion strikes. That's the hardest concept—the alternating pattern. Two different rules take turns, just like Riya and Raj.

In exam language, the first, third, fifth figures follow one rule (say, rotating 90° clockwise). The second, fourth, sixth figures follow a completely different rule (maybe becoming a water image or adding a line). If you assume one rule runs the whole show, you'll get the next figure wrong every single time.

The real-life trick: The Odd-Even Check

Next time you're stuck, number the problem figures in your head: 1,2,3,4,5. Look at the odd-numbered figures (1,3,5). They'll be connected by one consistent change. Now look at the even-numbered figures (2,4). They'll be connected by another consistent change. This instantly splits the dance into two separate rhythms, and you'll never get lost.

Think of it as two separate snail trails. One snail leaves a slime of spinning shapes; the other leaves a trail of mirrored shapes. They just happen to move one after the other. Once you see the two trails, you can predict exactly where the next snail will stop.

The unforgettable trick: Always ask "Whose turn is it?" before predicting the next shape. If the last given figure is at position 4 (even), the next is position 5—odd—so use the odd rule. If the last is 3 (odd), the next is 4—even—so switch to the even rule. It's that simple.
