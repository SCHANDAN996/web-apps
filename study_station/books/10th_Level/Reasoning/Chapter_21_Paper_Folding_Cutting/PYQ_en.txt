3. Top 10 High-Yield PYQ Models with Solutions & Traps

Here are 10 question models derived from PYQs, along with the psychological traps they employ.

Q1. The Classic Single-Fold Mirror
Question: A piece of paper is folded in half vertically. A semi-circle is cut out from the folded edge. How will it appear when opened?
Solution: The fold acts as a mirror. The semi-circle will be reflected to form a full circle along the vertical axis.
🧠 Psychological Hook: The Rush Job. The brain’s first instinct is to complete the circle. The trap is presenting a "complete" circle that is slightly offset or has uneven halves.
🕒 Time Rule: A 15-second question. If you can't decide on the evenness of the circle within 15 seconds, you're overthinking.

Q2. The Double-Fold Symmetry
Question: A square paper is folded in half vertically, then horizontally. A small triangle is cut from the corner that has no folds (i.e., all four edges are single layers). How many triangles will appear when unfolded?
Solution: 4. The first vertical fold creates 2 triangles. The second horizontal fold mirrors these, creating a symmetric pattern of 4 triangles.
🧠 Psychological Hook: The Counting Illusion. Options will include 2, 3, and 4 triangles. The mind, in a hurry, might stop at the first mirroring and choose 2.
🕒 Time Rule: A 45-second question. The extra fold requires deliberate mental unfolding.

Q3. The "Touch-or-Not" Trap
Question: Paper is folded into a triangle. A cut is made on the folded paper very close to, but not touching, the fold line. What is the result?
Solution: Two separate, distinct shapes that do not connect. The cut is mirrored but not merged.
🧠 Psychological Hook: Trick of Proximity. The brain groups things that are close together. It assumes the cuts will join because they're near, not because they touch.
🕒 Time Rule: 45 seconds. This requires high visual precision.

Q4. The Punch on the Fold
Question: A square paper is folded twice to form a smaller square. A single hole is punched exactly on the corner where the two folds meet. How will it appear when opened?
Solution: A single hole in the center of the original square. The hole is mirrored on each fold, but since it's on the fold, the four mirrored holes merge into one.
🧠 Psychological Hook: The 2ⁿ Deception. Students blindly apply the 2² = 4 holes rule and panic when they don't see it. The examiner exploits formula dependency without conceptual understanding.

Q5. The Asymmetrical Cut Conundrum
Question: A paper is folded once. An asymmetrical shape, like an 'L' or a scalene triangle, is cut from the folded edge.
Solution: The 'L' will be reflected to create a shape that is a mirror image of itself. The final shape will be symmetrical overall, composed of the original and its mirror.
🧠 Psychological Hook: Rotation vs. Reflection. The examiner provides an option where the 'L' is simply rotated to complete the pattern, which is the most common error.

Q6. The Zig-Zag Cut & Quadrant Mirroring
Question: A circular paper is folded into a quarter-circle. A zig-zag cut is made on the curved edge.
Solution: The zig-zag will be reflected across both folds, creating a pattern that is repeated 4 times around the center of the full circle.-
20

🧠 Psychological Hook: Pattern Recognition Over Process. The mind tries to guess the pattern as a whole rather than building it step-by-step. Options with similar but wrongly rotated zig-zags are presented.
🕒 Time Rule: This is a skip-worthy question for many. If the mental step-by-step visualization isn't clear in 15 seconds, flag it and return.

Q7. The Triple-Fold Time Sink
Question: A paper is folded in half three times. A small notch is cut from one of the corners.
Solution: The notch will be reflected 2³ = 8 times, creating a pattern of 8 notches around the edges of the original paper.
🧠 Psychological Hook: Cognitive Overload. The sheer number of mirroring steps is intimidating. The trap is to spend too long trying to visualize all three folds at once rather than sequentially.
🕒 Time Rule: Prime candidate for the 15-second skip rule. The time-to-marks ratio is generally poor for these questions under exam pressure.

Q8. The Punch Away from the Fold
Question: A square paper is folded diagonally. A hole is punched in the center of the resulting triangle.
Solution: Two holes. One is the original punch, and the other is its mirror image across the diagonal fold. They are separate and distinct.
🧠 Psychological Hook: Creating a False Center. The mind sees the single punch in the center of the triangle and assumes it will be the center of the square, leading to an option with a single, large, central hole.

Q9. The Option Rotation Trap
Question: The correct final unfolded figure is a specific 'T' shape. One option shows the exact same 'T' but rotated by 90°.
Solution: The rotated 'T' is incorrect. Folds only produce mirror images, not rotations.
🧠 Psychological Hook: The "Looks Right" Deception. The rotated option appears similar at a glance, exploiting the brain's tendency to recognize shapes irrespective of orientation.

Q10. The Complex Design on a Transparent Sheet
Question: A transparent sheet has a complex design with a dotted fold line.
Solution: The part of the design on one side of the line will be superimposed on the other side after folding. The final pattern is the union of the two parts.
🧠 Psychological Hook: The Invisible Overlap. It's difficult to visualize which elements will overlap and which will be visible on top. The trap is presenting an option where the overlapping elements are placed side-by-side instead of being merged.
🕒 Time Rule: A clear 45-second question that relies more on image manipulation than abstract logic.

⚠️ The Examiner's Psychological Traps: An Insider's Summary

Understanding these three cognitive traps is your best defense against making avoidable mistakes.

The Rotation vs. Reflection Trap: This is the single most common trick. The examiner exploits the brain's tendency to recognize a shape regardless of its orientation. You see the correct elements, just rotated, and your brain registers it as "correct". Your defense: Always ask "Is this a mirror image or a rotated image?" Folds only create mirrors.-
3

The Touch-or-Not Illusion: The proximity of a cut to a fold line can create a powerful illusion. A cut that's close to the fold but not touching it can mentally "merge" with its mirror image. The examiner provides a joined shape as a trap. Your defense: Pause and check the last folded image. Is the cut actually on the line, or is there a sliver of space?

The Blurry/Similar Image Trap: When multiple options look nearly identical, it's a test of your precision and patience. The difference might be the angle of a cut, the size of a hole, or a slight shift in position. Your defense: Systematically compare one feature at a time. "The cut in option A is triangular. Is mine triangular? Yes. Now, is the base of the triangle up or down?" This methodical approach defeats the trap.
