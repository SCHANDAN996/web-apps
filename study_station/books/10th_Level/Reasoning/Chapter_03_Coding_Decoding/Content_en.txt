🔍 A Detective Story to Begin

In 1942, during World War II, a British intelligence officer intercepted a mysterious radio transmission: "GUR DHVXR OEBJA SBK WHZCF BIRE GUR YNML QBT." There was no obvious meaning, no map coordinates. Only an old cipher rule: shift every single letter backward by a fixed number of positions. Within minutes, the message unfolded: "THE QUICK BROWN FOX JUMPS OVER THE LAZY DOG." An impending ambush was uncovered and prevented.

That is the power of Coding-Decoding. And every government exam question tests this exact investigative skill — discovering the hidden transformation rule connecting the original word to its code.

📦 Learn in Chunks — The 5 Core Types of Coding-Decoding

Every problem is built on a single mathematical or positional transformation rule:

1. Letter Coding: Shifting letters forward or backward by fixed or increasing steps. E.g., 'RAM' written as 'SBN' represents a uniform +1 shift.
2. Number Coding: Assigning numerical values to letters based on their alphabetical positions. E.g., 'BAT' = 2120 (B=2, A=1, T=20).
3. Reverse/Opposite Coding: Pairing letters from opposite ends of the alphabet (A↔Z, B↔Y). Formula: Opposite Rank = 27 – Normal Rank.
4. Substitution Coding: Replacing names or objects with fictitious labels (e.g., "If 'pen' is called 'book', and 'book' is called 'cap'...").
5. Mixed / Alphanumeric Coding: Combining letters, numbers, and symbols simultaneously.

🧠 Quick Check: If the opposite letter of 'E' is 'V', what is the opposite letter of 'H'? (E=5, 27 - 5 = 22 = V; H=8, 27 - 8 = 19 = S. Answer: S.)

🧠 Your Most Critical Weapon — Alphabet Numbering & "EJOTY"

Remembering A=1 to Z=26 is basic, but in the heat of the exam hall, recalling the exact rank of 'Q' or 'V' can cause hesitation.

✨ Memory Mnemonic: EJOTY

• E = 5
• J = 10
• O = 15
• T = 20
• Y = 25

Remember every fifth letter as an anchor. Now any letter is instant:
• M is 3 positions after J(10) → 13.
• S is 1 position before T(20) → 19.
For reverse positions, remember the Golden Formula:
Reverse Position = 27 – Forward Position.
This single formula is your ultimate safety shield.

🔎 The 3-Step Pattern Detection Formula

Whenever you encounter a coding problem, apply these three steps immediately:

Step 1: Write Vertically
Write the given word and its code directly beneath each other.
Example:
C  O  L  D
D  P  M  E

Step 2: Calculate the Positional Difference
Examine each vertical letter pair: C→D (+1), O→P (+1), L→M (+1), D→E (+1). The uniform rule is +1.

Step 3: Apply to the Target Word
Apply the identical transformation to the target word. In multiple-choice questions, verify the first and last letters first — option elimination often reveals the correct answer halfway through.

🧠 Active Recall: If 'HOT' is coded as 'GNS', identify the rule. (H→G is -1, O→N is -1, T→S is -1. Uniform -1 shift.)

⚠️ Double-Layer Patterns & Shortcuts

If a simple forward/backward shift does not work, check these common secondary patterns:
• Reversal: The word is reversed first (e.g., COLD becomes DLOC) and then shifted (+1).
• Vowels vs. Consonants: Different operations applied to vowels (e.g., +2) and consonants (e.g., -1).
• Positional Swapping: Swapping the first and last letters while shifting interior letters.

Master Tip: Never spend more than 30–40 seconds searching for an elusive pattern. Eliminate obvious mismatches, flag the question, and move forward.

⚠️ Examiner's Traps & How to Avoid Them

1. Competing Shift Patterns
Examiners sometimes design codes where the first two letters suggest a simple +1 pattern, but the remaining letters follow a progressive (+1, +2, +3) pattern. Always verify the rule across the entire word before marking your answer.

2. Intimidating Vocabulary
Words like "OXYMORON", "QUAGMIRE", or "XYLOPHONE" are chosen specifically to cause panic. Remember: Coding rules apply strictly to letter positions, not word meanings. Convert the letters into numbers, and the fear disappears.

3. Double-Layer Transformation Traps
Some questions reverse the entire word before applying a positional shift. If a straightforward vertical comparison fails, immediately check whether the code matches the reverse spelling of the word.
