🧠 Feynman’s Corner: The Toughest Classification Puzzle — Made Easy!

Let me tell you a story. Two siblings, Anu and Ravi, had a basket with five things: a red apple, a green apple, a banana, an orange, and a carrot. Their mom said, “Take out the one that doesn’t belong.” Anu shouted, “Carrot! It’s a vegetable, the rest are fruits.” Ravi argued, “No, the green apple is odd because all others are either red, yellow, orange, or orange-ish. Green is different.” Mom laughed. “You’re both right — but only one answer wins in an exam. How do you decide?”

This is exactly the hardest thing in Classification — when more than one pattern stares at you. In an exam, the question-master deliberately puts options where two or three different bases (reasons) can work. If you pick the first one you see, you might fall into a trap. So what’s the real secret?

Think of each option as a friend in a group. You want to find who doesn’t belong. But you must look for a rule that exactly 4 friends follow and 1 friend breaks. If you find a rule that all 5 follow, that rule is useless. If you find a rule that only 3 follow, that’s also not the exam’s rule — because the odd one would be 2, but we only need exactly 1 odd. So the magic trick: a correct classification base is one that fits 4 items and fails on exactly 1. That’s it. It’s that simple.

Here’s a real-deal Feynman trick, unforgettable: The “Four-Yes, One-No” Dance. Picture yourself asking each option, “Do you have this property? Yes or No?” You’re looking for the property where you get four head-nods and one shaking head. Let’s dance it out with letters: H, I, J, K, L. Property: Is the letter a vowel? I says Yes (vowel), H,J,K,L say No. Four Nos, one Yes — that works, I is odd. But if you picked “Is the letter in the first half of alphabet?” H,I,J,K,L — I is 9, still first half; all are first half. Five Yes. That’s a trap property. So the dance quickly shows you: four-yes-one-no or four-no-one-yes is your winner. Any property giving five same answers is just the examiner laughing at you.

Now let’s destroy the old confusion with Anu and Ravi’s basket. Base “Fruit vs Vegetable” gives Carrot No, others Yes — four Yes, one No. Perfect odd one. Base “Green vs Not Green” gives Green Apple Yes, others No — one Yes, four No. Also four-one pattern! Two valid patterns? In a real exam, only one will be in the options. If both answers are choices, the examiner expects the most direct, primary category. The rule of thumb: when multiple bases produce a four-one pattern, pick the one that uses the most obvious, immediate feature (like edible category over colour). And don’t worry — well-designed questions avoid giving both answers in the choices. If they do, one will be a deeper, more specific base that the question intends.
