Statement & Course of Action appears regularly in the reasoning sections of SSC, Banking, and Railway examinations. It is never the heaviest topic in a paper, but it is a dependable source of 1–2 quick marks for a prepared candidate. The analysis below describes the *pattern* of these questions — what examiners repeatedly test and where they hide their traps.

📈 How Often Does It Appear?

- **SSC (CGL, CHSL):** Almost every year, typically 1–2 questions of easy-to-moderate difficulty. Usually a simple, direct Problem-and-Solution scenario; answers rarely require complex justification.
- **Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO):** Regular, 1–2 questions of moderate difficulty. Frequently uses Data-Driven or Policy scenarios, with options mixing a strong administrative step and an extreme/impractical one.
- **Railway (RRB NTPC, Group D):** Occasional — some shifts have one easy question, many have none. Usually a straightforward, fact-based statement with a single obvious action.
- **SSC MTS / GD:** Rare. When it does appear, the question tends to be deceptive: the trap lies in making an extreme or assumption-based option look "strong" and "decisive."

🧠 Sub-Topic Weightage (Qualitative)

The core logical principle is always the same — does the course of action logically and practically follow from the statement? — but the scenarios vary in flavour.

| Sub-Topic / Scenario Type | Frequency | Key Traits |
|---|---|---|
| Problem-and-Solution | High | Direct issue (crime, accident, congestion) with an immediate administrative step. |
| Fact-and-Improvement | Moderate | Neutral fact (low attendance, pollution data) requiring a positive, feasible step. |
| Data-Driven / Policy | Low | Statistical report (crop yield, revenue loss) demanding a policy-level intervention. |
| Extreme / Trap-based | Occasional | The wrong option deliberately uses an extreme, impractical, or assumption-based action. |

🔥 Top 10 High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions & Psychological Traps

*(These are representative questions built on the recurring patterns of SSC/Bank/Railway papers. Source: PYQ-style.)*

1. Statement: The traffic congestion in the city has significantly increased during peak hours.
Courses: I. Improve public transportation infrastructure. II. Impose hefty fines on individual drivers during peak hours.
Answer: Only I follows.
Psychological Trap: The allure of "tough action." Option II feels strong and punitive, but punishing a legal activity is illogical — an overreach trap. I is a direct, feasible solution.
Skip Rule: Do not skip; apply the "legal action" test within 15 seconds.

2. Statement: There is a large increase in villagers migrating to urban areas due to repeated crop failure and financial problems.
Courses: I. Provide alternate income sources in villages. II. Give migrants access to all urban housing.
Answer: Only I follows.
Psychological Trap: The "humane diversion." II is compassionate but addresses a symptom, not the root cause. I is the sustainable administrative solution.
Skip Rule: Do not skip; identify the root cause within 20 seconds.

3. Statement: A large private bank has retrenched one-third of its employees due to huge losses.
Courses: (A) Government asks the public to stop transactions with the bank. (B) Government directs the bank to refrain from retrenchment. (C) Government initiates an enquiry into the bank's affairs.
Answer: Only (C) follows.
Psychological Trap: The "urgent crisis" illusion. (A) and (B) are extreme, hasty decisions. (C) is the only prudent administrative step before acting — a classic procedural trap.
Skip Rule: 45-second rule; if you don't see the "enquiry first" logic, mark for review and move on.

4. Statement: A major railway accident was averted due to a signalman's presence of mind.
Courses: I. The signalman should be rewarded. II. The track should be cleared of all traffic for 50 km.
Answer: Only I follows.
Psychological Trap: The "overcorrection" trap. II is an extreme, impractical measure. I is a direct, logical consequence of the praise in the statement.

5. Statement: A high number of accidents occur at a railway crossing due to lack of proper signaling.
Courses: I. Install automated signaling immediately. II. Divert all trains to other routes.
Answer: Only I follows.
Psychological Trap: The "perfect safety" fallacy. II feels absolutely safe but is utterly impractical. I is a direct, feasible technical fix.

6. Statement: Many people have encroached on government land and built houses.
Courses: I. Government should immediately remove all unauthorized constructions. II. Government should regularize all structures built before a cut-off date.
Answer: Neither follows; a phased, legal process is needed.
Psychological Trap: The "binary simplification" trap. Both options are extreme endpoints — wholesale demolition or blanket regularization. The logical action is a middle-path, procedural step, which is absent here.
Skip Rule: 45-second rule; if forced, flag it as an "extreme binary" trap.

7. Statement: The condition of all major roads has deteriorated due to incessant rain.
Courses: I. Immediate repairs should be undertaken. II. A committee should be formed to investigate road quality.
Answer: Only I follows (immediate relief).
Psychological Trap: The "process vs. action" conflict. II is procedural and slow; I is the direct, immediate action the situation demands.

8. Statement: The prices of food grains have substantially increased due to a prolonged truck owners' strike.
Courses: I. Government makes alternative supply arrangements. II. Government cancels all vehicle licenses of truck owners.
Answer: Only I follows.
Psychological Trap: The "collective punishment" trap. II punishes an entire group without investigating the cause of the strike — an extreme, unfeasible overreaction.

9. Statement: Many students fell ill after consuming food from the school canteen.
Courses: I. Immediately terminate the canteen contract. II. Advise students not to eat from the canteen for now. III. Immediately arrest the canteen owner.
Answer: Only II follows.
Psychological Trap: The "instant justice" fallacy. I and III are extreme, premature actions before any investigation. II is a precautionary, immediate, and practical step.

10. Statement: The meteorological department has forecast less rainfall during next year's monsoon.
Courses: I. Farmers should be advised to use drought-resistant seeds. II. Government should prepare a water conservation plan. III. The affected area should be evacuated immediately.
Answer: Both I and II follow.
Psychological Trap: The "emergency exaggeration" trap. III is a baseless, extreme reaction to a mere forecast. I and II are proactive, data-driven policy steps.

⏱️ Time Management & Skip Strategy

**15-Second Rule (Detection):**
If you cannot identify the core problem in the statement within 15 seconds (e.g., a complex, multi-clause statement or ambiguous language), mark the question for review and skip it immediately. Data-heavy questions are the primary candidates for this rule.

**45-Second Rule (Resolution):**
If you are still debating between two options after 45 seconds, you have likely fallen into a trap. The examiner has probably placed an "almost correct" but extreme, irrelevant, or assumption-based option next to the correct one. In this case, quickly re-apply the CLEAR filter (Check Legality, Logic, Extremity, Assumptions, Root cause). If still stuck, select the option that is least extreme, most practical, and most directly linked to the root cause, mark for review, and move on.
