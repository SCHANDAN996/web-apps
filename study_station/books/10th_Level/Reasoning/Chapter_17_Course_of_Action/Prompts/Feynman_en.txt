Richard Feynman’s Way to Crack “Statement & Course of Action”

Hello, young thinker! Let me tell you a secret: The hardest part of “Statement & Course of Action” isn’t really hard—it just seems hard because grown‑up exam writers use big words and try to confuse you. Today, we’ll melt that confusion away with a story and a trick so simple, a 12‑year‑old can teach it to a friend.

The Hardest Concept: What Really Counts as an Action?
In this topic, they give you a problem (Statement). Then they offer ideas to fix it (Courses of Action). But here’s the catch—not every “good idea” is a real action.
A real action is something you can actually do right then, with the things you have, without magic. Wishes, advice, and big dreams are NOT actions.

Imagine your room is on fire.
Someone shouts, “We should all be more careful.” Is that going to put out the fire? No! That’s just a wish.
Another person says, “Grab the fire extinguisher and spray it here!” That’s an action—concrete, immediate, and doable.

That’s the whole game. You simply ask: “Can I do this NOW, exactly as it’s written, without a magic wand?”

A Fun Real‑Life Story
Last summer, my nephew Sam saw that his cookie jar was empty. He was 11, and he loved cookies more than anything. The statement was: “The cookie jar is empty.”

He thought of three things to do:

“Someone should bake cookies.”

“I will call Grandma because she makes the best cookies, but she lives two hours away.”

“I’ll take my pocket money and walk to the corner shop to buy a pack right now.”

Sam realized the first one was a wish—who will bake them? The second needed too much time and a car ride—not immediate. The third was an action he could take now: he had money, the shop was near, and he could do it himself.

He came back with cookies in 10 minutes. That’s how you choose a course of action: pick the one that you can really carry out at that moment.

An Unforgettable Trick: The “Fire Extinguisher Test”
Whenever you see a course of action, imagine you’re standing in front of a small, manageable fire. Ask: “If this were a fire, would this suggestion put it out right now, or is it just someone telling me to be more careful next time?”

“Ban all fire” → Too extreme, impossible to enforce.

“Launch a fire safety campaign” → Good, but too slow for a fire happening NOW.

“Pour water on it” → Immediate, practical, right tool. That’s your winner.

So, the trick is: If it wouldn’t put out the fire in front of you, it’s probably not the right action for a problem that needs an immediate fix.

Blurting Sheet Instruction
Now, I want you to take a blank sheet of paper. Without looking back, write down everything you remember about how to spot a real course of action. Draw the fire example. Scribble the difference between a wish and an action. Don’t worry about neatness—just let it all out. This is called “blurting,” and it makes your brain stronger than re‑reading ten times.

Once you’re done, look back and see what you missed. Then fill it in with a green pen.

And here’s a question to chew on:
If a sign says “Wet floor,” and someone slips, which of these is an action—putting up a warning board or wiping the floor dry immediately? Why?

Think about it. The answer shows you’ve truly got it. Keep being curious!
