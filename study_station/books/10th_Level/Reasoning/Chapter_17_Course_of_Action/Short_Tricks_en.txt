🧠 Mnemonic 1: CLEAR — The Magic Word

To test any Course of Action, just shout "CLEAR!"
Concrete step (not a vague wish)
Linked directly to the problem
Extreme‑free (no “all”, “forever”, “destroy”)
Assumption‑free (don’t add your own story)
Relevant & practical (can a real authority do it?)

Outrageous image: Imagine a traffic cop holding a giant magnifying glass with the letters C‑L‑E‑A‑R on it. If the action passes, the glass turns green; if not, a loud siren screams “REJECTED!”

🎩 Mnemonic 2: VIP D — The Bouncer at the Club

When two options look alike, call VIP D to throw the bad ones out.
Vague → Bouncer: “Too blurry, you’re out!”
Irrelevant → Bouncer: “This isn’t your party!”
Punishment first → Bouncer: “Whoa, no violence before inquiry!”
Demands impossible → Bouncer: “Magic wand? Next please!”

Fun visual: Picture a stern bouncer in sunglasses holding a velvet rope. The options line up; only the non‑VIP‑D ones get inside the “Correct Answer” club.

🔥 Mnemonic 3: The Fire‑Extinguisher Test — “Would It Douse the Fire?”

Imagine a small fire blazing in front of you.
Ask: “If I apply this course of action, will it put out the fire RIGHT NOW?”

“Start an awareness campaign” → No, the fire burns while you print flyers.

“Ban all fire forever” → Impossible.

“Pour water on it” → Yes!

Outrageous memory: Picture a firefighter who throws a bucket of words onto the fire. Only the bucket labelled “Concrete Action” actually puts it out; the other buckets just make the flames laugh.

🧩 Mnemonic 4: The “Wish‑Action” Alarm

Every time you see words like:
“should be done”, “people must”, “it is hoped that”
Set off your mental alarm — WISH, NOT ACTION!

Crazy image: A genie pops out of a lamp and says “Your wish is my command!” But the genie’s list only has vague wishes. A second genie, the Action Genie, appears and says “Concrete steps only, please.” Only the Action Genie’s orders are accepted.

🔨 Mnemonic 5: The Hammer of Extremes

If an option contains extreme words, imagine hitting it with a giant hammer labelled “EXTREME — REJECT!”
Words like: all, every, forever, shut down everything, dismiss all, total ban.
The hammer smashes them instantly.

Visual: A judge in a courtroom uses a hammer instead of a gavel. Every time an extreme option is spoken, the hammer slams down with a loud “NO!”

🕵️ Mnemonic 6: The Detective of Assumptions

A detective named Sherlock CLEAR (yes, Sherlock) examines each option and asks:
“Is this based solely on the statement, or did you smuggle in your own assumption?”
If an option assumes something not stated, he says “Elementary, my dear Watson — REJECTED!”

Fun image: Sherlock Holmes holding a magnifying glass over the statement. Extra words outside the statement glow red and get crossed out.

🚦 Mnemonic 7: The Traffic Light of Decision

Red Light = Immediate rejection (extreme, vague, punishment first, impossible demand)

Yellow Light = Caution (it sounds okay but is long‑term when immediate action needed)

Green Light = CLEAR pass (concrete, linked, extreme‑free, assumption‑free, practical)

Remember: “Red, stop. Yellow, think. Green, go for it!”

🧟 Mnemonic 8: The Zombie “Both Follow” Trap

When two options look like they might both follow, imagine a zombie that says “Both… both…”
Ask yourself: “Are they truly independent and non‑contradictory, each solving a part of the problem without the other being unnecessary?”
If yes → both follow. If one is actually a vague wish or an extreme measure → the zombie is fooling you!

Crazy visual: A zombie with two heads, each whispering “Pick me!”. You must cut off the head that is extreme or irrelevant.

⏳ Mnemonic 9: The Time‑Travel Test

If the statement describes an urgent crisis (fire, accident, attack), ask:
“If I travel back in time to that exact moment, would this action help immediately?”

Long‑term policy → “Not now, future me will handle it.”

Immediate rescue → “Yes!”

Fun image: You’re holding a stopwatch. If the action takes more than seconds to start helping, the stopwatch breaks and the option is discarded.

📣 Mnemonic 10: The “3E” Trap Detector

Many traps fall into 3E:

Extreme

Emotional appeal (sounds good but not practical)

E‑wish (every “should” that is really a wish)

Outrageous memory: A three‑headed dragon named “EEE”. Whenever you see an option with any of these, the dragon breathes fire and the option burns.

🎯 Mnemonic 11: “Root, Not Symptom” — The Tree Analogy

If the problem is a sick tree, some actions treat the leaves (symptoms), others treat the roots.
Always pick the action that goes for the root.
Example: “Children malnourished” — giving supplements is root, asking them to be careful is symptom.

Visual: A giant tree with “ROOT CAUSE” written on the roots. A gardener with an axe tries to chop the leaves; you must point him to the roots.

🚨 Mnemonic 12: The Alarm Bell for “Advice Disguised as Action”

When you see phrases like:

“People should be made aware”

“They must be advised”

“A campaign should be launched to tell them”

Ring the alarm bell — Unless the problem is specifically ignorance, and the campaign is a concrete government‑run program, it’s probably just advice, not an action!

Crazy sound: Every time you read “advised”, a loud bell rings “DING! ADVICE ALERT!”

⏭️ Skip Strategy — When to Move On

1. The 15‑Second Rule
If within 15 seconds you cannot identify the core problem in the statement (e.g., the language is confusing, multiple issues are tangled), mark the question, skip it, and return later. Data‑heavy or emotionally charged statements often hide the core.

2. The 45‑Second Trap‑Detect
If you’re still debating between two options after 45 seconds, you’ve fallen into a trap. The examiner has placed an “almost correct” extreme, vague, or assumption‑based option next to the real one. Stop, quickly re‑apply CLEAR + VIP D, and if still unsure, pick the least extreme, most practical option, mark it, and move on.

3. The “No Concrete Step” Skip
If after reading the courses of action, none seems like a real administrative step — they are all wishes, advice, or extreme bans — and the pattern feels unfamiliar, skip immediately. This is rare but appears in high‑difficulty questions (like SSC CGL / MTS). The return with fresh eyes often reveals that neither follows.

Final note: Never spend more than 60 seconds on a Statement & Course of Action question. It’s a reasoning puzzle, not a life decision. Trust the mnemonics, apply the filters, and keep moving.
