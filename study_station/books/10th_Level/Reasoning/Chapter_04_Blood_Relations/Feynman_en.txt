The Wedding Puzzle: How a 10-Minute Map Saved the Day

Last winter, 12-year-old Rohan attended a huge family wedding. His aunt pointed to a girl across the hall and said, “She is the daughter of your grandfather’s only son.” Rohan froze. Cousin? Sister? Someone else? He almost hid in the bathroom.

Then his older cousin Priya pulled out a napkin and drew something. “Look, Rohan,” she said. “Your grandfather’s only son is your dad. So that girl is your dad’s daughter. That means she is your sister.” Rohan’s confusion melted. On the napkin was a tiny map that explained everything. That day Rohan learned a secret: relationships are not words — they are a picture waiting to be drawn.

The hardest concept in Blood Relations is not vocabulary. It’s holding a long chain of words in your head without getting tangled. “My mother’s brother’s wife’s father’s only granddaughter” sounds like a spell from a wizard’s book. But if you draw it with two simple shapes, it becomes as clear as a river.

The Lego Tower Trick

Think of a relationship chain as a Lego tower. Each block is a person. You don’t try to build the whole tower in your mind at once. You place one block at a time, clicking it onto the last one.

Here is the trick that never fails:

Grab a piece of paper. Make yourself (or the person speaking) a stick figure: a square if male, a circle if female.

Read the first relationship. Draw that person next to you with the correct shape and a line.

Read the next relationship. Draw that person connected to the last one you drew. Always attach the new person to the last person you added.

Never skip a word. The chain is: “mother’s brother’s wife”. So draw your mother first, then her brother, then his wife. Only then move to the next part.

When the chain ends, step back and look at your map. The answer will be sitting right there.

This is exactly how scientists solve messy problems — they don’t juggle everything in their heads. They draw a step-by-step path. Your pencil is a magic wand that untangles any knot.

The “Only” Superpower

Here is an extra unforgettable trick: whenever you see the word “only” in a question, treat it like a shortcut button. “My father’s only son” means there is no other son — so if the speaker is a male, that only son is the speaker himself. “My mother’s only daughter” — if the speaker is female, it’s her. One word shrinks the whole tree instantly. Circle it every time.

Why This Works for Every Exam

Examiners throw long, twisted sentences at you because they want to see if you panic or stay calm and draw. They are not testing your memory of a hundred relation names. They are testing your patience to build the map, one Lego block at a time. Be the kid who reaches for the napkin, not the bathroom door.

🧠 Blurting Sheet Challenge

Now close the book. Take a blank sheet of paper. Without looking, write down:

The two shapes you will use to draw males and females.

The three steps you will follow when you see a long relationship chain.

What you must do every time you see the word “only”.

Why you should always attach the new person to the last person you drew.

Once you’ve written everything you remember, open the chapter again and fill in what you missed with a different colored pen. This is called “blurting” — it tells your brain, “This is important, keep it!”
