🧩 Breaking the Code: Small Chunks That Stick
1. The 30‑Second Scan Rule

Before you touch pen to paper, scan the series and ask three questions:

Direction: Is it going up, down, or dancing (up‑down)?

Speed: Is the change slow (addition/subtraction), fast (multiplication/division), or explosive (squares/cubes)?

Gaps: Are the gaps constant or changing?

🧠 Active Recall: Look at this series for 5 seconds: 7, 14, 28, 56, ?. What type is it? (Answer: fast growth, so multiplication by 2.)

2. The Difference Ladder (Your Secret Weapon)

When addition/subtraction patterns aren’t obvious, write the differences between consecutive terms. If those differences still don’t show a constant pattern, take a second difference. This “ladder” reveals hidden arithmetic progressions.

Example: 4, 9, 16, 25, ?
Differences: +5, +7, +9 → these increase by 2 each time, so next difference is +11 → 36. (Notice these are also 2², 3², 4², 5², 6²!)

🧠 Active Recall: Build the difference ladder for: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30. What pattern do you see?

3. The Magic of Multiplication & Division

If numbers double, triple, or halve rapidly, multiplication or division is at play. But examiners love mixing them with a little addition/subtraction.

Pattern like ×2 +1: 3, 7, 15, 31… (each step: ×2+1)

Pattern like ÷2 +3: 20, 13, 9.5, 7.75… (hardly tested but possible)

Always check if the operation is clean multiplication first, then tweak with a constant.

🧠 Active Recall: Identify the missing term: 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, ?. (Answer: 63, pattern ×2+1)

4. Squares & Cubes: Your Memory Power

Squares (1,4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100,121,144,169,196,225) and cubes (1,8,27,64,125,216,343) appear so often that you must know them like your own phone number. Often the series is a square ± a number.
Example: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ? → 1²+1, 2²+1, 3²+1, 4²+1, 5²+1, so 6²+1=37.

🧠 Active Recall: Which square or cube lies behind this series? 0, 7, 26, 63, 124. (Answer: 1³‑1, 2³‑1, 3³‑1, 4³‑1, 5³‑1)

5. Letter Series: Turn Letters into Numbers

The alphabet is just numbers in disguise. Write A=1, B=2, …, Z=26. Then treat every letter series like a number series.

Simple forward: A, C, E, G… → +2 → next I.

Reverse: Z, X, V, T… → -2 → next R (remember Z=26, X=24).

Opposite pairs: A and Z, B and Y, C and X… because A(1) + Z(26) = 27. So if you see AZ, BY, CX, the next pair must sum to 27 with first letter +1: DW.

💡 Mnemonic Trick for Opposites:
“A to Z and B to Y, sum twenty-seven, don’t ask why!”
Also remember the word “AZBY” – it sounds like “As bee” – to instantly recall that A‑Z, B‑Y, C‑X, D‑W, etc., are paired.

🧠 Active Recall: What comes next: J, M, P, S, ? (Hint: convert to numbers). (Answer: V, because 10,13,16,19… +3 each time.)

6. The Alternate Series Trap (Two-in-One)

Some series hide two separate patterns – one for the odd positions and one for the even positions.
Example: 3, 10, 5, 20, 7, 30, ?.
Separate them: 3,5,7… (+2) and 10,20,30… (+10). So next term is 9 (odd position).
Always split the series visually: underline the 1st, 3rd, 5th… terms and circle the 2nd, 4th, 6th… terms. This small action prevents confusion.

🧠 Active Recall: Separate and find the next term: 2, 15, 4, 12, 6, 9, ?. (Answer: 8, because odd positions 2,4,6,8… +2; even positions 15,12,9… -3.)

7. The ‘Wrong Term’ Questions

Here the pattern is consistent except one term that breaks it. Never trust your gut; always verify the rule on at least three terms. Once you find the rule, apply it to the whole series and mark the misfit.
Example: 5, 10, 17, 26, 36, 50. Pattern n²+1: 2²+1=5, 3²+1=10, 4²+1=17, 5²+1=26, 6²+1=37 (not 36!), 7²+1=50. So 36 is wrong.

⚠️ Caution: Examiner’s Trap Box

Trap 1 – The “Double Relationship” Illusion
You see 3, 8, 15, 24, ?. It could be 2²‑1, 3²‑1, 4²‑1, 5²‑1 → 35. But the examiner might give options like 34, 35, 36, 37. If you mistakenly see 1×3, 2×4, 3×5, 4×6, 5×7=35 you still get 35. That’s fine, but sometimes one pattern gives two different answers. Never stop at the first pattern you spot. Check if the pattern you found works perfectly for all given terms without exception.

Trap 2 – “Fake Easy Start, Real Tough Middle”
The series begins with 2, 4, 8, 16… You happily mark 32 (×2), but the actual pattern is 2, 4, 8, 16, 31 (difference of differences revealing a cubic pattern). Always calculate differences beyond the obvious multiplication when you have 4+ terms. If a simple pattern breaks at the 3rd or 4th term, it’s a red flag.

Trap 3 – Letter Series “Reversal Goggles”
In a series like Z, X, V, T, ? you might quickly answer R (‑2). But what if the series is Z, X, V, T, Q because the pattern is ‑2, ‑2, ‑2, ‑3? Unlikely, but possible. To be safe, confirm that the gap stays consistent using the number conversion. Remember that the reverse position (Z=1 from back) is also a trap. Use the formula: Backward position = 27 – forward position. This helps you spot real reversals instantly.
