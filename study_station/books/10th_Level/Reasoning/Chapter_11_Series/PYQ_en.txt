📊 PYQ Analysis — Number/Letter Series (Foundation Level)

1. How Often This Topic Appears (Pattern, Not Numbers)

Number and Letter Series is a permanent fixture of SSC (CGL, CHSL), Banking (IBPS/SBI PO & Clerk), Railway (NTPC, Group D) and Delhi Police / State exams. Almost every paper of these exams carries a few questions from this chapter, so it can never be skipped in preparation.

Trend observations (qualitative):

- Banking exams (especially PO prelims) give this topic the most stable weightage — a fixed block of series questions appears in nearly every paper.
- SSC and Railway papers ask fewer questions but ask them every year, usually of easy-to-moderate difficulty.
- Delhi Police / State exams have reintroduced series questions in recent years after a gap, signalling renewed importance.
- Difficulty is slowly shifting from plain addition/subtraction series towards mixed operations and wrong-term detection, especially in Banking.

2. Sub-Topic Importance (What Is Asked Most)

Number Series — most to least frequent:

- Addition/Subtraction series (including difference ladders): the single most common type; easy; dominates SSC CGL, CHSL and Railway.
- Multiplication/Division series: easy to moderate; common in Banking and SSC.
- Square/Cube ± n series: moderate; a favourite of SSC CGL (especially Tier-2) and Banking.
- Mixed operations (×2+1, ×3−2 etc.): moderate; typical of Banking PO and SSC CGL.
- Wrong-term detection: moderate; a Banking PO and RRB NTPC speciality.
- Double/Alternate (interleaved) series: the hardest type; appears in Banking mains-level and CSAT-style papers.

Letter Series — most to least frequent:

- Forward/backward letter shift: the most common; easy; SSC CHSL and CGL.
- Pattern-based letter series (gaps increasing/decreasing): moderate; Banking and SSC.
- Opposite-pair (letter positions summing to 27, e.g. A–Z, B–Y): moderate; a recurring SSC favourite.
- Letter-to-number conversion: easy to moderate; seen in Delhi Police / State exams.

| Exam | Dominant Type | Unique Trait |

4. Ten High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions

Q1. (SSC CGL style): 0, 7, 26, 63, ?

Solution: Pattern = n³ − 1 (starting n = 1). 1³−1 = 0, 2³−1 = 7, 3³−1 = 26, 4³−1 = 63, so next = 5³−1 = 124.

🧠 Psychological Hook: "0, 7" — many students think it is n²−1 because 0 = 1²−1, but 7 is not 2²−1 (which is 3). The examiner deliberately uses numbers that fit two different initial patterns, knowing students will latch onto the first one. Always verify on at least 3 terms.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Attempt. The cube pattern emerges within 10 seconds if you know cubes up to 5. Do NOT skip.

Q2. (SSC CHSL style): 16, 18, 21, 26, 33, ?

Solution: Differences: +2, +3, +5, +7 → consecutive prime numbers. Next prime = 11, so 33 + 11 = 44.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The differences are prime numbers — but 2 is a prime. Many students misclassify 2 as "just even" and abandon the prime theory. The examiner exploits the common blind spot that 2 is the only even prime.

⏱️ Skip Rule: SKIP if >45 seconds. The prime-difference pattern is subtle; if you don't spot it within 30 seconds, the chance of a correct guess is low.

Q3. (SSC CGL style): 1, 3, 8, 19, 42, ?

Solution: Pattern = ×2+1, ×2+2, ×2+3, ×2+4, so next ×2+5: 42×2 + 5 = 89.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The pattern itself changes — the added constant increases (+1, +2, +3, …). Students who blindly test ×2+1 across all terms fail. The examiner banks on candidates testing only the first two terms and concluding wrongly.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Attempt. Write the operations term by term and the pattern appears in about 20 seconds.

Q4. (SSC CGL style): 95, 115, 145, 155, ?

Solution: 5×19 = 95, 5×23 = 115, 5×29 = 145, 5×31 = 155 — the multipliers are consecutive primes. Next prime after 31 is 37, so 5×37 = 185.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The numbers look random, but all are multiples of 5. The student who checks for a common factor cracks it; the one who goes straight to differences (+20, +30, +10) gets lost because the differences are inconsistent. The trap: a hidden common factor.

⏱️ Skip Rule: SKIP if >45 seconds and the difference ladder fails. Factor-based series are rare but time-consuming.

Q5. (SSC CGL style): 216, 209, 202, 522, 515, 508, 633, 626, ?

Solution: The series is three consecutive blocks of three terms each, and within every block the terms decrease by 7: (216, 209, 202), (522, 515, 508), (633, 626, ?). So ? = 626 − 7 = 619.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The series length (9 terms) and the sudden jump from 202 to 522 scream "split me into blocks!" But students conditioned to treat the whole series as one unit miss the block structure. The examiner exploits the habit of not counting terms and not checking for repeating groups.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Attempt. Spot the −7 pattern in the first block and you solve it in 20 seconds.

Q6. (SSC CGL style): X, Q, K, F, ?

Solution: Convert to forward alphabet positions (A = 1): X = 24, Q = 17, K = 11, F = 6. Differences: −7, −6, −5, so next difference −4 → 6 − 4 = 2 → B.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The gaps shrink steadily (−7, −6, −5), but students who try to "see" the letters without converting to numbers guess wrongly. Always convert letters to positions first.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Attempt. Letter-to-number conversion takes 10 seconds; the pattern emerges immediately.

Q7. (Banking PO style — find the wrong term): 5531, 5506, 5425, 5304, 5135, 4910, 4621

Solution: Differences: −25, −81, −121, −169, −225, −289. These should be squares of consecutive odd numbers: 5², 7², 9², 11², 13², 15² = 25, 49, 81, 121, 169, 225. The second difference should be −49 (7²), not −81. So 5506 is wrong — it should be 5531 − 49 = 5482. (After correction, all later differences also shift consistently.)

🧠 Psychological Hook: The squares look correct at a glance — 25, 81, 121 are all perfect squares. But the missing 49 (7²) is the trap. Banking examiners love wrong-term questions where the mistake is one omitted step in an otherwise perfect pattern. Students verify the first difference (25 = 5²) and assume everything is correct.

⏱️ Skip Rule: SKIP if >45 seconds. This needs second-level difference analysis. Attempt only if you have practised similar questions.

Q8. (RRB NTPC style — choose the incorrect term): 31, 37, 41, 43, 47, 51, 53

Solution: The series should be consecutive prime numbers: 31, 37, 41, 43, 47, 53, 59. But 51 is NOT prime (51 = 3 × 17). So 51 is the wrong term.

🧠 Psychological Hook: 51 looks "prime-ish" — it is odd and not obviously divisible. But its digit sum (5 + 1 = 6) is divisible by 3, so 51 is divisible by 3. The examiner exploits weak prime-number knowledge. Know primes up to 100 cold: 51, 57, 87 and 91 are classic traps.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Do NOT skip. A prime check takes 10 seconds if primes are memorised.

Q9. (CSAT / Tier-2 style): 24, X, 12, 18, 36, 90. Find X.

Solution: Ratios between consecutive known terms: 18/12 = 1.5, 36/18 = 2, 90/36 = 2.5 → multipliers increase by +0.5. Working backward, the earlier multipliers must be 1.0 and 0.5: 24 × 0.5 = 12, then 12 × 1.0 = 12. So X = 12.

🧠 Psychological Hook: The sudden drop from 24 to 12 is deliberately confusing. Most students test addition/subtraction first, but the plateau (12, 12) breaks that idea. The "drop then steady rise" shape is a signature trap. The student who thinks in multipliers, not differences, wins.

⏱️ Skip Rule: SKIP if >45 seconds. If the ratio method does not click within 30 seconds, mark for review and move on.

Q10. (CSAT / Tier-2 style): 1, 3, 6, 11, 18, X, 42. Find X.

Solution: Differences: 2, 3, 5, 7, ?, ? — consecutive primes. Next prime after 7 is 11, so X = 18 + 11 = 29. Verify: 29 + 13 = 42. ✓

🧠 Psychological Hook: Same prime-difference trap as Q2, but here the options are usually close together (e.g. 26, 27, 29, 30). A student who forgets that 2 is prime and starts from 3 gets 27 — which is deliberately placed in the options. The "false answer in the options" is the most dangerous trap in all series questions.

⏱️ Skip Rule: Attempt if you know primes (20 seconds). If unsure and the exam has negative marking, skip rather than guess.

7. Key Takeaways

- SSC CGL & CHSL: Master Square/Cube ± n and the Difference Ladder — the bulk of SSC series questions come from these.
- Banking PO: Mixed operations (×2+1, ×3−2 etc.) and wrong-term detection dominate. Practise "difference of difference."
- Railway: Basic arithmetic series with small gaps. Speed matters — aim for 15–20 seconds per question.
- Delhi Police / State exams: Conceptual patterns — ratios, primes, hidden factors. Options are deliberately close; verify on all terms.
- Universal Trap: The "Fake Easy Start" — the first 2–3 terms suggest one pattern, but the 4th term breaks it. Always verify on a minimum of 4 terms.
- Prime Numbers: Know primes up to 100 (especially the traps: 51, 57, 87, 91). They appear in SSC, Banking and CSAT-style papers.
- Letter Series: The "sum = 27" opposite-pair rule (A–Z, B–Y, …) recurs regularly in SSC letter-series questions.
