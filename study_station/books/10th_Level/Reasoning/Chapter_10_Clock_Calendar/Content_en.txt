Why Clock & Calendar Feels Tricky

Most students treat Clock as pure geometry and Calendar as pure memorisation. The truth? Clock is a game of relative speed. Calendar is a system of remainders. Once you shift from “I must remember everything” to “I’ll understand the core rule,” the whole topic lightens.

We will go step by step, in tiny, digestible chunks. After every 2–3 chunks, I’ll ask you an Active Recall question (look for 🧠). Do not skip them. They force your brain to retrieve the rule, which is how real learning sticks.

CLOCK
Chunk 1 – The Two Speeds You Must Know

Minute hand (MH) covers 360° in 60 minutes → 6° per minute.

Hour hand (HH) covers 360° in 12 hours (720 minutes) → 0.5° per minute.

🧠 Stop. Close your eyes. Recall: MH speed = ?°, HH speed = ?° per minute.

Chunk 2 – Relative Speed: The Magic Number 5.5

The minute hand gains on the hour hand every minute.
Gain per minute = 6° – 0.5° = 5.5°.

This one number solves everything—coincidence, opposite, right angle.

To gain 360° (a full lap, i.e., hands coincide again), time = 360 / 5.5 = 65⁵⁄₁₁ minutes.

In 12 hours, they coincide 11 times.

In 12 hours, they are opposite 11 times, at right angles 22 times.

🧠 If the hands are together now, after how many minutes will they next be together?

Chunk 3 – Angle Between Hands Formula

The formula you must own:
θ = | 30H – 5.5M |
(where H = hour as per clock face, M = minutes)

If θ > 180°, take 360° – θ for the smaller angle.

Example: 2:20 → |30×2 – 5.5×20| = |60 – 110| = 50°.

🧠 Find the angle at 7:25 using the formula. Do it now before reading further.

Chunk 4 – Mirror and Water Images

Mirror image time (what you see in a mirror): Subtract the given time from 11:60.
e.g., 3:25 → 11:60 – 3:25 = 8:35.

Water image time (reflection in water): Subtract from 18:30 or think of it as 17:90 minus the given time.
e.g., 3:25 → 18:30 – 3:25 = 15:05, which is 3:05 PM.

Mnemonic: Mirror → Minus from Midnight (11:60). Water → Work with 18:30 (half past six). Silly, but it stays.

🧠 The clock shows 4:40. What is its mirror image time?

Chunk 5 – Faulty Clock

A faulty clock gains/loses time. The relationship:
True time : Clock time = True interval : Clock interval
If a clock gains 5 minutes in 24 hours, the ratio of true time to clock time is 24×60 : 24×60+5 = 1440:1445.

So if the faulty clock shows 10:00 AM after 24 true hours, true time = 10:00 AM × (1440/1445) – adjust accordingly. But most questions ask “when will it show correct time again?” That happens after it has gained/lost exactly 12 hours (720 minutes). Time = 720 / gain per day × 1 day.

🧠 *A watch gains 6 minutes per day. After how many days will it show the right time again? (720/6 = 120 days)*

CALENDAR
Chunk 1 – Odd Days: The Backbone

Ordinary year: 365 days → 52 weeks + 1 odd day.

Leap year: 366 days → 52 weeks + 2 odd days.

Leap year rule: Divisible by 4. Century years (like 1900) must be divisible by 400 to be leap. 2000 was leap, 1900 was not.

🧠 *How many odd days in 3 ordinary years? (3 × 1 = 3 odd days, i.e., 3 days shift forward)*

Chunk 2 – Century Codes for Speed

Memorise these four numbers:

Century	Code
1600–1699	6
1700–1799	4
1800–1899	2
1900–1999	0
2000–2099	6

For most competitive exams (1900s and 2000s), just remember: 1900s = 0, 2000s = 6. That’s enough.

Chunk 4 – Day Calculation Formula

Day = (Date + Month Code + Year Code + Century Code) mod 7

Year Code = (Last two digits of year + ⌊last two digits/4⌋) mod 7.
Then add century code.

After mod 7: 0 = Sunday, 1 = Monday, … 6 = Saturday. (Check your reference; this works for a common system where 0 = Sunday.)

Example: 15th August 1947
Date = 15, Month code (Aug) = 2, Year (47) → 47 + 11 (⌊47/4⌋) = 58 mod 7 = 2 (since 56 is multiple of 7). Century code (1900) = 0. Sum = 15+2+2+0 = 19 → 19 mod 7 = 5 → Friday. Yes, India’s Independence Day was a Friday.

🧠 Calculate 26th Jan 1950. (Republic Day was Thursday. Try it.)

Chunk 5 – Same Calendar Year

For an ordinary year, the calendar repeats after 6 years (sometimes 11). Leap year calendar repeats after 28 years. In exams, they often ask: “Which year after 2024 will have the same calendar as 2024?” 2024 is leap, so 2024 + 28 = 2052.

🧠 *If 2025 is ordinary, the same calendar will be 2031? Yes, because 2025 odd days = 1, 2026:2, 2027:3, 2028 (leap):5, 2029:6, 2030:0, 2031:1 — after 6 years the odd day matches and the 2028 leap does not disturb.* (Wait, careful: after a leap year the gap changes. But for simplicity, the 6-year rule holds for many ordinary years between leaps; always calculate odd days if uncertain.)

⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

Options with multiple plausible angles
When you apply formula |30H – 5.5M|, you may get a number like 132°. The examiner will place 132°, 228°, 48°, and 130° as options. The trap: They want the smaller angle, so you must choose 132° if it’s ≤180°, else 360–θ. They also keep a slight off-by-one-degree option (130° instead of 132°) to catch those who approximate. Always calculate exact decimal: 5.5 × M exactly.

Difficult wording to confuse mirror/water
“What time will a clock show in water if the clock’s reflection in water shows 3:40?” This double reflection question means you do the water image twice, or they just give a reflected time and ask for real time. Many students apply mirror rule instead of water rule. The trick: Water = subtract from 18:30; Mirror = subtract from 11:60. Underline the word “water” or “mirror” before touching numbers.

Dual relationships in Calendar – same date, different century
“Which year after 2004 will have the same calendar?” The straightforward 11-year shift fails because 2000 was a century leap, altering odd days. The examiner sets options: 2010, 2011, 2012, 2015. You must calculate exact odd day pattern instead of blindly adding 6 or 11. The trap preys on those who memorize a rigid pattern without checking leap year effects.

“Hands overlap” vs “hands coincide” in 12-hour period
The phrasing “How many times do the hands of a clock overlap between 1 PM and 11:59 PM?” They expect 10 times (since at 12 they coincide exactly at 12:00 AM next day, not within). If the question says “from 1 to 12”, the count is 11, but they’ll put 11 and 10 both as options. Read boundaries.
