Clock & Calendar
Learning Goals

Identify when hands meet, are opposite, or at right angles—without drawing a clock face.

Determine the day of any date—past or future—confidently.

Spot examiner traps and never get tricked by similar-looking options.

Build speed so that you solve a Calendar sum in under 30 seconds and a Clock sum in under 45 seconds.

Why Clock & Calendar Feels Tricky

Most students treat Clock as pure geometry and Calendar as pure memorisation. The truth? Clock is a game of relative speed. Calendar is a system of remainders. Once you shift from “I must remember everything” to “I’ll understand the core rule,” the whole topic lightens.

We will go step by step, in tiny, digestible chunks. After every 2–3 chunks, I’ll ask you an Active Recall question (look for 🧠). Do not skip them. They force your brain to retrieve the rule, which is how real learning sticks.

CLOCK
Chunk 1 – The Two Speeds You Must Know

Minute hand (MH) covers 360° in 60 minutes → 6° per minute.

Hour hand (HH) covers 360° in 12 hours (720 minutes) → 0.5° per minute.

🧠 Stop. Close your eyes. Recall: MH speed = ?°, HH speed = ?° per minute.

Chunk 2 – Relative Speed: The Magic Number 5.5

The minute hand gains on the hour hand every minute.
Gain per minute = 6° – 0.5° = 5.5°.

This one number solves everything—coincidence, opposite, right angle.

To gain 360° (a full lap, i.e., hands coincide again), time = 360 / 5.5 = 65⁵⁄₁₁ minutes.

In 12 hours, they coincide 11 times.

In 12 hours, they are opposite 11 times, at right angles 22 times.

🧠 If the hands are together now, after how many minutes will they next be together?

Chunk 3 – Angle Between Hands Formula

The formula you must own:
θ = | 30H – 5.5M |
(where H = hour as per clock face, M = minutes)

If θ > 180°, take 360° – θ for the smaller angle.

Example: 2:20 → |30×2 – 5.5×20| = |60 – 110| = 50°.

🧠 Find the angle at 7:25 using the formula. Do it now before reading further.

Chunk 4 – Mirror and Water Images

Mirror image time (what you see in a mirror): Subtract the given time from 11:60.
e.g., 3:25 → 11:60 – 3:25 = 8:35.

Water image time (reflection in water): Subtract the given time from 18:30.
e.g., 3:25 → 18:30 – 3:25 = 15:05, which is 3:05 PM.

Mnemonic: Mirror → Minus from Midnight (11:60). Water → Work with 18:30 (half past six). Silly, but it stays.

🧠 The clock shows 4:40. What is its mirror image time?

Chunk 5 – Faulty Clock

A faulty clock gains or loses time at a steady rate. The key relationship compares elapsed times:

True elapsed time = Clock elapsed time × (True minutes per cycle / Faulty minutes per cycle)

Example: A clock gains 5 minutes every 24 hours. So while 1445 minutes pass on this clock, only 1440 minutes of true time have passed. If this clock shows that 1445 minutes have elapsed since it was set right, the true elapsed time = 1445 × (1440/1445) = 1440 minutes = exactly 24 hours.

The most common exam question: “When will it show the correct time again?” A clock shows the correct time again after it has gained (or lost) exactly 12 hours = 720 minutes.
Days needed = 720 ÷ (minutes gained or lost per day).

🧠 A watch gains 6 minutes per day. After how many days will it show the right time again? (720 ÷ 6 = 120 days)

CALENDAR
Chunk 1 – Odd Days: The Backbone

Ordinary year: 365 days → 52 weeks + 1 odd day.

Leap year: 366 days → 52 weeks + 2 odd days.

Leap year rule: Divisible by 4. Century years (like 1900) must be divisible by 400 to be leap. 2000 was leap, 1900 was not.

🧠 How many odd days in 3 ordinary years? (3 × 1 = 3 odd days, i.e., 3 days shift forward)

Chunk 2 – Century Codes for Speed

Memorise these numbers:

Century	Code
1600–1699	6
1700–1799	4
1800–1899	2
1900–1999	0
2000–2099	6

For most competitive exams (1900s and 2000s), just remember: 1900s = 0, 2000s = 6. That’s enough.

Chunk 4 – Day Calculation Formula

Day = (Date + Month Code + Year Code + Century Code) mod 7

Year Code = (Last two digits of year + ⌊last two digits/4⌋) mod 7.
Then add century code.

After mod 7: 0 = Sunday, 1 = Monday, … 6 = Saturday.

Example: 15th August 1947
Date = 15, Month code (Aug) = 2, Year (47) → 47 + ⌊47/4⌋ = 47 + 11 = 58 → 58 mod 7 = 2. Century code (1900s) = 0. Sum = 15 + 2 + 2 + 0 = 19 → 19 mod 7 = 5 → Friday. Yes, India’s Independence Day in 1947 was a Friday.

🧠 Calculate 26th January 1950. (It was a Thursday. Try it.)

Chunk 5 – Same Calendar Year

A year’s calendar repeats when the total odd days accumulated in between become a multiple of 7 AND the leap/ordinary nature matches.

Ordinary years usually repeat after 6 or 11 years; a leap year’s calendar repeats after 28 years.

Example: “Which year after 2024 will have the same calendar as 2024?” 2024 is a leap year, so 2024 + 28 = 2052.

🧠 If 2025 is ordinary, will 2031 have the same calendar? Yes: odd days from 2025 to 2030 add up to 1+1+1+2+1+1 = 7 (a multiple of 7), and both 2025 and 2031 are ordinary years. When in doubt, always count odd days instead of blindly adding 6 or 11.

⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

Options with multiple plausible angles
When you apply the formula |30H – 5.5M|, you may get a number like 132°. The examiner will place 132°, 228°, 48°, and 130° as options. The trap: they want the smaller angle, so if θ > 180° you must take 360° – θ. They also keep a slightly-off option (130° instead of 132°) to catch those who approximate. Always compute 5.5 × M exactly, never round early.

Confusing mirror and water wording
“What time will a clock show in water if the clock’s reflection in water shows 3:40?” Many students apply the mirror rule instead of the water rule, or panic at the double reflection. The trick: Water = subtract from 18:30; Mirror = subtract from 11:60. Underline the word “water” or “mirror” before touching numbers.

Same-calendar questions across leap boundaries
“Which year after 2004 will have the same calendar as 2004?” Blindly adding 6 or 11 fails here: 2004 is a leap year, so it can only match another leap year whose odd-day total in between is a multiple of 7 — that happens after 28 years (2032). Options like 2010, 2011, 2015 trap students who memorised a rigid “+6 or +11” pattern without checking whether the year is leap. Always check the leap/ordinary nature first, then count odd days.

“Hands overlap” count within given boundaries
“How many times do the hands of a clock coincide between 1 PM and 11 PM?” The hands coincide roughly every 65⁵⁄₁₁ minutes, but the count depends on the exact boundaries given. Examiners place both the inclusive and exclusive counts (e.g., 10 and 11) as options. Read the time limits carefully and check whether a coincidence falls exactly on a boundary.
