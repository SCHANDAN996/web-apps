# Conjunction — The Glue of English Sentences

## 🔗 Why This Chapter Matters

Imagine two railway coaches without a coupling. Each coach is fine on its own, but the train cannot move. Sentences are the same: *"He was tired. He kept working."* — two separate coaches. Add one small word — **but** — and the train moves: *"He was tired, but he kept working."* That small word is a **Conjunction**, and examiners at SSC, Railway, Bank and State exams love testing it because one wrong connector flips the whole meaning of a sentence.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Define a conjunction and identify it in any sentence.
2. Classify conjunctions into **Coordinating, Subordinating and Correlative** types.
3. Apply the **12 Golden Rules** of conjunctions to spot errors in exam questions.
4. Avoid the classic traps examiners set (e.g., *"Although… but"*, *"Unless… not"*).
5. Solve error-spotting and fill-in-the-blank questions within the 15/45-second rule.

---

## 1. What is a Conjunction?

A **conjunction** is a word that **joins** words, phrases, or clauses (groups of words with a subject and a verb).

- *Ram **and** Shyam are friends.* (joins two words)
- *She is poor **but** honest.* (joins two qualities)
- *I waited **because** the train was late.* (joins two clauses)

Think of a conjunction as a **bridge**. The two sides of the bridge must match in strength — that idea is the heart of almost every exam question on this topic.

## 2. The Three Families of Conjunctions

### Family 1: Coordinating Conjunctions — the "Equal Partners"

These join two **equal** parts (two words, two phrases, or two independent clauses). Remember them with the mnemonic:

> **FANBOYS** — **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o

- *He was ill, **so** he stayed home.*
- *Do you want tea **or** coffee?*

### Family 2: Subordinating Conjunctions — the "Boss and Servant"

These join a **dependent (subordinate) clause** to a **main clause**. The dependent clause cannot stand alone.

Common ones: *because, since, as, although, though, if, unless, until, while, when, after, before, that, whether, so that, in order that, lest.*

- ***Although** it was raining, the match continued.*
- *Work hard **lest** you should fail.*

### Family 3: Correlative Conjunctions — the "Twin Pairs"

These always work **in pairs**. Both partners must appear, and they must be the correct partners!

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| either … or | *Either come in or go out.* |
| neither … nor | *Neither Ram nor Shyam came.* |
| both … and | *Both Sita and Gita sang.* |
| not only … but also | *He is not only rich but also kind.* |
| whether … or | *I don't know whether he will come or not.* |
| no sooner … than | *No sooner did she arrive than the show began.* |
| hardly/scarcely … when | *Hardly had I slept when the phone rang.* |
| such … as / such … that | *Such was his speed that none could catch him.* |

## 3. The 12 Golden Rules of Conjunctions ⭐

1. **Although/Though** is never followed by **but**. Use *yet* or nothing.
   ❌ *Although he is rich, but he is miserly.* ✅ *Although he is rich, yet he is miserly.*
2. **Since** (reason) is followed by the main clause without *therefore*. ❌ *Since he was ill, therefore he left.* ✅ *Since he was ill, he left.*
3. **Because** and **so** never come together. ❌ *Because he was late, so he missed the bus.*
4. **Unless** means "if…not" — never add *not* after it. ❌ *Unless you do not work, you will fail.* ✅ *Unless you work, you will fail.*
5. **Until** refers to **time**; **unless** refers to **condition**. *Wait until I return.* / *Unless you try, you cannot win.*
6. **Lest** is always followed by **should** (never "will/would", never "not"). *Run fast lest you should miss the train.*
7. **Neither** pairs with **nor**; **either** pairs with **or**. ❌ *Neither Ram or Shyam.*
8. **No sooner** takes **than**; **hardly/scarcely** takes **when**. ❌ *No sooner had he come when…*
9. **Not only … but also** — the same part of speech must follow both. ❌ *He not only plays cricket but also football.* ✅ *He plays not only cricket but also football.*
10. **Both** is never followed by *as well as* in the same breath. ❌ *Both Ram as well as Shyam.* ✅ *Both Ram and Shyam.* / *Ram as well as Shyam.*
11. **The reason … is because** is wrong; use **that**. ✅ *The reason he failed is that he did not study.*
12. **As if / As though** take a past-tense (unreal) clause. *He talks as if he were a king.*

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Double Conjunction:** Examiners insert *but* after *although*, or *so* after *because*, because it "sounds right" to Hindi speakers (*"yadyapi … phir bhi"*). In English, one connector is enough.

**Trap 2 — The Wrong Twin:** *Neither … or*, *No sooner … when*, *Hardly … than* — the pairs are deliberately swapped. Memorise the fixed pairs; never trust your ear.

**Trap 3 — The Hidden Negative:** *Unless, until, lest* already carry a negative idea. Adding *not* creates a double negative and reverses the meaning — a favourite error-spotting item.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi से English

| In Hindi we think… | Wrong English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| "यद्यपि वह गरीब है, फिर भी ईमानदार है" | *Although he is poor, but he is honest.* | *Although he is poor, yet he is honest.* |
| "क्योंकि वह बीमार था, इसलिए नहीं आया" | *Because he was ill, so he did not come.* | *Because he was ill, he did not come.* |
| "जब तक तुम नहीं जाओगे…" | *Until you do not go…* | *Until you go…* |
| "वह ऐसे बोलता है जैसे वह राजा है" | *He talks as if he is a king.* | *He talks as if he were a king.* |

Hindi uses **two** connectors (*yadyapi–phir bhi*, *kyunki–isliye*); English uses **one**. This single difference causes most conjunction errors by Indian candidates.

## 📊 Summary Table

| Type | Job | Key Words | Golden Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coordinating | Joins equals | FANBOYS | *He tried, but failed.* |
| Subordinating | Joins dependent to main | because, although, if, unless, until, lest | *Unless you run, you will miss it.* |
| Correlative | Fixed twin pairs | either…or, neither…nor, no sooner…than | *Neither tea nor coffee.* |

**Memory Hook:** *"One bridge, one builder"* — two clauses need only one conjunction; if two appear, one is an intruder.
