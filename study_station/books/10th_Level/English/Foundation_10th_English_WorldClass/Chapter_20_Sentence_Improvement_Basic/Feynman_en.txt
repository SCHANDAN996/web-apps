🧒 FEYNMAN TECHNIQUE — Sentence Improvement (Basic)

The Hardest Concept, Explained to a 12-Year-Old

The trickiest part of Sentence Improvement is this: the sentence SOUNDS fine when you read it, but it is still wrong. Why? Because our ears are trained by Hindi, not by English grammar rules.

Imagine you have a bicycle. The chain must fit the gear exactly — one tooth off, and the cycle makes noise. A sentence is the same. The SUBJECT is the gear, the VERB is the chain. They must match exactly. This matching is called Subject-Verb Agreement, and it is the heart of most Sentence Improvement questions.

A Daily-Life Example

Suppose your friend Ravi has two dogs. You say:

❌ "Ravi, along with his two dogs, are going to the park."

It sounds okay, right? Because "dogs" is sitting right next to the verb, your ear says "dogs are going." But WAIT — who is really going? RAVI. The phrase "along with his two dogs" is just extra information, like a side-car attached to a bike. The bike is still driven by one person.

✅ "Ravi, along with his two dogs, IS going to the park."

The Unforgettable Trick: The "Cover-Up Test"

Whenever a long phrase comes between the subject and the verb, COVER IT WITH YOUR FINGER and read the sentence again.

- "The quality of the mangoes (cover: of the mangoes) WAS good." ✔
- "Each of the boys (cover: of the boys) HAS a pen." ✔
- "One of my friends (cover: of my friends) LIVES in Delhi." ✔

If the sentence still makes sense after covering the middle part, you have found the real subject. Now match the verb to THAT subject only.

Three More "Sound-Right" Traps a 12-Year-Old Can Catch

1. "He don't like tea." → Sounds fine in fast speech, but "he" needs "doesn't": ✅ "He doesn't like tea." (He/She/It = verb + s/es)
2. "I am having a car." → You cannot "be having" ownership. ✅ "I have a car." (Verbs like have, know, believe, love don't take -ing for states.)
3. "What you are doing?" → Hindi order sneaks in! In English questions, the helper verb jumps in front: ✅ "What are you doing?"

Why This Works

Your brain learns grammar the way it learns cycling — not by reading rules, but by noticing the wobble. Every time you catch a "sound-right but rule-wrong" sentence, your brain builds a new alarm bell. After 50 such catches, the correct form will start sounding right too.
