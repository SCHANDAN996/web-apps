# Sentence Improvement (Basic)

## Why This Chapter Can Change Your Score

Imagine two candidates in the SSC GD or RRB Group D exam hall. Both know the same grammar. But one reads a sentence, *feels* something is wrong, and freezes. The other runs a quick mental checklist — subject, verb, tense, agreement — and marks the answer in 20 seconds. The difference is not talent. It is **method**.

Sentence Improvement questions give you a sentence with an underlined part and ask: *which option makes it correct?* The good news? You don't need to know all of English grammar. You need to spot a handful of repeating errors — and examiners repeat the same traps year after year.

**Learning Objectives — after this chapter you will be able to:**
1. Identify the 5 most common error types in underlined sentence parts.
2. Apply the 12 Golden Rules to eliminate wrong options fast.
3. Avoid the classic traps Hindi-speaking learners fall into.
4. Decide in seconds whether to attempt or skip a question.

---

## Part 1: What Is a Sentence Improvement Question?

You are shown a sentence like this:

> *He **do not** like tea.*
> (a) does not (b) did not (c) is not (d) No improvement

Your job: check if the underlined part is grammatically correct. If yes, choose "No improvement." If no, pick the option that fixes it — **without changing the meaning**.

Here, "He" is singular, so "do not" is wrong. The answer is **(a) does not**.

The golden principle: **Fix only what is broken. Never improve what is already correct.** Many students lose marks by "improving" a correct sentence because an option *sounds* fancier.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In the sentence "She go to school daily," what is wrong, and what is the fix? (Answer: "go" → "goes" — singular subject "She" needs a singular verb.)

---

## Part 2: The 5 Error Types That Cover Most Questions

**1. Subject–Verb Agreement.** The verb must match the subject in number.
- *The list of items **are** on the table.* ❌ → *The list of items **is** on the table.* ✔
- Trap: the real subject is "list," not "items." Words between the subject and verb are there to confuse you.

**2. Tense Consistency.** The time of the action must be clear and consistent.
- *Yesterday I **go** to the market.* ❌ → *Yesterday I **went** to the market.* ✔
- Time words are your clues: *yesterday* → past; *since 2010* → present perfect; *daily/always* → simple present.

**3. Wrong Verb Form after Modals.** After can/could/should/must/will, always use the base verb (V1).
- *He can **swims**.* ❌ → *He can **swim**.* ✔

**4. Articles (a/an/the).** "A" before consonant sounds, "an" before vowel sounds, "the" for specific things.
- *He is **a** honest man.* ❌ → *He is **an** honest man.* ✔ (Sound matters, not spelling!)

**5. Prepositions.** Fixed pairs must be memorised: *afraid **of**, good **at**, depend **on**, listen **to**, married **to**.*
- *She is good **in** singing.* ❌ → *She is good **at** singing.* ✔

🧠 **Active Recall:** Fix these without looking above: (i) "He must goes now." (ii) "I am afraid from dogs." (Answers: (i) must *go*; (ii) afraid *of*.)

---

## Part 3: The "Sound-Right" Danger

Your ear is trained on Hindi and spoken English, so wrong sentences often *sound* fine. "He don't know" sounds natural in films — but it is wrong in the exam ("He doesn't know"). **Trust rules, not your ear.** When two options both sound okay, check subject–verb agreement first; it is the examiner's favourite hiding place.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why is "One of my friends **are** a doctor" wrong? (Answer: the subject is "One," which is singular → "One of my friends **is** a doctor.")

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic: **"S-V-T-M-A-P"** — *"Smart Verifiers Take My Answer Paper"*

Run this checklist on every underlined part:
- **S**ubject–Verb agreement
- **V**erb form (V1 after modals, V2 for past)
- **T**ense matches the time clue
- **M**eaning unchanged
- **A**rticle correct (a/an/the)
- **P**reposition is the fixed partner

---

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Intervening Phrase:** *"The quality of the mangoes **were** good."* The subject is "quality" (singular), not "mangoes." Correct: **was**. Examiners plant a plural noun right before the verb to bait you.

**Trap 2 — The Sound-Right Option:** *"Neither of the boys **have** come."* Sounds fine, but "neither" is singular → **has**. If it sounds natural in casual speech, double-check it.

**Trap 3 — The Over-Improvement:** The underlined part is already correct, but an option looks "more formal." If the original follows all rules, confidently choose **No improvement**. Examiners include it in roughly a fair share of questions — don't fear it.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge: Hindi से English

| हम हिंदी से सोचकर कहते हैं ❌ | Correct English ✔ |
|---|---|
| What you are doing? | What **are you** doing? (question = verb before subject) |
| He is having a car. | He **has** a car. (possession = simple present) |
| I am knowing the answer. | I **know** the answer. (know/like/believe — no -ing) |
| He is more taller than me. | He is **taller** than me. (never "more + -er") |
| My all friends came. | **All my** friends came. |
| She is my cousin sister. | She is my **cousin**. (English has no "cousin sister") |
| I and Ram went. | **Ram and I** went. (yourself last — politeness rule) |

## 📋 Summary Table

| Error Type | Signal to Watch | Quick Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Subject–Verb | Phrase between subject & verb | Find the *real* subject |
| Tense | yesterday, since, daily, already | Match verb to time clue |
| Modal + Verb | can, must, should, will | Always base verb (V1) |
| Article | a/an before noun | Check the *sound*, not spelling |
| Preposition | afraid, good, depend, listen | Memorise fixed pairs |
| Double Comparative | more + -er | Use only one |
| No Improvement | Sentence passes all checks | Don't over-fix! |
