# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Idioms & Phrases

## How Exams Test This Chapter

Idioms & Phrases is one of the most **predictable** vocabulary areas in SSC (CGL, CHSL, GD, MTS), RRB (NTPC, Group D), Bank (IBPS, SBI) and State exams. The pattern has stayed remarkably stable over the years:

- **Direct meaning questions** dominate: an idiom is given, and you pick its correct meaning from four options. This is the standard format in SSC CGL/CHSL Tier-I and RRB NTPC.
- **Reverse format**: the meaning is given in a sentence, and you choose the idiom that fits. Common in IBPS and SSC MTS.
- **Sentence-usage format**: a sentence uses an idiom (sometimes underlined), and you identify what it conveys. Seen in SSC CGL Tier-II and Bank mains.
- **Fill-in-the-blank with idioms**: choose the idiom that completes the sentence logically. Frequent in CHSL and State exams.

## Which Sub-Topics Repeat Most Often

Based on the recurring pattern across exams, the highest-yield idiom families are:

1. **Body-part idioms** — *by hook or by crook, to bite the dust, to wash one's dirty linen in public, to have a bee in one's bonnet, to face the music.*
2. **Colour idioms** — *once in a blue moon, red tape, a white elephant, black sheep, green with envy.*
3. **Animal idioms** — *to let the cat out of the bag, a wild goose chase, to kill two birds with one stone, a dark horse, to rain cats and dogs.*
4. **Death/difficulty idioms** — *to kick the bucket, to bite the dust, a dead letter, at sixes and sevens, in hot water.*
5. **Money & success idioms** — *to burn a hole in the pocket, to make both ends meet, a feather in one's cap, to hit the jackpot.*

## The Examiner's Traps (Read This Twice)

- **Trap 1 — Literal Meaning Lure:** The most common trap. One option always states the *literal* meaning of the words. For "to let the cat out of the bag", the wrong option says "to free a cat". Never pick the literal option.
- **Trap 2 — Sound-Right Confusion:** Two options sound plausible; only one matches the *established* meaning. E.g., "a white elephant" = a costly but useless possession, NOT "a rare and precious thing".
- **Trap 3 — Similar-Looking Idioms:** Examiners mix up idioms that share words — "to bite the dust" (to fail/die) vs "to eat humble pie" (to apologise); "at sixes and sevens" (in confusion) vs "on cloud nine" (very happy).
- **Trap 4 — Over-thinking:** Foundation-level idioms are direct. If you know the meaning, mark it and move on. Do not invent a "deeper" interpretation.

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions)

**Q1. "To let the cat out of the bag" means:**
(a) To free a trapped animal
(b) To reveal a secret
(c) To create a disturbance
(d) To buy something expensive
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to disclose a secret, usually accidentally. Option (a) is the classic **Literal Meaning Lure** — reject any option that takes the words at face value.
**Trap type:** Literal Meaning Lure | **Source:** PYQ-style (recurs across SSC CGL/CHSL)

**Q2. "Once in a blue moon" means:**
(a) On a rare occasion
(b) Every month
(c) During a lunar eclipse
(d) At midnight
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** A "blue moon" is a rare event, so the idiom means "very rarely". Option (c) is the literal trap; (b) reverses the meaning.
**Trap type:** Literal Meaning Lure | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q3. "To bite the dust" means:**
(a) To eat in a hurry
(b) To suffer defeat or failure
(c) To clean the floor
(d) To work very hard
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to fail, be defeated, or die. Do not confuse it with "to eat humble pie" (to apologise) — a **Similar-Looking Idioms** trap.
**Trap type:** Similar-Looking Idioms | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q4. "A white elephant" means:**
(a) A rare and precious gift
(b) A symbol of peace
(c) A costly but useless possession
(d) A royal animal
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** A white elephant is expensive to maintain but of no real use. Option (a) is the **Sound-Right** trap — "rare and precious" feels positive, but the idiom is negative.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right Confusion | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q5. "To burn the midnight oil" means:**
(a) To waste resources
(b) To work or study late into the night
(c) To light lamps at night
(d) To stay awake due to worry
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom refers to working or studying till late night (from the days of oil lamps). Option (d) is a **Sound-Right** trap — staying awake is not the same as working.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right Confusion | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q6. "At sixes and sevens" means:**
(a) In a state of confusion
(b) In perfect order
(c) Very lucky
(d) Counting carelessly
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The idiom means disorderly or confused. Do not confuse with "on cloud nine" (extremely happy) — number idioms are often mixed up in options.
**Trap type:** Similar-Looking Idioms | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q7. "To kill two birds with one stone" means:**
(a) To be cruel to animals
(b) To achieve two aims with a single effort
(c) To waste one's energy
(d) To take unnecessary risks
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** One action accomplishing two results. Option (a) is the literal trap — idioms about animals are never about animals.
**Trap type:** Literal Meaning Lure | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q8. "Red tape" means:**
(a) A festive decoration
(b) Official delay due to excessive formalities
(c) A warning signal
(d) A type of legal document
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "Red tape" refers to bureaucratic delay and rigid official procedures. Option (d) is the **Sound-Right** trap — it is related to offices but is not a document.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right Confusion | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q9. "To face the music" means:**
(a) To attend a concert
(b) To enjoy oneself
(c) To face the consequences of one's actions
(d) To criticise someone's performance
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to accept punishment or unpleasant consequences. Option (a) is the literal trap.
**Trap type:** Literal Meaning Lure | **Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10. "A blessing in disguise" means:**
(a) A hidden curse
(b) A good thing that seemed bad at first
(c) A religious ceremony
(d) A gift from an unknown person
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Something that appears unfortunate but turns out to be beneficial. Option (a) reverses the meaning — a common **Over-thinking** trap where students assume "disguise" must mean something negative.
**Trap type:** Over-thinking | **Source:** PYQ-style

## Applying the 15/45-Second Rule

**Q2 ("Once in a blue moon") — 15-second question.**
This is pure recall. You either know the idiom or you don't. Read the idiom → recall meaning ("very rarely") → scan options → mark (a). Do not re-read the options twice. Target: 15 seconds.

**Q10 ("A blessing in disguise") — 45-second question.**
Here the options are designed to make you think. Break it down: "blessing" = good, "in disguise" = hidden. So: a good thing that was hidden (looked bad). Now match with options — (b) fits exactly; (a) is the reverse; (d) is a distractor built from "unknown". This reasoning justifies 40–45 seconds. If still unsure after 45 seconds, eliminate the literal and reversed options, mark the best fit, and move on.

**Rule of thumb:** Direct-meaning idioms = 15 sec (Fact-based). Idioms with reversed/hidden logic or sentence-usage questions = 45 sec (Inference-based). Never spend more than 45 seconds on any idiom question — the next question is easier marks.
