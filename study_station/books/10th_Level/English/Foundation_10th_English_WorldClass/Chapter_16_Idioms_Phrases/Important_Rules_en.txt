# 📋 Important Rules & Tips — Idioms & Phrases

## (a) 12 High-Yield Rules

| # | Rule | Example | Examiner's Trap |
|---|------|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | An idiom's meaning is **never** the sum of its words. Translate the whole phrase, not word-by-word. | "Kick the bucket" = to die (not to hit a bucket) | Options always include a literal meaning — it is the most attractive wrong answer. |
| 2 | Idioms are **fixed expressions** — you cannot change the words or the word order. (Tense may change with the sentence: "kicked the bucket" is fine.) | "Beat about the bush" ✓; "Beat around the tree" ✗ | A near-correct option with one word changed is a classic trap. |
| 3 | Learn idioms with a **context sentence**, not in isolation. | "He let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party." | Exams test usage in a sentence; memory of meaning alone fails in "choose the correct sentence" questions. |
| 4 | Distinguish **similar-looking idioms** — one word changes everything. | "A white elephant" (a costly, useless possession) vs "a white lie" (a harmless lie) | Examiners pair look-alike idioms in the same question. |
| 5 | **Phrasal verbs** (verb + preposition) are tested like idioms; the particle decides the meaning. | "Put off" = postpone; "put out" = extinguish | Same verb + different particle options (off/out/up/on) confuse rushed readers. |
| 6 | Some idioms are **always negative or ironic** in tone. | "A white elephant" = a costly, useless possession | Students pick a positive option because "elephant" sounds grand. |
| 7 | **Colour/animal/body-part idioms** form big exam families — learn them in groups. | "Once in a blue moon" (rarely); "a black sheep" (a disgrace) | Mixing families: "red-handed" (caught in the act) confused with "blue-blooded" (royal). |
| 8 | **One-word substitution vs idiom**: an idiom is a phrase; a single word is not an idiom. | "To burn the midnight oil" (idiom) vs "nocturnal" (one word) | Questions swap the two formats to test whether you read the instruction. |
| 9 | In "choose the correct meaning" questions, **eliminate the literal option first**. | "Break the ice" → literal "to crack frozen water" is the decoy | The literal option is placed early to catch 15-second skimmers. |
| 10 | **Prepositions inside idioms are fixed** — no logic, only memory. | "In high spirits" (not "with high spirits") | Wrong-preposition options look perfectly grammatical. |
| 11 | Idioms of **foreign origin** (Latin/French) appear in CGL/CHSL — learn the common few. | "Alma mater" = one's old school/college; "bona fide" = genuine | Students skip these assuming they are "too advanced" — they are frequent. |
| 12 | When two options seem close, match the idiom's **emotional tone** (positive/negative/neutral) with the sentence. | "His speech was a feather in his cap" (achievement — positive) | Tone-blind guessing between two plausible meanings. |

## (b) Time Management — The 15/45-Second Rule

| Type | What it looks like | Time | Strategy |
|------|-------------------|------|----------|
| **Fact-based (15 sec)** | "What is the meaning of 'to bite the dust'?" — direct recall of a known idiom | 15 sec | Answer instantly if known; if unknown, mark and move on — thinking longer will not create the memory. |
| **Fact-based (15 sec)** | Match the idiom / choose the correct phrasal verb particle | 15 sec | Eliminate the literal option, pick the memorised meaning. |
| **Inference-based (45 sec)** | Idiom used inside a long sentence; you must judge tone and context | 45 sec | Read the full sentence, decide positive/negative tone, then match. |
| **Inference-based (45 sec)** | "Which sentence uses the idiom correctly?" — 4 full sentences to check | 45 sec | Check each sentence for fixed form (Rule 2) and tone (Rule 12); eliminate one by one. |

**Golden rule:** Never spend 45 seconds on a recall question you simply don't know — skip it and return only if time remains.

## (c) Morphology — Roots & Affixes that Unlock Idioms and Vocabulary

| Root / Affix | Meaning | Derived Words | Idiom Connection |
|--------------|---------|---------------|------------------|
| **dict** (Latin) | say, speak | dictate, predict, verdict, dictionary | "Word of mouth" — speech-based expressions |
| **port** (Latin) | carry | transport, export, portable, support | "Carry weight" = to be influential |
| **manu** (Latin) | hand | manual, manufacture, manuscript | "To have the upper hand"; "hand in glove" |
| **capit/cap** (Latin) | head | capital, captain, decapitate | "A feather in one's cap"; "to put on one's thinking cap" |
| **cord** (Latin) | heart | cordial, accord, discord | "Heart to heart talk"; "to take heart" |
| **ped/pod** (Greek) | foot | pedal, pedestrian, podium | "To put one's foot down"; "cold feet" |
| **-phobia** (Greek) | fear | claustrophobia, hydrophobia | "Once bitten, twice shy" — fear-based idioms |
| **anti-** (Greek prefix) | against | antidote, antisocial, antonym | "To go against the grain" |
| **re-** (Latin prefix) | again | rebuild, return, revise | "To turn over a new leaf" — starting again |
| **mal-** (Latin prefix) | bad | malnutrition, malfunction, malice | "A bad egg" = a dishonest person |

**How to use this table:** When an unknown idiom contains a body part or action word (hand, heart, foot, head), recall the root family — the idiom's meaning almost always links to that root's core sense. This converts a blind guess into an informed elimination.
