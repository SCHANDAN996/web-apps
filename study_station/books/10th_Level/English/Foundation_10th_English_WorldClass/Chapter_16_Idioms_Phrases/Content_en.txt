# Idioms & Phrases

## 🎯 Why This Chapter Matters

Imagine this: the exam paper says *"He let the cat out of the bag."* You know every word — cat, bag, out — yet the sentence makes no sense. That is exactly where most students lose marks, and where **you** will gain them. Idioms are fixed expressions whose meaning is **not** the sum of their words. SSC, Railway, Bank and State exams ask them again and again — and the beautiful part? The same set of high-frequency idioms keeps rotating across papers. Learn them once, score forever.

**Learning Objectives** — after this chapter you will be able to:
1. Recognise that idioms have a *figurative* meaning, not a literal one.
2. Recall the highest-frequency idioms asked in SSC/Railway/Bank exams.
3. Guess an unknown idiom's meaning using context and word clues.
4. Avoid the examiner's favourite traps (literal-meaning options, similar-sounding idioms).
5. Use Greek/Latin roots to decode unfamiliar vocabulary inside idioms.

---

## 1. What Exactly Is an Idiom?

An **idiom** is a group of words whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of the individual words. It is fixed — you cannot change its words or their order.

- *To kick the bucket* = to die (not to hit a bucket with your foot!)
- *A piece of cake* = something very easy
- *Under the weather* = feeling ill

A **phrase** is a small group of words that acts as a unit but keeps its literal meaning: *in the morning*, *a bunch of keys*. In exams, "Idioms & Phrases" questions almost always test the **figurative** meaning.

**Why can't we translate idioms word-by-word?** Because idioms are born from culture, history and stories. "To burn the midnight oil" comes from the days of oil lamps — studying late into the night. If you translate it literally into any language, the meaning dies.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — (1) What is an idiom? (2) What does "a piece of cake" mean? (3) Why does literal translation fail?

## 2. The Golden Categories — Learn Idioms in Families

Don't memorise idioms randomly. Group them; the brain remembers families, not strangers.

**🐾 Animal idioms**
| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A black sheep | A disgraceful member of a family/group |
| To kill two birds with one stone | To achieve two things with one action |
| A wild goose chase | A useless search |
| To let the cat out of the bag | To reveal a secret |
| To rain cats and dogs | To rain very heavily |

**🎨 Colour idioms**
| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Once in a blue moon | Very rarely |
| Red tape | Official delay; excessive formalities |
| A white lie | A harmless lie |
| Green with envy | Very jealous |
| In black and white | In writing; clearly stated |

**🍽️ Food idioms**
| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A hot potato | A controversial issue |
| To spill the beans | To disclose a secret |
| The apple of one's eye | Someone very dear |
| To bring home the bacon | To earn a living |
| In a nutshell | In brief |

**🧍 Body idioms**
| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| To bite the dust | To fail; to be defeated |
| By hook or by crook | By any means, fair or foul |
| To face the music | To accept punishment/criticism |
| To wash one's hands of | To refuse responsibility |
| A bone of contention | A cause of dispute |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the tables. Write meanings of: *red tape, a hot potato, to bite the dust, once in a blue moon*. Check yourself.

## 3. The "Story Method" — How to Remember Any Idiom

Your brain forgets lists but remembers **pictures and stories**. For every idiom, create a 2-second mental movie.

**Mnemonic Example — "To burn the midnight oil":**
Picture yourself at 2 a.m., an old oil lamp burning on your desk, books open, exam tomorrow. The lamp = late-night hard work. You will never forget it now.

**Mnemonic — "To sit on the fence":**
Imagine a man literally sitting on a fence between two fields, unable to jump to either side = **unable to decide; neutral**.

Make your own movies for 5 idioms daily. Steady daily practice builds a bank of idioms far larger than any exam will ever ask.

## 4. Word Roots Inside Idioms — Decode the Unknown

Many idioms and the words around them carry Greek/Latin roots. Learn the root, unlock dozens of words.

| Root | Meaning | Derived Words |
|---|---|---|
| **dict** (Latin) | say, speak | dictate, predict, verdict, dictionary |
| **port** (Latin) | carry | transport, export, portable, support |
| **spect** (Latin) | see, look | inspect, respect, spectator, prospect |
| **cred** (Latin) | believe | credible, credit, incredible, creed |
| **rupt** (Latin) | break | interrupt, erupt, bankrupt, corrupt |
| **manu** (Latin) | hand | manual, manufacture, manuscript |
| **bene** (Latin) | good | benefit, benevolent, benediction |
| **mal** (Latin) | bad | malice, malfunction, malnutrition |

**📖 Roots in Context — Read this passage:**

> When the bank went *bankrupt*, inspectors arrived to *inspect* the accounts. The manager's earlier *prediction* that the scheme would *benefit* everyone proved false. His *malicious* deals, done through *manual* entries to avoid digital records, had *corrupted* the whole system. The court's *verdict* was severe, and the news was *transported* across the country within hours.

Notice how one root-family (rupt, spect, dict, bene, mal, manu, port, cred) quietly powers the entire paragraph. In the exam, even if an idiom is new, a familiar root inside it whispers the answer.

🧠 **Active Recall:** What do *dict, rupt, cred* mean? Give one word for each.

## 5. How Exams Test You — The 4 Question Styles

1. **Direct meaning:** "What does 'to burn the midnight oil' mean?" → four options, one correct.
2. **Idiom for a meaning:** "To reveal a secret" → choose the correct idiom.
3. **Sentence completion:** "The minister's statement ___ and the scandal became public." (let the cat out of the bag)
4. **Usage in a sentence:** pick the sentence where the idiom is used correctly.

Styles 1 and 2 are **15-second questions** (pure recall). Styles 3 and 4 may need **45 seconds** (read the full sentence, check the context).

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Literal-Meaning Option.** For "to kick the bucket," one option will say "to strike a bucket with the foot." It *sounds* silly, yet under pressure students pick it because it matches the words. **Rule: the literal option is almost always wrong.**

**Trap 2 — Similar-Sounding Idioms.** *To eat humble pie* (to apologise humbly) vs *to eat one's words* (to take back what one said). *At sixes and sevens* (in confusion) vs *on cloud nine* (very happy). Examiners mix these in options. Learn confusing pairs **together**.

**Trap 3 — Changed Words.** "To let the dog out of the bag" is **wrong** — idioms are frozen. Only *cat* is correct. If an option changes even one word of the standard idiom, reject it.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi Speakers, Watch Out!

| In Hindi we think… | Wrong English | Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| "जीवन के उतार-चढ़ाव" | an ups and downs of life | the **ups and downs** of life (no 'an'; it means good and bad times) |
| "मुझे बहुत खुशी हुई" | I was on the cloud nine | I was **on cloud nine** (no 'the') |
| "उसने रात-रात मेहनत की" | He burnt midnight's oil | He **burnt the midnight oil** |
| "यह मेरे लिए बहुत आसान है" | It is a cake piece for me | It is **a piece of cake** for me |

**Golden rule:** Never build an idiom from Hindi. Learn the English idiom as one fixed block, like a single long word.
