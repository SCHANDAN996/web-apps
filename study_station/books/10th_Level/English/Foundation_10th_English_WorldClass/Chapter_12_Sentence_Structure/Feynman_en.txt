# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Sentence Structure, Explained Like You're 12

## The Hardest Concept: Subject–Verb Agreement (and why your ear betrays you)

Imagine you're carrying a tray of samosas to your friends. The rule of the tray is simple: **the tray decides how many hands you need, not the samosas on it.**

A sentence works the same way. The **subject is the tray**. The **verb is your hands**. Everything between them — "of the boys", "along with his friends", "as well as the teacher" — those are just samosas sitting on the tray. They look heavy, they look important, but they change **nothing**.

### The Daily-Life Example

Say this out loud:

> "The **box** of chocolates **is** on the table."

Your ear screams *"chocolates are!" because "chocolates" is the word right next to the verb. But stop. What is actually on the table? The **box**. The chocolates are just inside it. The box is the tray; the chocolates are the samosas.

Now the exam version:

> "The **quality** of the mangoes **was** good." ✅
> (Not "were" — "of the mangoes" is just a samosa sitting on the tray.)

### The Unforgettable Trick: **"Cover the Middle"** 🫣

Whenever a sentence feels confusing, do this physically in the exam hall (with your eyes, not your hand!):

1. Find the verb.
2. Ask "who/what is doing it?" — that's your real subject.
3. **Mentally cover everything between the subject and the verb** with your thumb.
4. Now match: singular tray → singular hand (is/was/has); plural tray → plural hands (are/were/have).

> "The players, along with the captain, **were** felicitated."
> Cover "along with the captain" → "The players … were" ✅

**Golden memory line:** *"With, along with, as well as, together with, in addition to — these are hitchhikers. They ride the sentence but never drive it."* The verb only listens to the first subject.

## Second Hardest: What Even IS a Sentence?

A sentence is like a complete WhatsApp message. It needs:

- **Someone** (Subject) — who are we talking about?
- **Something happening or being** (Verb/Predicate) — what about them?
- **A complete thought** — the reader shouldn't be left asking "...and then what?"

| Fragment (incomplete message) | Full Sentence |
|---|---|
| "Because he was late." | "Because he was late, he missed the bus." |
| "Running in the park." | "She is running in the park." |

**Trick:** Read it aloud. If it sounds like you got cut off mid-sentence, it's a fragment. Exams love hiding fragments as options in "spot the error" questions.

## Third: The Four Sentence Types — The Family Analogy 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦

Think of a family of four brothers:

1. **Simple** — the loner. One subject, one verb, one complete thought. *"Ravi plays cricket."
2. **Compound** — twins joined by a handshake (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). *"Ravi plays cricket, **and** Mohan plays football."
3. **Complex** — big brother + little brother who can't stand alone. One independent clause + one dependent clause (joined by because, although, when, if, since). *"**Although** it rained, the match continued."
4. **Compound-Complex** — the joint family: at least two independent clauses + at least one dependent. *"Although it rained, the match continued, **and** the crowd cheered."

**Mnemonic:** *"Simple stands Solo, Compound needs a Comma-buddy, Complex has a Clinger."

**Exam shortcut:** Count the **finite verbs**. One finite verb = Simple. Two independent clauses = Compound. Spot "because/although/since/when" + only one independent clause = Complex.

## ⚠️ The Three Traps That Fool 12-Year-Olds and Toppers Alike

1. **The Sound-Right Trap:** "Each of the boys **have** a pen" *sounds* fine. It's wrong. "Each" is always singular → **has**. Your ear is not a grammar book.
2. **The Inversion Trap:** "Here **comes** the bus" — the subject (bus) comes *after* the verb. Don't let word order flip your answer.
3. **The There-Trap:** "There **are** three books on the desk" — "there" is never the subject. Look *after* the verb for the real subject.
