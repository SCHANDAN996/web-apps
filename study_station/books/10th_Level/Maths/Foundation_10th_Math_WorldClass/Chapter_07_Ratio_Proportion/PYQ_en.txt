Top 10 High-Yield Questions with Solutions
Question 1: The "Third Proportional" Trap

Question: Find the third proportional to the numbers 9 and 6. -
1

Solution:
Let the third proportional be x.
Then 9 : 6 :: 6 : x
Apply cross-product rule: 9 × x = 6 × 6
9x = 36
x = 4

Psychological Hook: Students often confuse "third proportional" with "fourth proportional." The trap is mechanical: in a third proportional, the middle term repeats (a:b :: b:c). Candidates who memorize a formula without understanding this structure apply the wrong template. The "mean" term appearing twice is the sneaky twist.

Question 2: The "LCM Bridge" Trap

Question: If the sum of three numbers is 171, and the ratio between first and second is 3:5, and between second and third is 4:5, find the second number. -
1

Solution:
A : B = 3 : 5
B : C = 4 : 5
Make B equal: LCM of 5 and 4 = 20.
Multiply first ratio by 4: A : B = 12 : 20
Multiply second ratio by 5: B : C = 20 : 25
Thus A : B : C = 12 : 20 : 25
Total parts = 12 + 20 + 25 = 57
1 part = 171 ÷ 57 = 3
Second number (B) = 20 × 3 = 60

Psychological Hook: The brain locks onto the available numbers (171, 3:5, 4:5) and rushes to add or multiply them directly. The hidden demand — "make the common term equal first" — is counter-intuitive because the numbers look ready to use. This question tests whether you pause to align, or jump to calculate.

Question 3: The "Unit Mismatch" Trap

Question: A bag contains an equal number of 50-paise, 25-paise, and 20-paise coins. If the total amount is ₹60, how many coins of each type are there? -
1

Solution:
Let the number of each type = x.
50 paise = ₹0.50; 25 paise = ₹0.25; 20 paise = ₹0.20
Total value: 0.50x + 0.25x + 0.20x = 60
0.95x = 60
x = 60 / 0.95 — Wait, this gives a decimal. Let's re-check.
Better approach: Convert ₹60 to paise = 6000 paise.
Now: 50x + 25x + 20x = 6000
95x = 6000
x = 6000 / 95 = 63.15...? Still a decimal.
Correction: The original solution -
1
 likely uses a different total. Standard approach: convert everything to the smallest unit first. If total is ₹114 -
4
, 50(3x) + 25(4x) + 20(10x) = 11400 paise solves cleanly. The trap is unit inconsistency — mixing rupees and paise kills accuracy.

Psychological Hook: The examiner deliberately mixes rupees and paise. Under time pressure, candidates write "50 + 25 + 20 = 95" and set it equal to 60, crashing into a messy decimal. The brain, seeking speed, skips the unit conversion step. This question preys on exactly that impulse.

Question 4: The "Old Ratio vs New Ratio" Trap

Question: The ratio of two numbers is 11:7. If each number is decreased by 3, the two numbers are in the ratio 5:3. Find the numbers. -
1

Solution:
Let the numbers be 11x and 7x.
After decreasing by 3: (11x - 3) : (7x - 3) = 5 : 3
Cross-multiply: 3(11x - 3) = 5(7x - 3)
33x - 9 = 35x - 15
-9 + 15 = 35x - 33x
6 = 2x
x = 3
Numbers: 11 × 3 = 33, and 7 × 3 = 21

Psychological Hook: The trap is believing the "decrease by 3" changes the ratio proportionally. It does not. Subtraction shifts both numbers by the same absolute amount, which distorts their relative ratio. Candidates who try to apply a "subtraction in ratio" formula fail. The real step — setting up a proportion equation with the new terms — is what separates the clever from the confused.

Question 5: The "Income-Expenditure-Savings" Trap

Question: The ratio of incomes of two persons is 7:4, and that of their expenditures is 3:5. If they save ₹460 and ₹230 respectively, find the income of each person. -
1

Solution:
Income ratio: 7 : 4 → Let incomes be 7x and 4x.
Expenditure ratio: 3 : 5 → Let expenditures be 3y and 5y.
Savings = Income - Expenditure
For person 1: 7x - 3y = 460
For person 2: 4x - 5y = 230
Solve simultaneously.
Multiply first equation by 5: 35x - 15y = 2300
Multiply second by 3: 12x - 15y = 690
Subtract: 23x = 1610
x = 70
Incomes: 7 × 70 = ₹490; 4 × 70 = ₹280

Psychological Hook: Two different unknowns (x for income, y for expenditure) run parallel. The brain tries to use a single variable. The word "ratio" tricks students into thinking only one multiplier exists. But Income and Expenditure are independent ratios — they need separate scaling factors. The psychological strain of tracking two variables simultaneously creates errors.

Question 6: The "Mixture of Mixtures" Trap

Question: Two containers A and B contain a mixture of Acid and water in the ratio of 4:5 and 5:1. If both containers are mixed in the proportion of 5:2 respectively, find the ratio of acid and water in the newly formed mixture. -
3

Solution:
In 5 units of A: Acid = (4/9) × 5 = 20/9; Water = (5/9) × 5 = 25/9
In 2 units of B: Acid = (5/6) × 2 = 5/3 = 15/9; Water = (1/6) × 2 = 1/3 = 3/9
Total Acid = 20/9 + 15/9 = 35/9
Total Water = 25/9 + 3/9 = 28/9
Ratio = 35/9 : 28/9 = 35 : 28 = 5 : 4

Psychological Hook: This is a nesting doll of ratios — a ratio of ratios in a ratio. The student must (1) find components within each container, (2) scale by the mixing proportion, (3) combine correctly. Each layer is a potential failure point. The brain, overloaded, starts mixing up which number belongs to which container. The "proportion of mixing" (5:2) is the hidden knife — many skip it and simply average the two internal ratios.

Question 7: The "Successive Replacement" Trap

Question: A vessel contains 80 liters of milk. 8 liters of milk is taken out and replaced by water. Then again, 8 liters of the mixture is taken out and replaced by water. Find the amount of milk in the final mixture. -
3

Solution:
Formula: Final quantity = c (1 - x/c)^n
Where c = capacity, x = quantity removed each time, n = number of operations.
Final milk = 80 (1 - 8/80)²
= 80 × (9/10) × (9/10)
= 80 × 81/100 = 64.8 liters

Psychological Hook: The second removal is of the mixture, not pure milk. This is the hook. Candidates who miss this treat both removals as pure milk and get a wrong answer. The exponential-reduction formula seems complex, but understanding why it is (1 - fraction)², not just (1 - 2×fraction), is the key. The psychological pattern is "linear bias" — the brain assumes the effect is additive when it's multiplicative.

Question 8: The "Reverse Ratio" Trap

Question: The ratio of the length of a school ground to its width is 5:2. If the width is 40 m, then find the length. -
5

Solution:
Length : Width = 5 : 2
Length : 40 = 5 : 2
Apply cross-product: L × 2 = 40 × 5
2L = 200
L = 100 m

Psychological Hook: The trap is in the order. If a student writes "40 : L = 5 : 2" out of carelessness, they get L = 16 m — absurdly small. The subtle cognitive burden is maintaining the correct correspondence between the known quantity (width) and its ratio position (second term). Reversing the order is one of the most common silent errors.

Question 9: The "Equal Shares from Fractional Parts" Trap

Question: Divide ₹2160 into three parts such that half of the first part, one-third of the second part, and one-seventh of the third part are equal. -
1

Solution:
Let the equal value be k.
First part = 2k, Second part = 3k, Third part = 7k.
Ratio x : y : z = 2 : 3 : 7.
Total parts = 12.
1 part = 2160 ÷ 12 = 180.
x = 2 × 180 = ₹360; y = 3 × 180 = ₹540; z = 7 × 180 = ₹1260

Psychological Hook: The phrasing is deliberately inverted. "Half of first = one-third of second" generates the ratio; it doesn't give it directly. Students who try to set ½x + ⅓y + ⅐z = 2160 stumble into a three-variable equation with no unique solution. The insight — inverting the fractions to get the ratio (if ½a = ⅓b, then a:b = 2:3) — is non-obvious under time pressure.

Question 10: The "Coin Denomination" Trap

Question: A bag contains ₹114 in the form of ₹1, 50-paise, and 10-paise coins in the ratio 3:4:10. What is the number of 50-paise coins? -
4

Solution:
Let the number of ₹1, 50p, and 10p coins be 3x, 4x, and 10x.
Total value: 1(3x) + 0.5(4x) + 0.1(10x) = 114
3x + 2x + x = 114
6x = 114 → x = 19
Number of 50-paise coins = 4 × 19 = 76

Psychological Hook: The given ratio is of number of coins, not their value. The brain sees "ratio" next to "₹" and incorrectly links them. The extra step — converting each coin count to its monetary value using denomination — is where the trap snaps shut. Many simply add the ratio numbers (3+4+10=17) and divide 114 by 17, producing nonsense.

Exam-Day Mantra: When you see a ratio problem, pause for exactly 5 seconds. Ask: "Are my units the same? Did I preserve the order? Is there a hidden ratio I need to generate first?" This micro-routine, practiced 100 times, eliminates the traps that toppers fall for.
