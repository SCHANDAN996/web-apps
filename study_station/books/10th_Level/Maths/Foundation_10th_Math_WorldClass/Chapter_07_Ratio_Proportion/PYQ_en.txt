📊 PYQ Analysis: Ratio & Proportion
Level: Foundation | Language: English

Pattern Analysis: How Exams Test This Chapter

Based on a review of the style of questions asked in SSC, Railway, Bank, and State-level exams, Ratio & Proportion questions cluster around a few recurring sub-topics:

- Basic ratio simplification and combining two or three ratios into one (the "LCM bridge") — appears in some form almost every time the chapter is tested.
- Proportional distribution — dividing an amount in a given ratio, including fractional conditions.
- Income–Expenditure–Savings problems with two independent ratios.
- Mixture and alligation, including successive replacement of liquid.
- Coin-denomination problems where a ratio of counts must be converted into value.
- Age problems and "old ratio vs new ratio after adding/subtracting a fixed number."

Difficulty has shifted over the years from direct one-step ratio division toward multi-step problems that combine two ideas (e.g., a mixture problem that also needs the LCM bridge). Examiners increasingly rely on traps — near-correct statements, reversed order, unit mismatches — rather than harder arithmetic.

Key Insight: Mixture problems and income–expenditure–savings problems are asked far more often now than a decade ago, while plain "divide X in ratio a:b" questions have become rarer. Combining two or three ratios into a single ratio remains a consistent presence.

Top 10 High-Yield Questions with Solutions

Question 1: The "Third Proportional" Trap

Question: Find the third proportional to the numbers 9 and 6.

Solution:
Let the third proportional be x.
Then 9 : 6 :: 6 : x
Apply the cross-product rule: 9 × x = 6 × 6
9x = 36
x = 4

Psychological Hook: Students often confuse "third proportional" with "fourth proportional." The trap is mechanical: in a third proportional, the middle term repeats (a : b :: b : c). Candidates who memorize a formula without understanding this structure apply the wrong template. The "mean" term appearing twice is the sneaky twist.

Question 2: The "LCM Bridge" Trap

Question: If the sum of three numbers is 171, and the ratio between the first and second is 3:5, and between the second and third is 4:5, find the second number.

Solution:
A : B = 3 : 5
B : C = 4 : 5
Make B equal: LCM of 5 and 4 = 20.
Multiply the first ratio by 4: A : B = 12 : 20
Multiply the second ratio by 5: B : C = 20 : 25
Thus A : B : C = 12 : 20 : 25
Total parts = 12 + 20 + 25 = 57
1 part = 171 ÷ 57 = 3
Second number (B) = 20 × 3 = 60

Psychological Hook: The brain locks onto the available numbers (171, 3:5, 4:5) and rushes to add or multiply them directly. The hidden demand — "make the common term equal first" — is counter-intuitive because the numbers look ready to use. This question tests whether you pause to align, or jump to calculate.

Question 3: The "Unit Mismatch" Trap

Question: A bag contains an equal number of 50-paise, 25-paise, and 20-paise coins. If the total amount is ₹95, how many coins of each type are there?

Solution:
Let the number of coins of each type = x.
Convert the total to paise first: ₹95 = 9500 paise.
Total value: 50x + 25x + 20x = 9500
95x = 9500
x = 100
So there are 100 coins of each type.

Psychological Hook: The examiner deliberately mixes rupees and paise. Under time pressure, candidates write "50 + 25 + 20 = 95" and set it equal to 95 (rupees), crashing into a wrong answer. The brain, seeking speed, skips the unit-conversion step. This question preys on exactly that impulse: convert everything to the smallest unit before you touch the algebra.

Question 4: The "Old Ratio vs New Ratio" Trap

Question: The ratio of two numbers is 11:7. If each number is decreased by 3, the two numbers are in the ratio 5:3. Find the numbers.

Solution:
Let the numbers be 11x and 7x.
After decreasing by 3: (11x − 3) : (7x − 3) = 5 : 3
Cross-multiply: 3(11x − 3) = 5(7x − 3)
33x − 9 = 35x − 15
−9 + 15 = 35x − 33x
6 = 2x
x = 3
Numbers: 11 × 3 = 33, and 7 × 3 = 21

Psychological Hook: The trap is believing the "decrease by 3" changes the ratio proportionally. It does not. Subtraction shifts both numbers by the same absolute amount, which distorts their relative ratio. Candidates who try to apply a "subtraction in ratio" shortcut fail. The real step — setting up a proportion equation with the new terms — is what separates the careful from the confused.

Question 5: The "Income–Expenditure–Savings" Trap

Question: The ratio of incomes of two persons is 7:4, and that of their expenditures is 3:5. If they save ₹460 and ₹230 respectively, find the income of each person.

Solution:
Income ratio: 7 : 4 → Let incomes be 7x and 4x.
Expenditure ratio: 3 : 5 → Let expenditures be 3y and 5y.
Savings = Income − Expenditure
For person 1: 7x − 3y = 460
For person 2: 4x − 5y = 230
Solve simultaneously.
Multiply the first equation by 5: 35x − 15y = 2300
Multiply the second by 3: 12x − 15y = 690
Subtract: 23x = 1610
x = 70
Incomes: 7 × 70 = ₹490; 4 × 70 = ₹280

Psychological Hook: Two different unknowns (x for income, y for expenditure) run in parallel. The brain tries to use a single variable. The word "ratio" tricks students into thinking only one multiplier exists. But income and expenditure are independent ratios — they need separate scaling factors. The strain of tracking two variables simultaneously creates errors.

Question 6: The "Mixture of Mixtures" Trap

Question: Two containers A and B contain a mixture of acid and water in the ratio 4:5 and 5:1 respectively. If both containers are mixed in the proportion 5:2, find the ratio of acid and water in the newly formed mixture.

Solution:
In 5 units of A: Acid = (4/9) × 5 = 20/9; Water = (5/9) × 5 = 25/9
In 2 units of B: Acid = (5/6) × 2 = 5/3 = 15/9; Water = (1/6) × 2 = 1/3 = 3/9
Total Acid = 20/9 + 15/9 = 35/9
Total Water = 25/9 + 3/9 = 28/9
Ratio = 35/9 : 28/9 = 35 : 28 = 5 : 4

Psychological Hook: This is a nesting doll of ratios — a ratio of ratios in a ratio. The student must (1) find components within each container, (2) scale by the mixing proportion, (3) combine correctly. Each layer is a potential failure point. The "proportion of mixing" (5:2) is the hidden knife — many skip it and simply average the two internal ratios.

Question 7: The "Successive Replacement" Trap

Question: A vessel contains 80 litres of milk. 8 litres of milk is taken out and replaced by water. Then again, 8 litres of the mixture is taken out and replaced by water. Find the amount of milk in the final mixture.

Solution:
Formula: Final quantity = c (1 − x/c)ⁿ
where c = capacity, x = quantity removed each time, n = number of operations.
Final milk = 80 (1 − 8/80)²
= 80 × (9/10) × (9/10)
= 80 × 81/100 = 64.8 litres

Psychological Hook: The second removal is of the mixture, not pure milk. This is the hook. Candidates who miss this treat both removals as pure milk and get a wrong answer. Understanding why the formula is (1 − fraction)², and not (1 − 2 × fraction), is the key. The psychological pattern is "linear bias" — the brain assumes the effect is additive when it is multiplicative.

Question 8: The "Reverse Ratio" Trap

Question: The ratio of the length of a school ground to its width is 5:2. If the width is 40 m, find the length.

Solution:
Length : Width = 5 : 2
Length : 40 = 5 : 2
Apply the cross-product: L × 2 = 40 × 5
2L = 200
L = 100 m

Psychological Hook: The trap is in the order. If a student writes "40 : L = 5 : 2" out of carelessness, they get L = 16 m — absurdly small for a ground whose width is 40 m. The subtle cognitive burden is maintaining the correct correspondence between the known quantity (width) and its ratio position (second term). Reversing the order is one of the most common silent errors.

Question 9: The "Equal Shares from Fractional Parts" Trap

Question: Divide ₹2160 into three parts such that half of the first part, one-third of the second part, and one-seventh of the third part are equal.

Solution:
Let the equal value be k.
First part = 2k, Second part = 3k, Third part = 7k.
Ratio x : y : z = 2 : 3 : 7.
Total parts = 12.
1 part = 2160 ÷ 12 = 180.
x = 2 × 180 = ₹360; y = 3 × 180 = ₹540; z = 7 × 180 = ₹1260

Psychological Hook: The phrasing is deliberately inverted. "Half of first = one-third of second" generates the ratio; it does not give it directly. Students who try to set ½x + ⅓y + ⅐z = 2160 stumble into a three-variable equation with no unique solution. The insight — inverting the fractions to get the ratio (if ½a = ⅓b, then a : b = 2 : 3) — is non-obvious under time pressure.

Question 10: The "Coin Denomination" Trap

Question: A bag contains ₹114 in the form of ₹1, 50-paise, and 10-paise coins in the ratio 3:4:10. What is the number of 50-paise coins?

Solution:
Let the number of ₹1, 50p, and 10p coins be 3x, 4x, and 10x.
Total value: 1(3x) + 0.5(4x) + 0.1(10x) = 114
3x + 2x + x = 114
6x = 114 → x = 19
Number of 50-paise coins = 4 × 19 = 76

Psychological Hook: The given ratio is of the number of coins, not their value. The brain sees "ratio" next to "₹" and incorrectly links them. The extra step — converting each coin count to its monetary value using the denomination — is where the trap snaps shut. Many simply add the ratio numbers (3 + 4 + 10 = 17) and divide 114 by 17, producing nonsense.

Summary: The 5 Universal Traps

| Trap Category | How It Works | Questions Affected |
|---|---|---|
| Unit Mismatch | Mixing ₹ and paise, or other unlike units | Q3, Q10 |
| Order Reversal | Writing b:a when a:b is given | Q1, Q8 |
| Independent Multipliers | Using one variable for two independent ratios | Q5 |
| Linear Bias | Treating successive change as additive | Q7 |
| Hidden Ratio Generation | Not inverting fractional conditions into ratios | Q2, Q9 |

Exam-Day Mantra: When you see a ratio problem, pause for exactly 5 seconds. Ask: "Are my units the same? Did I preserve the order? Is there a hidden ratio I need to generate first?" This micro-routine, practised until it is automatic, eliminates the traps that even strong candidates fall for.
