📊 PYQ Analysis: Geometry (Foundation Level)

Geometry is a regular, dependable part of the Quantitative Aptitude section in SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO), Banking, and Railway exams. Questions from this chapter follow a stable pattern: triangles and their centres, circles (tangents, chords, cyclic quadrilaterals), quadrilaterals and polygons, and basic 3D mensuration appear again and again, usually with only the numbers changed. The ten questions below are high-yield archetypes built on concepts from NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics. Each is accompanied by the psychological "trap" that makes aspirants stumble despite knowing the formula.

**How exams typically distribute Geometry questions (pattern, in words):**
- Triangles and their centres (centroid, circumcentre, orthocentre, incentre) — asked most often.
- Circles — tangent properties, chords, cyclic quadrilaterals — a close second.
- Quadrilaterals and polygons — angle sums and ratio-based angle questions.
- 3D mensuration — cone/cylinder/sphere/cube volume and surface-area relations.

**High-yield sub-topics to master first:**
1. Tangent length from an external point
2. Centroid and area partition of a triangle
3. Cyclic quadrilateral — opposite angles
4. Equilateral triangle — height ↔ area conversion
5. Parallel chords and the radius
6. Direct common tangent of two circles
7. Intersecting chords theorem
8. Cone volume = one-third of a cylinder
9. Polygon interior angle sum
10. Sphere vs cube — equal surface area, unequal volume

**Q1. Tangent Length from an External Point**

A tangent is drawn to a circle of radius 6 cm from a point 10 cm from the centre. Find the length of the tangent.

Solution:
Let O be the centre, P the external point, and T the point of tangency. Since OT ⊥ PT, △OPT is right-angled.

PT = √(OP² − OT²) = √(10² − 6²) = √(100 − 36) = √64 = 8 cm

Answer: 8 cm.

🧠 Trap: Aspirants often add the numbers (10 + 6) or multiply, confusing tangent length with the distance from the external point to the farthest point on the circle. The right-angle property (radius ⊥ tangent) is the key.

Source: NCERT Class 10 Mathematics (Circles)

**Q2. Centroid & Area Partition of a Triangle**

If G is the centroid of ΔABC and area of ΔABC = 48 cm², find the area of ΔBGC.

Solution:
The centroid divides the triangle into three smaller triangles of equal area.

Area of ΔBGC = (1/3) × 48 = 16 cm²

Answer: 16 cm².

🧠 Trap: Many candidates confuse the centroid (G) with the orthocentre or circumcentre and apply different ratios. The 1/3 rule works only for the centroid, because it is the intersection of the medians — and the three medians partition a triangle into six small triangles of equal area.

Source: NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics (Triangles)

**Q3. Cyclic Quadrilateral: Opposite Angle Sum**

In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, find ∠A + ∠C.

Solution:
Theorem: The sum of opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral is 180°.

∠A + ∠C = 180°, and ∠B + ∠D = 180°

Answer: 180°.

🧠 Trap: A surprising number of aspirants answer 360° (the total interior angle sum of any quadrilateral) because they forget the special cyclic property. The question tests whether you recall the extra condition, not the generic quadrilateral rule.

Source: NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics (Circles)

**Q4. Equilateral Triangle: Height → Area**

The height of an equilateral triangle is 15 cm. Find its area.

Solution:
Height h = (√3/2)·a ⟹ a = 2h/√3 = 30/√3 = 10√3 cm.
Area = (√3/4)·a² = (√3/4) × 300 = 75√3 cm².

Answer: 75√3 cm².

🧠 Trap: Aspirants often plug the height directly into the area formula ((√3/4) × 15²), forgetting to convert height to side first. The relation a = 2h/√3 must be applied before using (√3/4)a².

Source: NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics (Triangles, Mensuration)

**Q5. Parallel Chords & Circle Radius**

AB = 10 cm and CD = 24 cm are parallel chords on opposite sides of the centre. The distance between them is 17 cm. Find the radius.

Solution:
Let the distances from the centre to AB and CD be x and y respectively. The perpendicular from the centre bisects each chord, so from the two right triangles:

r² = x² + 5²,  r² = y² + 12²

Also x + y = 17. Subtracting the equations: x² − y² = 119 ⟹ (x − y)(x + y) = 119 ⟹ x − y = 7. Solving with x + y = 17 gives x = 12, y = 5. Then r = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = 13 cm.

Answer: 13 cm.

🧠 Trap: The most common error is assuming the chords are equidistant from the centre (x = y), which gives a wrong radius. Always set up two separate right-triangle equations and use the given distance sum.

Source: PYQ-style (NCERT Class 9 Mathematics, Circles)

**Q6. Direct Common Tangent of Two Externally Touching Circles**

Two circles of radii 25 cm and 9 cm touch each other externally. Find the length of the direct common tangent.

Solution:
Distance between centres d = 25 + 9 = 34 cm.
Length of direct common tangent = √(d² − (r₁ − r₂)²) = √(34² − 16²) = √(1156 − 256) = √900 = 30 cm.

Answer: 30 cm.

Shortcut: When circles touch externally, the direct common tangent = 2√(r₁r₂). Here, 2√(25 × 9) = 2 × 15 = 30 cm.

🧠 Trap: Aspirants often confuse the "sum" and "difference" inside the root, or apply the transverse-tangent formula. For externally touching circles, the shortcut 2√(r₁r₂) bypasses the centre-distance calculation entirely.

Source: PYQ-style (NCERT Class 10 Mathematics, Circles)

**Q7. Chord Intersection Inside a Circle**

In a circle, chords AC and BD intersect at P. If AP = 4 cm, PC = 6 cm, and BP = 3 cm, find PD.

Solution:
Intersecting chords theorem: AP × PC = BP × PD.

4 × 6 = 3 × PD ⟹ PD = 24/3 = 8 cm.

Answer: 8 cm.

🧠 Trap: When no diagram is given, aspirants assign segments to the wrong chord, or reach for the tangent–secant theorem, which applies only when a tangent and a secant are drawn from an external point.

Source: NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics (Circles)

**Q8. Cone Volume: The One-Third Cylinder Relationship**

A cone and a cylinder have the same base radius and the same height. If the cylinder's volume is 270 cm³, find the cone's volume.

Solution:
Volume of cone = (1/3) × Volume of cylinder = (1/3) × 270 = 90 cm³.

Answer: 90 cm³.

🧠 Trap: Many aspirants confuse this with other fractional relationships (e.g., sphere vs cylinder). The mnemonic "Cone is one-third of a cylinder" is the reliable mental hook — same base, same height.

Source: NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics (Surface Areas and Volumes)

**Q9. Polygon Interior Angle Sum**

The interior angles of a pentagon are in the ratio 2:3:3:5:5. Find the smallest angle.

Solution:
Sum of interior angles of a pentagon = (5 − 2) × 180° = 540°.
Let the angles be 2x, 3x, 3x, 5x, 5x. Then

2x + 3x + 3x + 5x + 5x = 540° ⟹ 18x = 540° ⟹ x = 30°.

Smallest angle = 2x = 60°.

Answer: 60°.

🧠 Trap: The most common mistake is using 360° (the exterior-angle sum, or the quadrilateral sum) instead of (n − 2) × 180°. Another trap: misreading "pentagon" as "hexagon" and using 720°. Always underline the polygon's name and verify n before you begin.

Source: NCERT Class 8–9 Mathematics (Quadrilaterals, Polygons)

**Q10. Sphere vs Cube: Equal Surface Area, Unequal Volume**

A sphere and a cube have the same surface area. Which has a greater volume?

Solution:
Let the common surface area be S.

Cube: side a = √(S/6); Volume = a³ = (S/6)^(3/2) = S^(3/2) / (6√6) ≈ 0.068·S^(3/2).

Sphere: radius r = √(S/4π); Volume = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)π · (S/4π)^(3/2) = S^(3/2) / (6√π) ≈ 0.094·S^(3/2).

Since 1/(6√π) ≈ 0.094 > 1/(6√6) ≈ 0.068, the sphere's volume is larger for the same surface area.

Answer: The sphere.

🧠 Trap: The intuitive guess is often "cube," because we live in a world of boxes. But mathematically, the sphere is the most efficient shape for enclosing volume — it needs the least surface area for a given volume. This question exploits the "common-sense" instinct to choose the familiar shape. Memorise: "For equal surface area, the sphere always beats the cube on volume."

Source: PYQ-style (NCERT Class 9–10 Mathematics, Surface Areas and Volumes)
