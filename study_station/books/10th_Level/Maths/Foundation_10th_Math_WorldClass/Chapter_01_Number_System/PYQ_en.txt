🧠 PYQ Pattern Analysis — Number System

An analysis of number system questions asked in SSC, Banking, and Railway exams reveals a consistent pattern: examiners focus on a core set of 5–6 sub-topics, but their real skill lies in setting "traps." The difficulty is rarely in the mathematical operation itself, but in decoding what is actually being asked.

Recurring High-Weightage Topics: Most questions cluster around a few key areas:

- **Remainders:** Questions based on the remainder theorem, and finding the remainder when the divisor is changed, are exceptionally common (see Q2 and Q10 below).
- **Divisibility Rules:** Questions involving an unknown digit in a number, or checking divisibility by numbers like 9, 11, 12, 18, are a staple (see Q1, Q7, Q8).
- **LCM & HCF:** A classic topic, often involving finding the largest or smallest number satisfying given divisibility or remainder conditions (see Q7).
- **Unit Digit / Cyclicity:** Questions asking for the unit's digit of a large power or a long expression appear regularly (see Q6).
- **Number Types (Rational/Irrational, Prime/Composite):** These are fundamental and often form the hidden trap inside a more complex problem (see Q5, Q9).
- **Digit-Based Problems:** Classic "two-digit number and its reversed digits" problems are frequently seen, especially in banking exams (see Q4).

🎣 Top 10 High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions with "Hook & Trap" Analysis

Here are 10 archetypal questions of the kind these exams ask, each with its solution and the psychological "hook" or "trap" it uses to trick aspirants.

Q1. A 4-digit number is formed by repeating a 2-digit number, e.g., 1515, 3737. Any number of this form is always exactly divisible by?
(a) 7 (b) 11 (c) 13 (d) 101

Solution: Any 4-digit number of the form 'abab' can be written as 1000a + 100b + 10a + b = 1010a + 101b = 101(10a + b). Therefore, it is always a multiple of 101. Ans: (d) 101.

The Hook / Trap: The "Obvious Guess" Trap. The options 7, 11, and 13 are common divisibility-rule numbers. An unprepared student guesses one of these, while the answer is the less "cool" number 101. The trick is to use algebra, not mental rules.

Q2. A number when divided by 136 leaves a remainder of 36. If the same number is divided by 17, what will be the remainder?
(a) 9 (b) 7 (c) 3 (d) 2

Solution: Let the number be N. N = 136q + 36. Note that 136 = 17 × 8 (divisible by 17). So, N = 17(8q) + 36. Now divide 36 by 17: 36 = 17 × 2 + 2. Therefore, the remainder is 2. Ans: (d) 2.

The Hook / Trap: The "Complex Divisor" Trap. 136 looks random, and students get stuck trying to find the number. The key is recognizing that 136 is a multiple of the new divisor, 17. The anxiety of large numbers masks a simple logic.

Q3. The sum of five consecutive even numbers and four consecutive odd numbers is 96. If the smallest even number is 4, what is the difference between the largest even number and the smallest odd number?
(a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 2 (d) 3

Solution: Five consecutive even numbers starting with 4 are 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Their sum = 40. The sum of the four odd numbers = 96 − 40 = 56. Let the odd numbers be x, x+2, x+4, x+6. Their sum: 4x + 12 = 56 → 4x = 44 → x = 11. The odd numbers are 11, 13, 15, 17. The smallest odd is 11 and the largest even is 12. Difference = 12 − 11 = 1. Ans: (b) 1.

The Hook / Trap: The "Information Overload" Trap. The question bombards you with data (five evens, four odds, sums, differences). The trap is freezing up. The solution is simple, step-by-step decoding.

Q4. A two-digit number becomes 63 larger when its digits are reversed. The sum of the digits is 11. Find the number.
(a) 47 (b) 38 (c) 29 (d) 56

Solution: Let the number be 10a + b. The reversed number is 10b + a. According to the problem: (10b + a) − (10a + b) = 63 → 9b − 9a = 63 → b − a = 7. Also, a + b = 11. Solving: 2b = 18 → b = 9, so a = 2. The number is 29. Ans: (c) 29.

The Hook / Trap: The "Reverse and Subtract" Trap. Students often incorrectly set up the equation as (10a + b) − (10b + a) = 63, leading to a − b = 7 and a contradiction with the digit sum. The wording "becomes 63 larger" defines the correct order.

Q5. Which of the following is a prime number?
(a) 33 (b) 87 (c) 93 (d) 97

Solution: Check each number. 33 is divisible by 3 and 11. 87 is divisible by 3 (8+7 = 15). 93 is divisible by 3 (9+3 = 12). 97 is not divisible by any prime up to its square root (~10), i.e., 2, 3, 5, 7. Therefore, 97 is prime. Ans: (d) 97.

The Hook / Trap: The "Looks Like a Prime" Trap. Students who do not check the digit sum for divisibility by 3 will guess wrong. Always run the quick divisibility checks before declaring a number prime.

Q6. The unit digit in (12)^2173 is?
(a) 2 (b) 4 (c) 6 (d) 8

Solution: The unit's digit of 12 is 2. The cyclicity of 2 is 4: 2¹ = 2, 2² = 4, 2³ = 8, 2⁴ = 6. Divide the exponent by 4: 2173 ÷ 4 gives a remainder of 1. Therefore, the unit's digit is 2¹ = 2. Ans: (a) 2.

The Hook / Trap: The "Big Number" Intimidation Trap. The exponent 2173 looks scary. The trick is to ignore the base's tens digit and focus only on the unit digit's cyclicity. This question tests whether you can see the simple pattern through the noise.

Q7. What is the largest 4-digit number exactly divisible by 9, 12, and 18?
(a) 9936 (b) 9960 (c) 9990 (d) 9972

Solution: First, find the LCM of 9, 12, and 18, which is 36. The largest 4-digit number is 9999. Divide 9999 by 36: quotient 277, remainder 27. The required number is 9999 − 27 = 9972. Ans: (d) 9972.

The Hook / Trap: The "Direct Multiplication" Trap. A careless student might multiply the numbers (9 × 12 × 18 = 1944) and look for multiples of 1944. The key step is finding the least common multiple first.

Q8. If 'n' is any natural number, then by which largest number is (n³ − n) always divisible?
(a) 3 (b) 6 (c) 12 (d) 18

Solution: Factorize: n³ − n = n(n² − 1) = (n − 1)n(n + 1). This is the product of three consecutive natural numbers, which is always divisible by 2 and 3, hence by 6. For n = 2, (1)(2)(3) = 6, which is divisible by 6 but not by 12 or 18. So the largest such number is 6. Ans: (b) 6.

The Hook / Trap: The "Algebraic Forgetfulness" Trap. A student may not factorize n³ − n, or may not recognize that the product of three consecutive integers is always a multiple of 6. Testing only n = 3 (which gives 24) can mislead you toward 12 — always test the smallest case.

Q9. Consider the statement: √75 is a rational number. Is it true or false?

Solution: The statement is false. √75 = √(25 × 3) = 5√3. Since √3 is irrational, and the product of a non-zero rational (5) and an irrational (√3) is irrational, √75 is irrational. Ans: False.

The Hook / Trap: The "Imperfect Square Root" Trap. 75 is not a perfect square, so its square root cannot be expressed as a fraction of two integers. The trap is thinking that because 75 is a whole number, its root might be rational.

Q10. A number when divided by 5 leaves a remainder of 2. What is the remainder when the square of the number (n²) is divided by 5?
(a) 2 (b) 3 (c) 1 (d) 4

Solution: Let n = 5q + 2. Then n² = (5q + 2)² = 25q² + 20q + 4 = 5(5q² + 4q) + 4. When divided by 5, the remainder is 4. Quick check with an example: 7 ÷ 5 leaves remainder 2; 7² = 49, and 49 ÷ 5 leaves remainder 4. Ans: (d) 4.

The Hook / Trap: The "Formula Over Common Sense" Trap. Students try to recall complex remainder theorems and get confused. The fastest method is to take a small example (like 7), square it, and check the remainder. This trap tests whether you overcomplicate a simple idea.
