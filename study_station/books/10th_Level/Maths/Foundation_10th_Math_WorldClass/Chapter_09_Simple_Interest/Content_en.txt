The ₹5 Lakh Car That Cost Me Nothing (Almost!)

Imagine this: You lend your friend ₹5,00,000 to buy a car. Your friend promises, “I’ll return the money in 2 years, plus ₹50,000 extra as thanks.” Sounds fair, right? You just earned ₹50,000 for doing nothing. But wait — is that a good deal? What if a bank would have paid you ₹80,000 for the same money? In both cases, the extra money is called interest. The simplest form of this deal — where interest is calculated only on the original sum, year after year, without adding it back — is what we call Simple Interest.

In exams like SSC and Banking, Simple Interest (SI) might seem like a tiny topic, but it’s the foundation for Compound Interest, Installments, and even Data Interpretation. Nail SI today, and you’ll breeze through harder topics tomorrow. And the best part? It’s just 4th-grade math wrapped in a formula.

But first, let’s be real…

I Get It — You’ve Seen This Before, And It Still Feels Tricky

Maybe you’ve opened a book, stared at SI = (P×R×T)/100, and thought, “Why are there three ways to write time? When do I divide by 365?” Or you solved 10 questions, only to get confused by the 11th because it asked for “twice the sum” and your brain froze. You are not alone. Almost every topper has scribbled “SI = PRT” on their hand before an exam. The trick isn’t to work harder — it’s to remember the formula so weirdly that it sticks like a nursery rhyme. Chunk 1: The Golden Formula — And a Dirty Rat 🐀

Simple Interest answers one question: How much rent does your money earn? If you park ₹100 (Principal, P) with a bank at 10% per year (Rate, R), then after 1 year (Time, T), the bank pays you ₹10. After 2 years, another ₹10. After 3 years, another ₹10. The rent never grows — it’s always 10% of the original ₹100. That’s why it’s “simple.”

Chunk 2: Time — The Shape-Shifter in Exams

Time (T) is always in years in the formula. But examiners love testing you with months and days. The fix is simple:

If time is given in months, convert to years by dividing by 12. (e.g., 6 months = 6/12 = 0.5 years)

If time is given in days, divide by 365. (e.g., 73 days = 73/365 = 0.2 years)

Sometimes they use 366 for a leap year — but 99% of the time, use 365 unless they scream “leap year.”

Even easier: remember “M÷12, D÷365” as a chant. (M for months, D for days.)

Example: ₹2000 at 5% for 3 months.
SI = (2000 × 5 × 3/12) / 100 = (2000 × 5 × 0.25) / 100 = 2500/100 = ₹25. Simple.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Without looking: How do you convert 18 months into years for the SI formula? Answer: 18/12 = 1.5 years. Now ask yourself: If time is 73 days, what fraction do you use?

Chunk 3: When They Hide P, R, or T — The Detective Mode

Not every question will serve you P, R, and T on a plate. Sometimes they give you the SI and ask for the Principal. Or they say, “A sum doubles in 10 years, find the rate.” Don’t panic — it’s just rearranging the formula.

If a sum doubles, that means Amount = 2P, so SI = Amount – P = 2P – P = P.
So, SI = P. Plug into formula: P = (P × R × 10) / 100.
Cancel P from both sides (P ≠ 0): 1 = (R × 10)/100 → R = 10%.

The secret: Whenever you see “doubles,” “triples,” or “n times,” subtract the original P to get SI. For “doubles,” SI = P; for “triples,” SI = 2P; for “4 times,” SI = 3P, and so on. Observation: SI is always (n-1) × P when the sum becomes n times.

This single insight solves 80% of the hard-looking SI problems in under 30 seconds.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: A sum becomes 3 times in 8 years at simple interest. What is the rate of interest? (Try it now. Hint: SI = 2P, so 2P = (P × R × 8)/100 → R = 25%.) Check your answer.

Chunk 4: The Traps — Where Good Students Stumble

Trap 1: “Rate is 5% per annum, time is 6 months.” Many write T=6 and get a bloated SI. Remember: T is always in years. 6 months = 6/12 = 0.5.

Trap 2: “Amount after 3 years is ₹X, SI is given.” They ask for Principal, but some directly use X as P. Always: P = Amount – SI.

Trap 3: Two different principals, same rate and time, but SI difference is given. Just use the formula on the difference of principals. (SI1 – SI2) = (P1 – P2) × R × T / 100. This shortcut appears in nearly every banking exam.

Let’s frame an active recall for the difference trap.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: If SI on ₹5000 for 2 years is ₹500, and SI on ₹8000 for same rate and time is ₹800, find the rate. Here the difference in SI is 300 on a difference of 3000. So 300 = (3000 × R × 2)/100. Solve to get R = 5%. This saves solving two separate equations. Keep this in your mental tool kit.

Chunk 5: The 5-Second Half-Yearly, Quarterly, and “Per Annum” Hack

Simple Interest is always calculated per annum unless the question tricks you by giving a rate for a different period. If it says “5% half-yearly,” it means you get 5% every 6 months, so the yearly rate becomes 10%. Similarly, “2% quarterly” means 2% every 3 months, so the annual rate is 8%. In SI, you can simply convert the rate to annual and use the same formula — no complex compounding. The only twist: time must still be in years aligned with that annual rate.

Example: ₹10000 at 4% quarterly for 1 year.
Annual rate = 4% × 4 = 16%. T = 1 year. SI = (10000 × 16 × 1)/100 = ₹1600. Done.

Remember: “Quarterly? Multiply rate by 4; Half-yearly? Multiply by 2.” But keep T in years.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: What will be the simple interest on ₹5000 at 6% half-yearly for 2 years? (Convert rate: 6% × 2 = 12% per annum. Then SI = 5000×12×2/100 = ₹1200.) Confirm you didn’t forget to convert time.
