📊 PYQ Analysis: Permutation & Combination — Foundation Level

The analysis of previous year questions (PYQs) reveals 5 dominant archetypes that are tested repeatedly across SSC-CGL, CHSL, CPO, MTS, Railway NTPC, and Banking PO/Clerk exams. The questions are designed not merely to test formulaic recall but to assess your ability to recognise when order matters, identify hidden conditions, and avoid overcounting traps. The following 10 questions, drawn from actual PYQs and structured in precisely the manner they appear in examinations, represent the highest-yielding patterns you must master.

Q1. Forming Numbers (Without Repetition)

Question: How many 3-digit numbers can be formed using the digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 without repeating any digit?
(1) 120 (2) 100 (3) 90 (4) 80

Solution: Since the first digit (hundreds place) cannot be 0, it has only 5 choices. The second digit can be any of the remaining 5 digits, and the third can be any of the remaining 4 digits.
Total numbers = 5 × 5 × 4 = 100.
Answer: (2) 100

🧠 Psychological Trap — The "Together" Optical Illusion
When you read "always together," your brain's first instinct is to shrink the problem — fewer items, easier calculation. But the trap is forgetting to multiply by the internal arrangements within the block. The immediate visual of a single "block" is so compelling that candidates stop after arranging the 5 units and select 120. The exam setter knows that in a high-speed exam, your working memory can only hold about three chunks at once; the "block times internal factorial" step is the first to be ejected. Surviving this question requires you to pause and ask: did I forget the people inside the block?

🧠 Psychological Trap — The "Disguised Selection" Masquerade
The word "handshake" triggers imagery of a social gesture rather than a mathematical operation, and many aspirants fail to translate it into a combination problem. 🧠 Psychological Trap — The "Add vs. Multiply" Reflex
This question targets the most fundamental binary in combinatorics: AND events multiply, OR events add. Under exam stress, some candidates add 10 + 15 = 25 (an option not listed, causing panic) or inadvertently attempt other operations. The "exact composition" structure — exactly 2 men AND exactly 2 women — requires multiplication, but the word "exactly" can trigger an additive reflex because your brain associates "exact conditions" with summing constraints. The remedy is to vocalise the connective silently: "I need 2 men and 2 women" → multiply. Every time you detect "and" between independent selections, force yourself to write a multiplication sign.

🧠 Psychological Trap — The "Positional Anchoring" Misstep
The trap here is treating the vowel-first condition as a "vowel cohort" problem (all vowels together) and erroneously applying the block method. The question does NOT say "all vowels together" — it says "the first letter is a vowel." Many candidates overcomplicate: they try to select which vowel goes first, place the other vowel among the remaining letters, then arrange, leading to double-counting or incorrect formulas. The simplicity of the solution — just multiply the number of vowel choices for position 1 by the factorial of the rest — feels almost too easy, prompting second-guessing. The examiner relies on the fact that after several "vowels together" drill questions, your brain has been conditioned to see "vowel" and reflexively deploy the block technique, even when it's inappropriate.
