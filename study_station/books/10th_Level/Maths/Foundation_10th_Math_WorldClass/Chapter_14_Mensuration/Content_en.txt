The Queen, the Bull’s Hide, and a City – A True Story of Mensuration
Around 800 BCE, a clever queen named Dido fled her homeland and arrived on the shores of North Africa. She asked the local ruler for a tiny piece of land — just as much as could be covered by a bull’s hide. The ruler laughed and agreed.
Queen Dido then cut the hide into extremely thin, long strips, tied them into a single rope, and used that rope to encircle a whole hill. That enclosed land became the heart of the great city of Carthage.
She didn’t just know shapes — she used the secret that for a given perimeter, a circle encloses the maximum area. That is the power of mensuration: the right shape can change your destiny.

The Struggle Is Real (And I Know You Feel It)
If you have ever mixed up 2πr with πr², or stared blankly at a triangle wondering which side is the height, you are not alone. Almost every learner’s first reaction to mensuration is: “So many formulas! How will I remember them?”
The good news: you don’t need to memorise 20 different formulas. You just need to understand one simple idea:

Perimeter is like putting a fence around a playground — you measure the distance around.

Area is like laying carpet on the floor — you measure the surface covered.
Once that clicks, the formulas become your friends.

Chunk 1: Perimeter – The Fence of Any Shape

Rectangle and Square
A rectangle has two lengths (l) and two breadths (b). Walk along its boundary, and you cover l + b + l + b = 2(l + b). For a square, all four sides are equal (side = a), so just walk 4 times the side: perimeter = 4a.

Triangle
Just add the three sides (a, b, c). Simple as that.

Circle (The Fancy Shape)
Now, a circle doesn’t have straight sides. The perimeter of a circle is called the circumference. Imagine wrapping a string exactly once around a bangle — the length of that string is the circumference.
Ancient people found that no matter the size of the circle, if you divide the circumference (C) by the diameter (d), you always get a mysterious number slightly larger than 3 — which we now call π (pi, approx. 22/7 or 3.14).
Thus: C = πd, and since diameter = 2 × radius (r), we write C = 2πr.

🧠 Recall Prompt
A square park has side 15 m. What is its perimeter?
Now, a circular track has radius 7 m. Find its circumference (use π = 22/7). Check your answers before reading further.

Chunk 2: Area – The Carpet Inside

Area of a Rectangle
If you tile a rectangular floor, you multiply rows by columns: Area = length × breadth = l × b.
A square is just a special rectangle, so Area = a × a = a².

Area of a Triangle
Every triangle is exactly half of a rectangle that shares the same base and height. So, Area = (1/2) × base × height.
For a right-angled triangle, the two legs can be taken as base and height. For an equilateral triangle of side ‘a’, the height is (√3/2)a, so a shortcut formula pops up: Area = (√3/4) a².

Area of a Circle
Cut a circle into many thin slices (like pizza slices) and rearrange them alternately — they almost form a rectangle. The width becomes the radius (r), and the length becomes πr (half the circumference). Multiply them, and you get Area = πr². That’s why you see r², not r.

🧠 Recall Prompt
A rectangle has length 10 cm and breadth 6 cm. What is its area?
If you then cut that rectangle diagonally into two triangles, what is the area of one triangle?
(Answers: 60 cm², 30 cm².)

The Weird Mnemonic That Will Stick Forever

To never confuse Circumference (2πr) and Area (πr²), imagine this absurd scene:
You are baking two pies for your friend and you arrange them on a Round fence — “two pies are” (2πr) measures the round fence (circumference).
Then, for the area of the pie itself, you need “pie are square” but pies are not square — however, the formula says π r², which looks like “pie r squared”. So imagine a square pie with radius r written on it, and you shout: “Area = pie are squared!”
So:

Round fence = 2 pies arr🐍e (2πr)

Square pie in a pan = pie are squared (πr²)

It’s weird, it’s silly, and you will never forget it again.

Chunk 3: Everyday Applications – Where The Magic Happens

Painting a wall: You need the area of the rectangular wall to buy the correct amount of paint.

Fencing a plot: Perimeter tells you the length of wire or barbed fencing required.

Rotating a rope: A cow tied to a peg grazes in a circle. The rope is the radius, and the grass eaten depends on πr².

Floor tiling: A triangular corner piece of tile comes from half a rectangular tile — you use 1/2 × base × height.

When you see shapes in real life, silently ask: “Am I putting a fence or laying carpet?” That’s your cue to choose perimeter or area.

Quick Summary Table (Keep This As Your Cheat Sheet)
Shape	Perimeter (Boundary)	Area (Surface)
Rectangle (l × b)	2(l + b)	l × b
Square (side a)	4a	a²
Triangle (sides)	a + b + c	½ × base × height
Equilateral Δ (side a)	3a	(√3/4) a²
Circle (radius r)	2πr (circumference)	πr²
Semicircle (radius r)	πr + 2r	(πr²)/2
