📊 Top 10 High-Yield Data Interpretation PYQ-Style Questions with Solutions & Psychological Traps

This section analyses the types of Data Interpretation (DI) questions that SSC, Railway, Bank and State exams ask again and again. Each question below is a PYQ-style example showing the trap the examiner sets and how to beat it.

🔹 Question 1: The "Composition vs. Actual Number" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (Bank/SSC pattern)

Question:
Two pie-charts show the break-up of disease categories recorded among patients from two towns, Town A and Town B, as a percentage of the total number of patients in each town.

% of patients with Diabetes in Town A: 22%
% of patients with Diabetes in Town B: 30%

Which of the two towns has a higher number of persons with Diabetes?
(a) Town A
(b) Town B
(c) Same in Town A and Town B
(d) No inference can be drawn

Solution:
The correct answer is (d). We are given the percentage of diabetic patients in each town, but not the total number of patients (the base). Without the actual totals, we cannot compare absolute numbers. For example, if Town A has 1,000 patients, it has 220 diabetic persons; if Town B has only 500 patients, it has 150 — even though 30% > 22%.

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This question exploits the "Percentage-Number Equivalence" bias. Our minds are drawn to the larger percentage and we mistakenly equate it with a larger absolute number. The examiner highlights the percentages to make you jump to a conclusion without the base value. Always ask: "Percent of what?"

🔹 Question 2: The "Overlapping Categories" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (follow-up to Question 1)

Question:
From the same pie-charts: Town A's five disease categories sum to exactly 100% (13% + 18% + 22% + 34% + 13%), while Town B's categories sum to 121% (24% + 19% + 30% + 28% + 20%). What can we say about persons with more than one disease?
(a) There are likely to be persons with more than one disease in Town A.
(b) There are likely to be persons with more than one disease in Town B.
(c) There are likely to be persons with more than one disease in both towns.
(d) No inference can be drawn.

Solution:
The correct answer is (b). For Town A, the percentages sum to exactly 100%, so the categories are mutually exclusive — no person is counted twice. For Town B, the sum is 121%, which exceeds 100%, so some persons must have been counted in more than one category — i.e., they have more than one disease.

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Hidden Mathematical Cue" trap. Students focus on the visual slices and forget that a pie chart's percentages should sum to 100%. The crucial clue is hidden in the total. Always sum the percentages in a pie chart; if they exceed 100%, there is overlap.

🔹 Question 3: The "Multiple Data Sources" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (Bank PO pattern)

Question:
A line graph shows the profit percentage of Company B: 35% in 2009 and 50% in 2010. Company B's expenditure in 2009 and 2010 is ₹12 lakh and ₹14.5 lakh respectively. What was the total income of Company B for 2009 and 2010 combined?
(a) 39.75 lakh
(b) 37.95 lakh
(c) 38.75 lakh
(d) 38.55 lakh

Solution:
Profit % = (Income − Expenditure) / Expenditure × 100.

For 2009: 35 = (I₁ − 12)/12 × 100 → I₁ = 12 × 1.35 = ₹16.2 lakh
For 2010: 50 = (I₂ − 14.5)/14.5 × 100 → I₂ = 14.5 × 1.5 = ₹21.75 lakh

Total Income = 16.2 + 21.75 = ₹37.95 lakh. Answer: (b)

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Data Integration" trap. One piece of data (expenditure) is given in the text, while another (profit %) must be read from the graph. If you misread the graph or pick the wrong year's value, the whole calculation collapses. Cross-verify every value before starting.

🔹 Question 4: The "Percentage of a Percentage" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (SSC/Bank pattern)

Question:
In a school, 60% of the students are boys. Among boys, 75% play cricket. Among girls, 40% play cricket. What is the total percentage of students who play cricket?
(a) 57%
(b) 61%
(c) 65%
(d) 55%

Solution:
Boys playing cricket = 75% of 60% = 0.75 × 60 = 45% of all students.
Girls = 100% − 60% = 40% of students.
Girls playing cricket = 40% of 40% = 0.40 × 40 = 16% of all students.
Total cricket players = 45% + 16% = 61%. Answer: (b)

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Sequential Percentage Reduction" trap. The second condition (75% of boys) is a percentage of a percentage, but the brain often treats it as 75% of the total. Always identify the base for each percentage before multiplying.

🔹 Question 5: The "Absolute vs. Relative Size" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (Bank PO pattern)

Question:
A pie chart distributes 500 employees among five departments: A: 20%, B: 10%, C: 30%, D: 25%, E: 15%. A table gives the percentage of male employees in each department: A: 60%, B: 70%, C: 50%, D: 80%, E: 40%. Which department has the highest number of female employees?
(a) Department A
(b) Department C
(c) Department D
(d) Department E

Solution:
A: 100 employees → 40% female → 40
B: 50 employees → 30% female → 15
C: 150 employees → 50% female → 75 (Highest)
D: 125 employees → 20% female → 25
E: 75 employees → 60% female → 45
Answer: (b) Department C, with 75 female employees.

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Visual Misattribution" trap. Department E has the highest female percentage (60%), which tempts you to pick it, but its total staff is small. A large slice with a moderate female percentage can win on absolute numbers. Never judge by slice size or percentage alone — do the multiplication.

🔹 Question 6: The "Data Sufficiency Illusion" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (Bank exam pattern)

Question:
What is the profit earned by the company in 2015?
Statement 1: The company's revenue in 2015 was ₹10 lakh.
Statement 2: The company's expenditure in 2015 was ₹8 lakh.
(a) Statement 1 alone is sufficient, but Statement 2 alone is not.
(b) Statement 2 alone is sufficient, but Statement 1 alone is not.
(c) Both statements together are sufficient, but neither alone is sufficient.
(d) Each statement alone is sufficient.

Solution:
Profit = Revenue − Expenditure. Statement 1 alone gives only revenue; Statement 2 alone gives only expenditure. Neither alone can answer the question. Together: Profit = 10 − 8 = ₹2 lakh. Answer: (c)

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Almost There" trap. A statement giving a concrete number (like revenue) feels sufficient, but profit needs both revenue and cost. Always check whether the exact question asked can be answered with each statement alone.

🔹 Question 7: The "Trend ≠ Continuation" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (SSC/Bank reasoning-based DI)

Question:
A line graph shows that a company's sales increased every year from 2015 to 2020. A student concludes that sales will definitely increase in 2021 as well. Which is the most logical assessment?
(a) The conclusion is valid because the trend is clear.
(b) The conclusion is invalid because the data gives no information about 2021.
(c) The conclusion is valid because sales always follow trends.
(d) The conclusion is invalid because the data is insufficient for 2015–2020.

Solution:
The correct answer is (b). The data describes only past performance. A consistent past trend does not guarantee future results — market changes, competition, or other factors could reverse it. A trend describes what has happened, not what will happen.

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Trend-Extrapolation" bias. Our brains are pattern-seeking machines and naturally project a clear trend into the future. In DI, a valid inference is always bounded by the time frame of the given data.

🔹 Question 8: The "Approximation" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (SSC CGL/CHSL pattern)

Question:
A bar graph shows wheat production (in thousand tonnes) for 2011 to 2015: 43, 57, 68, 82, 97. What is the approximate percentage increase in production from 2011 to 2015?
(a) 115%
(b) 126%
(c) 130%
(d) 135%

Solution:
Increase = 97 − 43 = 54. Percentage increase = (54/43) × 100 ≈ 125.6% ≈ 126%. Answer: (b)

Note the danger: rounding 43 down to 40 and 54 up to 55 gives (55/40) × 100 ≈ 137%, which pulls you toward option (d) — a deliberate trap.

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Aggressive Approximation" trap. Approximation saves time, but rounding too aggressively when options are close together leads straight into a planted wrong answer. Judge the level of approximation by the gap between the options.

🔹 Question 9: The "Sequential vs. Base Value" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (Bank/SSC pattern)

Question:
The price of a stock is ₹100. On Monday it increases by 20%. On Tuesday it decreases by 20%. What is the net percentage change from the original price?
(a) 0%
(b) +4%
(c) −4%
(d) −2%

Solution:
Monday: 100 × 1.20 = ₹120.
Tuesday: decrease of 20% of 120 = 24 → Final price = 120 − 24 = ₹96.
Net change = 96 − 100 = −₹4, i.e., −4%. Answer: (c)

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the famous "Reversibility Fallacy". A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return to the original value, because the second percentage applies to the new, larger base (120). The intuitive answer (a) 0% is the trap. Remember: the base changes after the first step.

🔹 Question 10: The "Hidden Condition" Trap

Source: PYQ-style (SSC/Bank pattern)

Question:
Population data (in millions):
1911: 252 | 1921: 251 | 1931: 279 | 1941: 318 | 1951: 361 | 1961: 439 | 1971: 548 | 1981: 683

For which period was the absolute increase in population the maximum?
(a) 1911–1921
(b) 1941–1951
(c) 1961–1971
(d) 1971–1981

Solution:
1911–1921: 252 → 251 = decrease of 1 million
1941–1951: 318 → 361 = increase of 43 million
1961–1971: 439 → 548 = increase of 109 million
1971–1981: 548 → 683 = increase of 135 million (Maximum)
Answer: (d)

Psychological Hook/Trap:
This is the "Rate vs. Magnitude" trap. The word "growth" can push students into calculating percentage growth for every period — slow and unnecessary here. Read the question carefully: if it asks for absolute increase, simple subtraction is enough. Clarify exactly what is being asked before calculating.

📋 Summary of Psychological Traps in DI

| Trap Category | Key Question Pattern | Quick Check Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage-Number Equivalence | % given, absolute number asked | Pause and check: Do I have the base? |
| Hidden Mathematical Cues | Pie chart with overlapping data | Always sum the percentages in a pie chart. |
| Data Integration | Two or more linked data sources | Cross-verify all values before solving. |
| Percentage of a Percentage | Multi-layered percent conditions | Identify the base for each percentage. |
| Absolute vs. Relative Size | Pie chart + table combo | Don't judge by slice size alone; do the math. |
| Data Sufficiency Illusion | "Is the data enough?" questions | Check if the exact question is answerable with each statement alone. |
| Trend-Extrapolation | Future conclusion from past data | Data tells about the past, not the future. |
| Aggressive Approximation | "Approximately" with close options | Round only as much as the option gaps allow. |
| Reversibility Fallacy | Sequential % increase then decrease | The base changes after the first step. |
| Hidden Condition / Ambiguity | Unclear or contextual terms | Clarify what is asked before solving. |

🧠 Master Mnemonic for DI Traps: "P.A.C.T."

When you see a Data Interpretation problem, remember your P.A.C.T. with the examiner:

P (Percentage): Always identify the base before comparing any percentages.

A (Absolute): Don't let a relative value (percentage or ratio) fool you about absolute size.

C (Context): Check the source, the total, the units, and the definitions. Don't assume anything.

T (Trend): A trend shows what happened, not what will happen.
