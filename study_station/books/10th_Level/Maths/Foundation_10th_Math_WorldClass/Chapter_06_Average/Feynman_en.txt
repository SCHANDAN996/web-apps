# Feynman Explains: The Toughest Part of Average

Imagine Rahul loves cricket. He’s played 10 innings and his batting average is 45. Now he thinks:
> *“If I score 50 runs in the next innings, my average will become 50, right?”*

**Nope.** That’s where almost everyone trips. The real answer is **100 runs**.

Why? Let me tell you a cookie story.

---

### The Cookie Circle
There are 10 kids sitting in a circle. Each kid has a pile of cookies, but they started with different amounts. They decide to share equally. After sharing, each kid has 45 cookies.
That 45 is the **average** — the equal share.

Now, an 11th kid arrives. He doesn’t have any cookies yet.
The group announces:
> *“We want the new equal share to be 50 cookies for everybody.”*

So what happens? The new kid can’t just bring 50 cookies for himself!
If he brings only 50 cookies, then the total cookies becomes:
$$(10 \times 45) + 50 = 450 + 50 = 500$$
Divided among 11 kids:
$$500 \div 11 \approx 45.45$$
The average barely moved!

---

### Why Did 50 Fail?
Because the new kid’s cookies must be shared among **all 11 kids**, including himself!

The new equal share of 50 means all 11 kids need 50 cookies each:
$$11 \times 50 = 550 \text{ cookies total}$$

The group only had 450 cookies before. So the new kid must bring:
$$550 - 450 = 100 \text{ cookies!}$$

Not just his own 50, but an extra 50 to lift everyone else’s share from 45 to 50 ($10 \text{ kids} \times 5 \text{ extra} = 50$). He is lifting the entire group!

---

### The Golden Rule of Inclusion
> **“The new entrant must pay for the entire party, not just his own plate.”**

Whenever you add one new item and want to raise the average, the new value must equal:
$$\text{New value} = (\text{Desired new average} \times \text{New total count}) - (\text{Old average} \times \text{Old count})$$

---

### Now It's Your Turn
A fruit-seller worked for 4 days with an average profit of ₹30 per day. How much profit must he make on the fifth day so that his 5-day average profit becomes ₹40?

**Think:**
1. Old total = $4 \times 30 = 120$.
2. New total needed for 5 days at ₹40 = $5 \times 40 = 200$.
3. Fifth day profit required = $200 - 120 = ₹80$.

He needs ₹80, not ₹40! That is the party trick in action.
