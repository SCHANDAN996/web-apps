📖 Mixture & Alligation

The Milkman’s Secret

Imagine a clever milkman who wants to earn a little extra. He has pure milk that costs ₹50 per litre. He mixes some water (cost ₹0 per litre) into it. If he sells the mixture at ₹50 per litre, customers won’t suspect anything, but his profit shoots up because part of that mixture was free. Now here’s a shocking fact: you can calculate exactly how much water he added without a single x or y, in under 30 seconds, using a technique traders have used for centuries. That technique is Alligation. By the end of this chapter, you’ll be faster than the milkman himself.

🧠 Before you read on: If the milkman mixes 3 litres of water with 7 litres of milk and sells the whole 10 litres at ₹50 per litre, what is his profit compared to selling 10 litres of pure milk? (Keep that thought.)

The Struggle is Real — And it Ends Here

Some try equations, get tangled in variables, and waste precious minutes. But here’s the truth: exam setters don’t want you to form equations. They want to test whether you can use a shortcut that works for weightage, mixtures, and even profit and loss. Once you see the pattern, these questions become predictable, almost boring.

Take a deep breath. This chapter is going to replace confusion with a simple, visual tool. No more fear.

Meet the Alligation Cross — Your New Superpower

We have two ingredients: one cheaper (C), one dearer (D). We mix them to get a mean price (M). The magical alligation rule gives the ratio in which they must be mixed:

(Quantity of cheaper) : (Quantity of dearer) = (D – M) : (M – C)

Yes, just subtract crosswise. Draw a cross:

The ratio lands naturally in your hands. No formulas, no stress.

The “Don’t Miss Mom’s Cake” Trick

To never forget which difference goes to which ingredient, use this weird, funny mnemonic:

Don’t Miss Mom’s Cake

D – M: Don’t Miss (the difference on the left gives the quantity of the Dearer item? Wait, careful. Actually, D–M is paired with Cheaper? Let’s verify: Ratio = (D–M) : (M–C). So D–M corresponds to the cheaper ingredient, because it's opposite. That’s a common confusion. So we need a mnemonic that reminds us the cross-over. “Don’t Miss Mom’s Cake” can be adapted: Dearer’s difference Moves to Cheaper’s side. Too forced. Let’s craft a solid, unforgettable one: “Dear Cat, Meow Carefully”

D (Dearer) minus M (Mean) gives the proportion of Cheaper.
M minus C (Cheaper) gives the proportion of Dearer.
So “Dear Cat, Meow Carefully” – the first pair Dear and Cat links Dearer and Cheaper? Hmm.

Better: When you look at the cross, the difference on the right (M–C) goes with the left quantity (Dearer), and the difference on the left (D–M) goes with the right quantity (Cheaper). Like a figure-eight swap. Remember the phrase: “Difference dances diagonally.” Or a funny visual: Imagine a cow (Cheaper) holding a placard that says “D–M”, and a deer (Dearer) holding “M–C”. The cow reads the deer’s difference, the deer reads the cow’s difference. So, Cow holds Deer’s difference. Silly, but you’ll never flip it again.

Weighted Average — The Mother Concept

Alligation is nothing but an application of Weighted Average. When you mix 2 kg of ₹40/kg rice and 3 kg of ₹60/kg rice, the average price = (2×40 + 3×60) / (2+3) = 260/5 = ₹52/kg. The alligation cross simply reverses this: if average is given, it finds the weights.

So if a question says, “In what ratio must a grocer mix two pulses costing ₹15 and ₹20 per kg to get a mixture worth ₹16.50 per kg?” you draw the cross, subtract, and you’re done. The ratio of ₹15 to ₹20 will be (20 – 16.50) : (16.50 – 15) = 3.50 : 1.50 = 7:3. That’s it. Not even a single equation was harmed.

🧠 Recall prompt: A mixture of two liquids costs ₹30/L. The cheaper liquid is ₹24/L, the dearer is ₹36/L. Without a pen, what ratio do you get?

Replacement Problems — The “Take Out and Top Up” Dance

A classic trap: “A container has 40 litres of milk. 4 litres are withdrawn and replaced with water. This is repeated twice more. How much milk is left?” Students panic and start step-by-step messy calculations. But there’s a golden formula:

Amount of pure substance after n such operations = Initial quantity × [1 – (amount replaced each time / total quantity)]^n

Here, milk left = 40 × (1 – 4/40)^3 = 40 × (0.9)^3 = 40 × 0.729 = 29.16 litres. Done. Notice that the replacement is always the same amount each time. If different volumes are removed, apply sequentially, but competitive exams usually stick to equal replacements.

🧠 Recall: A jar has 80 L alcohol. 8 L is drawn and replaced with water, and the process is done 2 times. Find alcohol left. Use the formula.

Impure Mixtures and Money

Alligation isn’t just for liquids. It works for profit percent, average marks, even age mixtures. For example, a shopkeeper mixes two varieties of tea: one costs ₹130/kg and he sells at a profit of 20%, another costs ₹180/kg at 12% profit. If he sells the blend at a profit of 15%, find the ratio. Just treat profit percentages as “prices” and apply the alligation cross on 20%, 12%, and 15%. But remember: the cost prices are different, so you must first compare profit margins on the same base? Actually, you can alligate the profit per kg if cost prices differ, better to find selling prices or use alligation on profits if the profit percentages are on the same cost? No, if cost prices differ, you need to bring them to a common base — profit per kg. But for quick exam questions, usually they’ll ask “mixed in what ratio” and you alligate the profit percentages directly, which works when the profit is calculated on the cost price of each variety and the mixture’s profit is also on its cost price. It’s valid because the weighted average of profit percentages equals the overall profit percent only if the costs are equal. But careful — in reality, you need to alligate the profit amounts per kg, not percentages. However, many books claim you can alligate percentages if the cost prices are the same. Since this is Foundation level, I’ll show the safe approach: convert to profit per kg or apply alligation on the actual values. I’ll keep the content simple and exam-oriented: “For such problems, convert percentages to actual profit per kg using a standard base (say 100), then apply alligation.” I’ll mention that.

So I’ll include a tip: “Be cautious: Alligate profit % only when the cost price of both items is the same. Otherwise, use profit per kg.” I’ll phrase it simply.

Memory Trick for Replacement Formula

To remember the formula [1 – x/total]^n, think of a mischievous monkey removing a fraction of your patience each time you study. “Initial patience × (1 – fraction of distraction)^study sessions.” The fraction removed is replaced by daydreams. Comical, but sticks.

When Mixture of Mixtures Confuses You

Sometimes you mix two mixtures that already have components in different ratios. For instance, Vessel A has milk and water in ratio 2:3, Vessel B has 4:1. They are mixed to get a final mixture with ratio 1:1. What ratio of contents must be taken? Simply compute the concentration of one component (say milk) in each vessel: A = 2/5 milk, B = 4/5 milk. Target concentration = 1/2 milk. Then apply alligation on these fractions: (2/5, 4/5, and 1/2). Multiply by LCM to avoid fractions if you wish. The ratio becomes (4/5 – 1/2) : (1/2 – 2/5) = (3/10) : (1/10) = 3:1. So you take 3 parts from A and 1 part from B. Neat.
