📊 PYQ Analysis: LCM & HCF
📌 Introduction

LCM and HCF is a regular feature of the quantitative aptitude sections of SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS), Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk) and Railway (RRB NTPC, Group D) examinations. Questions from this chapter are usually of easy-to-medium difficulty, but cleverly placed trap options often push candidates into negative marking.

This section is a **pattern analysis**: it shows the question types examiners repeatedly ask from this chapter, the sub-topics that keep returning, and the traps they set — near-correct statements, remainder confusion, and look-alike numbers. The 10 high-yield question types below cover almost the entire territory from which examiners frame LCM–HCF questions.

Question 1: Product Rule — LCM × HCF = Product of Numbers

Question: The LCM of two numbers is 22 times their HCF. If one of the numbers is 132 and the sum of the LCM and HCF is 276, what is the other number?

Options: (a) 36  (b) 48  (c) 12  (d) 24

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Let HCF = H, then LCM = 22H.
Given: LCM + HCF = 276
⇒ 22H + H = 276
⇒ 23H = 276
⇒ H = 12
Therefore, LCM = 22 × 12 = 264.

Using the product rule: LCM × HCF = Number₁ × Number₂
⇒ 264 × 12 = 132 × x
⇒ 3168 = 132x
⇒ x = 24

Correct Answer: (d) 24

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: This is a direct formula-based question. The main risk is an arithmetic slip in 22H + H = 23H, which produces a wrong HCF and cascades through the whole solution. Keep the arithmetic clean.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 2: Ratio + LCM → HCF

Question: Three numbers are in the ratio 2 : 3 : 5 and their LCM is 90. Find their HCF.

Options: (a) 9  (b) 1  (c) 6  (d) 3

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Let the numbers be 2x, 3x, and 5x.
Since 2, 3, and 5 are mutually co-prime, their LCM is 2 × 3 × 5 × x = 30x.
Given: 30x = 90 ⇒ x = 3.
The numbers are: 6, 9, and 15.
6 = 2 × 3,  9 = 3²,  15 = 3 × 5.
The only common prime factor is 3 with lowest power 1.
Therefore, HCF = 3.

Correct Answer: (d) 3

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: When the ratio terms are co-prime, the common multiplier x is itself the HCF. Candidates often recalculate from scratch or pick the LCM-related number by mistake.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 3: Sum/Difference of LCM and HCF

Question: The HCF of two numbers is 1/20 of their LCM. If the difference between their LCM and HCF is 456, and one of the numbers is 96, find the other number.

Options: (a) 144  (b) 120  (c) 96  (d) 72

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Let HCF = H, then LCM = 20H.
Given: LCM – HCF = 456
⇒ 20H – H = 456
⇒ 19H = 456
⇒ H = 24
Therefore, LCM = 20 × 24 = 480.

Using the product rule: LCM × HCF = Number₁ × Number₂
⇒ 480 × 24 = 96 × x
⇒ 11520 = 96x
⇒ x = 120

Correct Answer: (b) 120

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Misreading "HCF is 1/20 of LCM" as "LCM is 1/20 of HCF" is a frequent reading trap. Also, seeing 96 in the question lures students into choosing option (c) 96. Always follow the algebraic relationship, not the visible numbers.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 4: Bells Ringing Together (LCM Application)

Question: Four bells ring at intervals of 8, 12, 16, and 28 seconds respectively. If they start tolling together at 1:00 AM, at what time will they toll together next?

Options: (a) 1:04:48 AM  (b) 1:06:12 AM  (c) 1:05:36 AM  (d) 1:05:12 AM

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Prime factorization of intervals:
8 = 2³
12 = 2² × 3
16 = 2⁴
28 = 2² × 7

LCM(8, 12, 16, 28) = 2⁴ × 3 × 7 = 16 × 21 = 336 seconds.
Converting: 336 seconds = 5 minutes 36 seconds.
Adding to 1:00:00 AM gives 1:05:36 AM.

Correct Answer: (c) 1:05:36 AM

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: "Toll together next" needs the elapsed time equal to the LCM. If the question instead asked "How many times do they toll together in 1 hour?", you would divide 3600 by the LCM and add 1 for the initial toll. Read which variant is being asked.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 5: Same Remainder Case (HCF of Differences)

Question: Find the greatest number that will divide 108, 124, and 156 so as to leave the same remainder in each case.

Options: (a) 16  (b) 12  (c) 8  (d) 4

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Theorem: When different numbers leave the same remainder on division by a common divisor D, the pairwise differences of the numbers are exactly divisible by D.
Computing differences:
124 – 108 = 16
156 – 124 = 32
156 – 108 = 48

HCF(16, 32, 48) = 16.

Correct Answer: (a) 16

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: The classic blunder is computing the HCF of the original numbers directly: HCF(108, 124, 156) = 4, which is deliberately placed as option (d). For "same remainder" problems, always take the HCF of the differences.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 6: LCM and HCF of Fractions

Question: Find the ratio of the LCM to the HCF of the fractions 2/7, 12/35, and 8/49.

Options: (a) 420 : 1  (b) 120 : 1  (c) 210 : 1  (d) 490 : 1

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Formulas for fractions:
LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators
HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators

1. LCM:
LCM(2, 12, 8) = 24
HCF(7, 35, 49) = 7
LCM = 24/7

2. HCF:
HCF(2, 12, 8) = 2
LCM(7, 35, 49) = 245
HCF = 2/245

3. Ratio:
(24/7) ÷ (2/245) = (24/7) × (245/2) = 12 × 35 = 420 : 1.

Correct Answer: (a) 420 : 1

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Mixing up the two fraction formulas. Memory rule: the quantity you want goes to the numerators (LCM → LCM of numerators; HCF → HCF of numerators), and the opposite operation is applied to the denominators.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 7: Smallest n-Digit Number Divisible by Given Numbers

Question: Find the smallest 5-digit number which is completely divisible by 12, 15, and 18.

Options: (a) 10020  (b) 10080  (c) 10025  (d) 10180

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Prime factorization:
12 = 2² × 3
15 = 3 × 5
18 = 2 × 3²
LCM(12, 15, 18) = 2² × 3² × 5 = 180.

The smallest 5-digit number is 10000.
Dividing: 10000 = 180 × 55 + 100 (remainder = 100).
To reach the next multiple, add (180 – 100) = 80:
Smallest divisible number = 10000 + 80 = 10080.

Correct Answer: (b) 10080

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Adding the remainder (100) instead of (LCM – remainder) gives 10100, which is not divisible by 180. For "smallest number greater than or equal to N", always add (divisor – remainder). For the "largest n-digit number" variant, subtract the remainder instead.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 8: Ratio + HCF → LCM

Question: Two numbers are in the ratio 3 : 4 and their HCF is 4. Find their LCM.

Options: (a) 12  (b) 24  (c) 36  (d) 48

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
Let the numbers be 3x and 4x.
Since 3 and 4 are co-prime, HCF(3x, 4x) = x.
Given HCF = 4 ⇒ x = 4.
The numbers are 12 and 16.
LCM = 3 × 4 × x = 12 × 4 = 48.
(Check by product rule: LCM = (12 × 16) / 4 = 192 / 4 = 48 ✓)

Correct Answer: (d) 48

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Candidates often stop at x = 4 and pick a multiple of it carelessly, or compute 3 × 4 = 12 (option a) by forgetting the multiplier. Remember: numbers = ratio terms × HCF, and LCM = product of ratio terms × HCF.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 9: Co-prime Numbers and LCM

Question: The product of two co-prime numbers is 117. What is their LCM?

Options: (a) 9  (b) 13  (c) 39  (d) 117

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
By definition, co-prime numbers have HCF = 1.
Using the product formula:
LCM × HCF = Product of numbers
⇒ LCM × 1 = 117
⇒ LCM = 117.

Correct Answer: (d) 117

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Over-complicating by factorising 117 (9 × 13) and then computing the LCM of the factors. When numbers are co-prime, apply HCF = 1 directly — the LCM is simply the product.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 10: Measuring Rod / Tiling — Practical HCF

Question: A rectangular courtyard is 140 cm long and 525 cm broad. It is to be paved with square tiles of equal size. What is the largest possible length of the side of each tile?

Options: (a) 64 cm  (b) 35 cm  (c) 21 cm  (d) 28 cm

<details> <summary><b>✅ View Solution</b></summary>

Step-by-step solution:
The "largest side" that divides both dimensions evenly is the HCF of 140 and 525.
140 = 2² × 5 × 7
525 = 3 × 5² × 7
Common prime factors: 5¹ and 7¹.
HCF = 5 × 7 = 35 cm.

Correct Answer: (b) 35 cm

</details>

🧠 Psychological Trap: Confusing the keywords: "largest", "maximum", "greatest size", "divide exactly" → HCF; "smallest", "meet together", "simultaneously", "next time" → LCM. Misreading the keyword flips the entire method.

Source: PYQ-style

Common examiner traps seen across these question types:
- Placing the HCF of the original numbers as an option in "same remainder" questions.
- Placing the number already given in the question as an option (as in Question 3).
- Keyword inversion: framing an HCF problem with LCM-sounding language and vice versa.
- Remainder vs (divisor – remainder) confusion in n-digit number questions.

🎯 3 Golden Rules for Exam Success

1. Master the Product Rule: "LCM × HCF = Product of Two Numbers". It directly or indirectly solves the majority of two-number questions asked from this chapter.
2. Keyword Recognition: "Together", "Simultaneously", "Next Time" → LCM; "Maximum", "Largest size", "Divide exactly" → HCF.
3. Verification Step: Plug your answer back into the formula or the divisibility condition — spending about 20 seconds on verification can save you from negative marking.
