📊 PYQ Analysis: Trigonometry (English)

This section analyses the *pattern* of Trigonometry questions asked in major competitive exams (SSC CGL, CHSL, CPO, Bank PO/Clerk, RRB NTPC/Group D). Instead of unverifiable year-wise counts, we focus on what actually helps you: which subtopics are asked repeatedly, the standard question templates, and the psychological traps that cause aspirants to lose marks.

Exam Pattern Overview (Qualitative)

- **SSC CGL Tier 1 / CHSL / RRB NTPC / Group D:** Usually 1–2 questions per paper. Mostly easy — direct ratio substitution, standard angle values, simple height & distance.
- **SSC CGL Tier 2 / CPO:** Slightly deeper — conditional identities, simplification of expressions, two-tower height & distance.
- **Bank PO/Clerk:** Rare and easy — standard angle values and direct identities.

**Pattern insight (in words, not numbers):** The large majority of trigonometry questions come from just two areas — (1) trigonometric ratios and identities, and (2) height & distance. The identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 is the single most-used formula. Radian measure, quadrant signs, and max-min values appear occasionally. If you master identities and height & distance, you cover almost everything these exams ask.

Top 10 High-Yield Questions with Solutions

Question 1: The Complementary Angle Trap

If sin θ = cos 40° where 0° < θ < 90°, then find θ.

Options: (a) 40° (b) 50° (c) 60° (d) 30°

Solution:
We use the complementary angle identity: sin θ = cos(90° − θ).
Given sin θ = cos 40°, equate: cos(90° − θ) = cos 40°.
Therefore: 90° − θ = 40° ⇒ θ = 50°.

Answer: (b) 50°

🧪 The Psychological Hook: This question baits the rushed student into answering 40° directly. The examiner exploits the fact that many aspirants, under time pressure, see cos 40° and immediately choose 40° without applying the complement rule. Trap: Visual matching — your brain wants to equate θ = 40° because it "looks right." Lesson: When you see sin θ equal to cos of something, always recall sin θ = cos(90° − θ).

Source: PYQ-style

Question 2: The "Hidden Identity" Trap

If sin θ = 3/5, find the value of (sec²θ − tan²θ).

Options: (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 2 (d) -1

Solution:
Here, many students rush to find cos θ from sin θ = 3/5 (which gives cos θ = 4/5), then compute sec θ = 5/4 and tan θ = 3/4, and plug into the expression:
sec²θ − tan²θ = (25/16) − (9/16) = 16/16 = 1.

BUT, the smarter approach: Recall the derived Pythagorean identity: 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ. Rearranging gives: sec²θ − tan²θ = 1. This is true for EVERY angle θ, regardless of sin θ's value. The given sin θ = 3/5 is a red herring — a distractor. You can answer in 2 seconds: the expression always equals 1.

Answer: (b) 1

🧪 The Psychological Hook: The examiner gives you a specific value (sin θ = 3/5) to trigger your "solving mode." Your brain thinks, "I have data, I must use it." But the expression sec²θ − tan²θ is an identity — it doesn't depend on θ at all. The data is a distraction designed to waste your time. Trap: Processing irrelevant information. Lesson: Before computing, scan the expression for identities.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 3: The "tan-cot Chord" Trap

If tan θ + cot θ = 2, find tan²θ + cot²θ.

Options: (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 2 (d) 4

Solution:
Use the algebraic identity: (a + b)² = a² + b² + 2ab.
Here, a = tan θ, b = cot θ. Note that tan θ × cot θ = 1.
So, (tan θ + cot θ)² = tan²θ + cot²θ + 2(1).
Given tan θ + cot θ = 2: 2² = tan²θ + cot²θ + 2.
4 = tan²θ + cot²θ + 2 ⇒ tan²θ + cot²θ = 2.

Answer: (c) 2

🧪 The Psychological Hook: The sum equals 2, and the product tan θ × cot θ is always 1. The number 2 here is "perfect": it leads to a clean square and a neat answer. Students who blindly square 2 to get 4 and forget to subtract 2ab fall into the trap of choosing option (d). Trap: Forgetting the −2ab term when squaring a sum. Lesson: Write the expansion fully; never skip the middle term.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 4: The "Angle of Depression = Angle of Elevation" Trap

From the top of a 100 m high lighthouse, the angle of depression of a ship is 30°. Find the distance of the ship from the base of the lighthouse.

Options: (a) 100 m (b) 100√3 m (c) 100/√3 m (d) 200 m

Solution:
The angle of depression from the lighthouse top to the ship is 30°. By the property of alternate interior angles, the angle of elevation from the ship to the lighthouse top is also 30°.
In the right triangle formed:

- Opposite (height) = 100 m
- Adjacent (distance) = d (unknown)
- Angle = 30°

We use tan 30° = opposite / adjacent = 100 / d.
1/√3 = 100/d ⇒ d = 100√3 m.

Answer: (b) 100√3 m (≈ 173.2 m)

🧪 The Psychological Hook: Many students confuse which angle to use or misdraw the triangle, placing the 30° at the wrong vertex. Others mix up tan and sin. If a student mistakenly uses sin 30° = 100/d → 1/2 = 100/d → d = 200 m, they pick the (d) trap. The examiner deliberately places 200 m as an option. Trap: Using sin instead of tan in a height-and-distance problem where the adjacent side is unknown. Lesson: Always draw the triangle and label opposite/adjacent/hypotenuse before choosing the ratio.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 5: The "Radian-Degree Conversion" Trap

If cos θ = cos(π/4), where θ is in degrees, then θ is:

Options: (a) 45° (b) 30° (c) 60° (d) 90°

Solution:
π/4 radians = 180°/4 = 45°.
So, cos θ = cos 45°. Since θ is an acute angle: θ = 45°.

Answer: (a) 45°

🧪 The Psychological Hook: Many students forget the radian-to-degree conversion and treat π/4 as if it were degrees directly, or misremember the conversion factor. Anxiety about radians often leads to guessing. Trap: Confusion between radian and degree measure. Lesson: Memorise: π radians = 180°, so multiply radians by 180/π to get degrees.

Source: NCERT Class 11 Mathematics

Question 6: The "Complementary Square" Trap

Find the value of sin² 25° + sin² 65°.

Options: (a) 0 (b) 1/2 (c) 1 (d) 2

Solution:
Notice: 65° = 90° − 25°. Using the complementary angle rule: sin 65° = sin(90° − 25°) = cos 25°.
Therefore: sin² 25° + sin² 65° = sin² 25° + cos² 25° = 1.
This uses the Pythagorean identity.

Answer: (c) 1

🧪 The Psychological Hook: The problem appears to require computing individual sine values of non-standard angles, which seems impossible without a calculator. Students panic, thinking they missed a formula. But the trick is pattern recognition: spotting that 25° + 65° = 90°. When angles sum to 90°, their sine and cosine swap. Trap: Not recognizing complementary pairs. Lesson: When you see two sine squares added, check if the angles sum to 90°. If A + B = 90°, then sin²A + sin²B = 1 because sin B = cos A.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 7: The "Min-Max Range" Trap

If sin θ + cos θ = √2, find sin θ × cos θ.

Options: (a) 0 (b) 1/2 (c) 1 (d) √2/2

Solution:
Square both sides: (sin θ + cos θ)² = (√2)².
Expanding: sin²θ + cos²θ + 2 sin θ cos θ = 2.
Using sin²θ + cos²θ = 1: 1 + 2 sin θ cos θ = 2.
2 sin θ cos θ = 1 ⇒ sin θ cos θ = 1/2.

Answer: (b) 1/2

🧪 The Psychological Hook: The maximum possible value of sin θ + cos θ is √2 (it occurs at θ = 45°). The question cleverly uses this maximum value. Many students try to find θ first (45°) and then compute, but the square-and-simplify method is faster and works for any given sum. Trap: Trying to solve for θ individually when squaring the sum is a one-step shortcut. Lesson: For any expression involving sin θ + cos θ, squaring it opens the door to using sin²θ + cos²θ = 1.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 8: The "Cot-Complement Swap" Trap

If tan 2θ = cot(θ − 15°), where 2θ is an acute angle, find θ.

Options: (a) 25° (b) 35° (c) 45° (d) 55°

Solution:
Using the complementary identity: cot φ = tan(90° − φ).
So, cot(θ − 15°) = tan(90° − (θ − 15°)) = tan(105° − θ).
Given tan 2θ = cot(θ − 15°), we have: tan 2θ = tan(105° − θ).
Since both are acute angles: 2θ = 105° − θ ⇒ 3θ = 105° ⇒ θ = 35°.

Answer: (b) 35°

🧪 The Psychological Hook: This problem combines the tan-cot relationship with algebraic manipulation. Students often forget that cot x = tan(90° − x) and instead write cot as 1/tan, leading to a reciprocal equation tan 2θ = 1 / tan(θ − 15°) — which is still solvable but more error-prone and time-consuming. Trap: Using the reciprocal relationship cot = 1/tan instead of the complementary-angle identity. Lesson: When you see tan A = cot B, instantly think tan A = tan(90° − B).

Source: PYQ-style

Question 9: The "Multi-Tower Comparison" Trap

Two towers stand on the same horizontal ground. From the midpoint of the line joining their feet, the angles of elevation of the tops are 30° and 45° respectively. Find the ratio of their heights.

Options: (a) 1:2 (b) 1:√3 (c) 1:3 (d) √3:1

Solution:
Let the heights be h₁ and h₂. Let the distance between the towers be 2d, so the midpoint is at distance d from each tower.
For Tower 1 (30°): tan 30° = h₁/d ⇒ 1/√3 = h₁/d ⇒ h₁ = d/√3.
For Tower 2 (45°): tan 45° = h₂/d ⇒ 1 = h₂/d ⇒ h₂ = d.
Ratio h₁ : h₂ = (d/√3) : d = 1 : √3.

Answer: (b) 1:√3

🧪 The Psychological Hook: The word "midpoint" creates spatial confusion. Many students incorrectly assume the towers are at the same point or misread where the observer stands. Visualizing the geometry correctly (two separate towers with feet connected by a line; observer at the midpoint of that line) is the key. Misreading the diagram leads to swapping the heights. Trap: Incorrect spatial visualization; taking h₁ at 45° and h₂ at 30° by mistake. Lesson: Always sketch the scenario. The larger angle of elevation (45°) corresponds to the taller tower for the same distance.

Source: PYQ-style

Question 10: The "Negative in Quadrant" Trap

If cos θ = 3/5 and θ lies in the fourth quadrant, find tan θ.

Options: (a) 4/3 (b) -4/3 (c) 3/4 (d) -3/4

Solution:
Using sin²θ + cos²θ = 1: sin²θ = 1 − 9/25 = 16/25 ⇒ sin θ = ± 4/5.
In the fourth quadrant, sin θ is negative and cos θ is positive.
So, sin θ = −4/5.
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ = (−4/5) / (3/5) = −4/3.

Answer: (b) -4/3

🧪 The Psychological Hook: This question tests not just trigonometry but also the ASTC (All–Sin–Tan–Cos) quadrant sign rule. Students who mechanically find sin θ = 4/5 and then tan θ = 4/3 without considering the quadrant fall straight into the trap and pick option (a). Trap: Ignoring the sign convention based on the quadrant. Lesson: Immediately recall the ASTC rule when the quadrant is mentioned: Fourth quadrant → only cos (and sec) are positive; sin, tan, cosec, cot are negative.

Source: NCERT Class 11 Mathematics

Summary: Recurring Psychological Traps in Trigonometry PYQs

| Trap Category | Example | Why Students Fall |
|---|---|---|
| Visual matching (copying the angle seen) | Q1: sin θ = cos 40° → answering 40° | The matching number "looks right" under time pressure |
| Processing irrelevant given data | Q2: sin θ = 3/5 given unnecessarily | Brain feels obliged to use all given data |
| Forgetting identity expansions | Q3: Squaring tan θ + cot θ | Skipping the middle term 2ab |
| Mixing tan and sin in H&D problems | Q4: Using sin instead of tan | Not labelling opposite/adjacent/hypotenuse first |
| Radian-degree confusion | Q5: π/4 misinterpretation | Weak conversion habit (× 180/π) |
| Not recognizing complementary pairs | Q6: 25° + 65° not seen as 90° | No pattern-check habit before computing |
| Sign of ratios per quadrant (ASTC) | Q10: 4th quadrant → tan negative | Quadrant rule not revised |

Topic Priority Map (based on exam pattern)

- **Trigonometric Identities** — highest priority
  - sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 and its derived forms (sec²θ − tan²θ = 1, cosec²θ − cot²θ = 1)
  - Complementary angle identities (sin ↔ cos, tan ↔ cot at 90° − θ)
  - Algebraic manipulation with tan θ + cot θ, sin θ + cos θ
- **Height & Distance** — second priority
  - Single tower/pole problems
  - Two-tower comparison
  - Angle of depression = angle of elevation (alternate interior angles)
- **Miscellaneous** — lower but regular
  - Radian-degree conversion
  - Quadrant signs (ASTC rule)
  - Max-min values of sin θ + cos θ type expressions

Key takeaway: Master the Pythagorean identity and complementary angles, practice drawing a labelled diagram for every height & distance problem, and ALWAYS check quadrant sign conventions. These three habits cover the overwhelming majority of trigonometry questions in SSC, Bank, and Railway exams.
