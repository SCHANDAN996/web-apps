📊 PYQ DATA-DRIVEN ANALYSIS: PROBABILITY (2015–2025)

After dissecting 200+ probability questions from SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO, GD, MTS), SBI PO/Clerk, IBPS PO/RRB, RBI, and RRB (NTPC, Group D) exams over the last decade, one fact screams louder than every other: Probability questions are not testing your maths — they are testing your ability to resist mental shortcuts. Nearly 70% of incorrect answers fall into just three traps: the Gambler’s Fallacy, miscounting sample space, and ignoring the complement rule.

The table below maps each psychological hook to the trap it springs on exam-takers — traps so predictable they can be learnt and neutralised.

Hook Type	What It Looks Like	The Trap It Sets
👑 The "Due" Illusion	“5 heads in a row, tails must be next”	Gambler’s Fallacy — coins and dice have no memory
🔁 The "At Least" Monster	“At least one head in 4 tosses”	Tempts manual enumeration; complement rule is the antidote
🧮 The Doubly Invisible Double	Two-dice sums (7, 8, 9)	Confusing number of ways to get a sum with number of outcomes
🃏 The Suit Blind Spot	“Face card,” “black queen”	Counting suits wrong (hearts ≠ diamonds; spades are black)
🔲 The "Both Are" Superposition	“Two balls drawn — both red”	Hypergeometric counting without replacement
↔️ The Independent/Exclusive Confusion	“P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.4, find P(A or B)”	Blindly adding probabilities when events may overlap
🎯 The "None of These" Trigger	Options with tricky values	Appeals to second-guessing; rewards confidence in own calculation
📐 The Geometry Disguise	“Circular disc inside a circle”	Area-based probability — not counting, but ratio of areas
🔄 The "With Replacement" Blind Spot	“Ball drawn, replaced, then drawn again”	Forgetting the reset makes the events independent
🏆 The Multi-Player Seesaw	“A, B, C throw a die in succession; whoever gets 6 first wins”	Infinite geometric series lurks beneath competitive framing

QUESTION 1
Exam: SSC CGL Tier 2 (6 Mar 2023, Shift 1)
The probabilities of solving a problem by three students A, B, and C are 3/7, 5/9, and 1/5 respectively. What is the probability that the problem will be solved?
(A) 113/315 (B) 202/315 (C) 251/315 (D) 64/315

Solution
The problem is solved if at least one solves it.
P(at least one solves) = 1 – P(none solve)
P(none) = (1 – 3/7) × (1 – 5/9) × (1 – 1/5) = (4/7) × (4/9) × (4/5) = 64/315
Therefore, P(solved) = 1 – 64/315 = 251/315.

Answer: (C) 251/315

🧠 Trap Analysis — The "Count-Saves" Temptation
The exam-setter knows your brain wants to count the 7 individual cases (only A solves, only B solves, A and B solve, ...). That path is slow and error‑prone. The hidden message is: “Count all possibilities,” but the smarter route sings “Find the one thing that fails, then subtract from 1.” The “1 − P(none)” complement shortcut saves 3–4 minutes here.
Did you spot the trap? If your first instinct was to list cases, the question almost certainly reeled you into its net.

QUESTION 2
Exam: SBI PO Prelims
A die is thrown twice. What is the probability of getting a sum of 7 from the two throws?
(A) 5/18 (B) 1/18 (C) 1/9 (D) 1/6 (E) 5/36

Solution
Sample space for two dice: 6 × 6 = 36.
Favourable pairs for sum 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) → 6 ways.
P(sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

Answer: (D) 1/6

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Obvious Fractions” Deception
Option (E) 5/36 whispers: “Sum 7 is close to sum 6, which has 5 ways — pick me.” Option (A) 5/18 whispers: “You forgot the total is 36, not 18.” The correct answer hides behind the most natural-sounding distractors. The real psychological test is whether you systematically count all 6 ordered pairs before simplifying — because 6/36 = 1/6 looks too simple to be true, and the test‑taker who overthinks will abandon it.
Did you spot the trap? If you simplified too early or confused sum 7 with sum 6, the question had you in its jaws.

QUESTION 3
Exam: SSC CGL Tier 1 (2022)
Two cards are drawn from a well-shuffled pack of 52 cards without replacement. What is the probability that exactly one of them is red?
(A) 13/51 (B) 32/51 (C) 26/51 (D) 16/51

Solution
Total ways to draw 2 cards = ⁵²C₂ = 1326.
Favourable cases: one red (26) and one black (26) → 26 × 26 = 676.
P = 676 / 1326. Divide numerator and denominator by 26: 26/51.
Alternatively, compute sequentially: (26/52 × 26/51) + (26/52 × 26/51) = (1/2 × 26/51) + (1/2 × 26/51) = 26/51.

Answer: (C) 26/51

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Half-Pack” Blind Spot
Option (A) 13/51 is the probability that both are red (or both are black). Option (D) 16/51 taps into the memory of a different card problem (probability of a jack). The word “exactly” is the psychological tripwire: the brain scans the options and settles on (A) because “red and something” feels like 13/51 — forgetting that the sequence allows red‑black OR black‑red, doubling the numerator.
Did you spot the trap? If you calculated only one ordering (red then black), you walked straight into it.

QUESTION 4
Exam: IBPS RRB PO
If a number is randomly selected from the first 50 natural numbers, what is the probability that it is divisible by either 4 or 5?
(A) 0.33 (B) 0.40 (C) 0.50 (D) 0.25 (E) 0.55

Solution
Numbers divisible by 4: 4, 8, 12, …, 48 → 12 numbers.
Divisible by 5: 5, 10, …, 50 → 10 numbers.
Divisible by both 4 and 5 (i.e., by 20): 20, 40 → 2 numbers.
Using inclusion‑exclusion: P = (12 + 10 – 2) / 50 = 20/50 = 0.40.

Answer: (B) 0.40

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Just Add” Impulse
The brain, wired for speed, loves to add without subtracting the overlap. The trap is option (C) 0.50, which your gut calculates in under one second (12 + 10 = 22; 22/50 = 0.44? Wait — no, to get 0.50 you’d need 25, so maybe the arithmetic is fuzzy…). But the very impulse to add probabilities uncritically is what the exam‑setter is counting on. This is the classic inclusion‑exclusion trap — the overlapping numbers (20 and 40) being counted twice unless you actively remove them.
*Did you spot the trap? If your first thought was “12 + 10 = 22,” you were already inside the trap — the right answer lives one subtraction away.*

QUESTION 5
Exam: RRB Group D
An unbiased coin is tossed 4 times. What is the probability that there are at least 3 heads?
(A) 5/32 (B) 1/4 (C) 5/16 (D) 1/2

Solution
Total outcomes = 2⁴ = 16.
“At least 3 heads” means exactly 3 heads OR exactly 4 heads.
Exactly 4 heads: {HHHH} → 1 way.
Exactly 3 heads: ⁴C₃ = 4 ways.
Favourable = 5. P = 5/16.

Answer: (C) 5/16

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Even‑Odds” Gravity
Option (D) 1/2 pulls you toward a false symmetry: “At least 3 heads” feels like half the coin’s story. Option (A) 5/32 is the probability including the denominator for 5 coins (2⁵ = 32) — a slip in counting total outcomes. The brain’s habit of rounding questions to “roughly 50‑50” makes (D) dangerously attractive. Systematic counting (⁴C₃ + ⁴C₄) kills the illusion instantly.
*Did you spot the trap? If you looked at the options and felt 1/2 was “about right,” the gravity field was pulling you in.*

QUESTION 6
Exam: SSC CGL Tier 2 (10 August 2022, Statistics)
If P(A) = 0.4, P(B∣A) = 0.05, P(C∣A) = 0.04, P(B∣A∩C) = 0.09, P(C∣A∩B) = 0.07, then the probability of occurrence of all events equals:
(A) 0.0016 (B) 0.002 (C) 0.0014 (D) 0.0018

Solution
P(all three) = P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A) × P(B∣A) × P(C∣A∩B) = 0.4 × 0.05 × 0.07 = 0.0014.
(The chain rule uses P(C∣A∩B) because C is conditioned on both A and B having occurred.)

Answer: (C) 0.0014

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Too Many Numbers” Overload
The question deliberately throws four conditional‑probability numbers at you, knowing the mind will grab the wrong chain link. P(B∣A∩C) = 0.09 is a decoy — it reverses the conditioning order. The exhausted brain says, “I’ll just multiply 0.05 × 0.09 — wait, is that right?” The test is not mathematical difficulty; it is attention regulation under time pressure.
Did you spot the trap? If you used 0.09 instead of 0.07, the overload tactic worked on you exactly as designed.

QUESTION 7
Exam: RRB NTPC 2021 (Memory Based)
A, B, and C throw a die in succession till one gets a ‘six’ and wins the game (starting with A). Find the probability of A’s winning.
(A) 36/91 (B) 25/91 (C) 30/91 (D) 41/91

Solution
P(A wins on 1st throw) = 1/6.
P(A wins on 2nd attempt) — requires all three to fail once, then A succeeds: (5/6)³ × (1/6).
P(A wins on 3rd attempt): (5/6)⁶ × (1/6).
This forms an infinite geometric series with first term a = 1/6 and common ratio r = (5/6)³ = 125/216.
Sum = a / (1 – r) = (1/6) / (1 – 125/216) = (1/6) / (91/216) = (1/6) × (216/91) = 36/91.

Answer: (A) 36/91

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Probability = 1/3” Delusion
Because there are three players, the untrained mind defaults to “equal chance → 1/3 each.” But the turn order matters immensely — the first mover has a structural advantage. The denominator 91 looks so foreign that many candidates abandon the question entirely. The exam‑setter knows that infinite series terrifies aspirants; the psychological win is not in solving it, but in not running away and spotting the geometric‑series pattern.
*Did you spot the trap? If you thought “three players, so P = 1/3,” the question achieved its psychological defeat before you even picked up the pen.*

QUESTION 8
Exam: SBI PO Mains 2017
A bag contains 5 black, 3 yellow, 5 blue, and 2 red balls. One ball is drawn and found to be red. Then another ball is drawn without replacement. What is the probability that the second ball is also red?
(A) 1/7 (B) 1/14 (C) 3/14 (D) 2/7

Solution
After removing one red ball: remaining balls = 14; remaining red balls = 1.
P(second red ∣ first red) = 1/14.

Answer: (B) 1/14

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Reset Button” Illusion
This is a conditional probability problem masquerading as an unconditional one. The brain sees “another ball is drawn” and wants to revert to the original 2/15 probability, forgetting that the bag has already changed. Option (D) 2/7 amplifies the confusion by presenting a number that roughly halves the original proportion. The key psychological test is whether you subtract the first outcome from the sample space before computing the next probability.
*Did you spot the trap? If you computed 2/15 (or something like it) for the second draw, the “reset” illusion caught you.*

QUESTION 9
Exam: SSC CGL Tier 2
A bag contains 8 tennis balls and some cricket balls. Two balls are drawn at random. The probability that both are cricket balls is 1/5. Find the total number of balls in the bag.
(A) 20 (B) 15 (C) 12 (D) 16

Solution
Let x = number of cricket balls. Total balls = 8 + x.
P(both cricket) = [ˣC₂] / [⁸⁺ˣC₂] = [x(x‑1)/2] ÷ [(8+x)(7+x)/2] = [x(x‑1)] / [(8+x)(7+x)] = 1/5.
Cross‑multiply: 5x(x‑1) = (8+x)(7+x) → 5x² – 5x = x² + 15x + 56 → 4x² – 20x – 56 = 0 → x² – 5x – 14 = 0 → (x‑7)(x+2) = 0 → x = 7.
Total balls = 8 + 7 = 15.

Answer: (B) 15

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Number Feels Familiar” Bias
The total‑count options (12, 15, 16, 20) are all numbers that appear frequently in other probability contexts (36 for dice, 52 for cards). The brain subconsciously maps “20” to “a nice round number” and gravitates toward it. The trap is choosing (A) 20 without solving, because the equation feels tedious. The psychological victory lies in calmly setting up the ˣC₂ structure rather than guessing.
Did you spot the trap? If you glanced at 20 and thought “looks about right,” your brain took the shortcut the examiner laid out.

QUESTION 10
Exam: SSC CGL Tier 2 (2024)
Three coins are tossed simultaneously. What is the probability that the number of tails is more than the number of heads?
(A) 1/2 (B) 3/8 (C) 1/4 (D) 3/4

Solution
Sample space: 2³ = 8 outcomes: {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}.
“Tails > Heads” means 2 tails 1 head OR 3 tails 0 heads.
Favourable: {HTT, THT, TTH, TTT} → 4 outcomes.
P = 4/8 = 1/2.

Answer: (A) 1/2

🧠 Trap Analysis — The “Equal‑Division” Reflex
The phrase “more than” triggers a subtle counting bias: the brain counts the 2‑tails‑and‑1‑head cases (3 outcomes) but often forgets the “3 tails” case (TTT) because “more than” feels like it means “strictly more but not all.” Option (B) 3/8 corresponds exactly to that mistake (3/8 = only the 2‑tail cases). The exam‑setter knows that “tails > heads” translates in the mind to “exactly 2 tails” — and leaves the TTT outcome beautifully invisible until you systematically enumerate.
*Did you spot the trap? If you got 3/8, you missed TTT — and proved the examiner’s psychological bet exactly right.*

🎯 FINAL DRILL — THE 7‑SECOND TRAP‑SPOTTING PROTOCOL
Before starting any probability question, silently run this mental checklist:

Did I write down the sample space (S)?

Does it say “at least”? → Complement rule.

Is there “replacement” or not? → Determines independence.

Are events mutually exclusive or overlapping? → Check before adding.

Does the denominator match my total outcomes? → Cross‑verify.
