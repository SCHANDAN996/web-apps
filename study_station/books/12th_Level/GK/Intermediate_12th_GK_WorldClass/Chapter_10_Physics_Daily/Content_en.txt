# Physics in Everyday Life

## 🌟 The Hook: Why Your Tea Cools, But the Sky Doesn't Fall

In 1666, legend says, an apple fell near Isaac Newton — and instead of just eating it, he asked *why it fell down and not up*. That one question gave us the law of gravitation. But here's the surprise: you don't need a laboratory to see physics. Every morning you perform dozens of physics experiments without knowing it — the pressure cooker whistles, the bicycle stays upright, the mirror shows your face, and the mobile in your pocket talks to a tower kilometres away.

In competitive exams like SSC CHSL, RRB NTPC and State PCS, "everyday physics" questions are among the most scoring — because they test **concepts, not formulas**. If you understand *why* things happen, you can answer in 15 seconds flat.

## 1. Mechanics Around Us: Force, Pressure and Friction

### Why does a sharp knife cut better?

Pressure = Force ÷ Area. A sharp knife has a very thin edge, so the same force acts on a tiny area — creating **huge pressure** that slices through vegetables. The same logic explains why:

- **School bags have broad straps** — larger area, less pressure on shoulders.
- **Nails have pointed tips** — small area, high pressure, easy to hammer in.
- **Camels walk easily on sand** — wide feet spread their weight, so they don't sink.

### Why doesn't a moving bicycle fall?

A moving cycle stays balanced because of the **gyroscopic effect** of the spinning wheels and the rider's continuous small corrections. Friction between tyres and road provides the grip. On a wet road, friction drops — that's why vehicles skid.

**Friction is a necessary evil**: it lets us walk, write and brake, but it also wears out machine parts and shoe soles.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why do tyres have treads (grooves)? Why is it harder to walk on ice than on concrete? Answer both before reading on.

## 2. Heat in the Kitchen and Beyond

### The pressure cooker: cooking faster with physics

Water normally boils at **100°C** (at sea level). Inside a sealed pressure cooker, steam raises the pressure, and water boils at about **120°C**. Higher temperature = food cooks faster. This is also why cooking takes *longer* on hills — atmospheric pressure is lower there, so water boils below 100°C.

### Conduction, Convection, Radiation — the three heat highways

| Mode | Needs a medium? | Daily example |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction | Yes (solids) | Metal spoon getting hot in tea |
| Convection | Yes (fluids/gases) | Room heater warming air; sea breeze |
| Radiation | **No** | Sun's heat reaching Earth through space |

**Why is a thermos flask double-walled?** The vacuum between the walls stops conduction and convection; the silvered surfaces reflect radiation. Hot stays hot, cold stays cold.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which mode of heat transfer works in empty space? Why do we wear white clothes in summer? (Hint: white reflects radiant heat.)

## 3. Light: Mirrors, Lenses and Everyday Magic

### Why does a straw look bent in a glass of water?

Light changes speed when it moves from water to air — this bending is called **refraction**. The same effect explains:

- A swimming pool looks **shallower** than it really is.
- Stars **twinkle** (refraction through turbulent air layers); planets don't twinkle because they appear as tiny discs, not points.
- The Sun is visible **before actual sunrise** and after actual sunset (atmospheric refraction).

### Mirrors you use daily

- **Rear-view mirror in vehicles** → *Convex* mirror: gives a wider field of view, image is erect and diminished.
- **Shaving/makeup mirror, dentist's mirror** → *Concave* mirror: gives an enlarged, erect image when the face is close.
- **Torch and car headlights** → concave reflector throws a parallel beam.

### Why is the sky blue?

Sunlight is scattered by air molecules. Blue light scatters the most (Rayleigh scattering), so the sky looks blue. At sunset, light travels through more atmosphere — blue is scattered away, leaving **red/orange**, which is why the setting Sun looks red. Danger signals are red because red scatters the *least* and travels farthest through fog.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Convex or concave — which mirror is used as a rear-view mirror, and why? Why are danger signals red and not blue?

## 4. Sound: Echoes, Stethoscopes and Silence in Space

Sound is a **mechanical wave** — it needs a medium. That's why sound cannot travel through vacuum (space is silent!). Light from lightning reaches us almost instantly; thunder arrives later because sound travels at only about **332 m/s** in air (much slower than light's 3 × 10⁸ m/s).

- **Echo** = reflected sound; to hear a distinct echo, the reflecting surface must be at least about **17 m** away.
- **Stethoscope** works on multiple reflection of sound inside the tube.
- **Ultrasound** (frequency above 20,000 Hz) is used in sonography and SONAR; humans hear only **20 Hz – 20,000 Hz**.
- Sound travels **fastest in solids**, slower in liquids, slowest in gases.

🧠 **Active Recall:** You see lightning and hear thunder 5 seconds later. Roughly how far is the storm? (≈ 332 × 5 ≈ 1.6 km.)

## 5. Electricity at Home

- **Why is the fuse wire special?** It is made of a low-melting-point alloy (tin-lead). Excess current heats and melts it, breaking the circuit and saving appliances.
- **Why are household circuits connected in parallel?** So each appliance gets full voltage (220 V in India) and can be switched independently.
- **Why does a bird sitting on a live wire not get shocked?** Current flows only when there is a potential difference across the body; both its feet are at the same potential.
- **Electric bulb**: the filament is **tungsten** (very high melting point ≈ 3422°C); the bulb is filled with inert gas (argon/nitrogen) to prevent oxidation.
- **Earthing** protects us from electric shock by sending leakage current into the ground.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Series or parallel — how are the fans and lights in your home connected, and why?

## 6. Floatation and Fluids

- **Archimedes' principle**: a body immersed in a fluid loses weight equal to the weight of fluid displaced. An iron ship floats because its hollow shape displaces enough water; a solid iron nail sinks.
- **Hydrogen balloons rise** because hydrogen is lighter than air (buoyancy).
- **Ice floats on water** because ice is less dense than water — and about 9/10 of an iceberg stays hidden underwater.
- **It is easier to swim in sea water** than in river water — salt water is denser, giving more upthrust.

## 🧠 Mnemonics to Lock It In

1. **"SCR" for heat transfer** — **S**olid (Conduction), **C**urrent of fluid (Convection), **R**ays (Radiation). Remember: *"Sun Comes Radiating."*
2. **"CC-RR" for mirrors** — **C**onvex for **C**ars (rear-view), **C**oncave for **R**azor (shaving) and **R**eflectors (torches).
3. **"SLG" for sound speed** — fastest in **S**olids, then **L**iquids, slowest in **G**ases. *"Sound Loves Glass (solid)."*
4. **"20–20K"** — human hearing range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Near-Correct Statement (लगभग सही कथन):**
*"Sound travels fastest in gases."* — 90% of students half-remember "sound needs a medium" and mark this true. The truth: sound is fastest in **solids**. One swapped word makes the whole statement false. Read every word.

**Trap 2 — Reverse Causation (विपरीत कार्य-कारण):**
*"Water boils at a lower temperature on hills, so food cooks faster there."* — The first half is true, but the conclusion is reversed. Lower boiling point means **cooler** boiling water, so cooking is **slower** on hills. Examiners love joining a true cause to a false effect.

**Trap 3 — Similar Names / Mirror Confusion (समान नाम):**
Questions swap **convex** and **concave**: "A dentist uses a convex mirror" — false, it's **concave**. Similarly, "twinkling of stars" is often confused with "scattering of light" — twinkling is due to **refraction**, blue sky due to **scattering**. Keep the pairs separate.

## 🔗 Static-Dynamic Link

The physics in this chapter is timeless, but it quietly powers today's headlines: **SONAR and ultrasound** principles are used in modern naval systems and medical imaging; **fibre-optic communication** (total internal reflection — an extension of refraction) carries India's 5G and broadband data; **pressure-temperature principles** are at work in ISRO's cryogenic rocket engines; and **electromagnetic induction** runs your kitchen's induction stove. When a current-affairs question mentions a technology, ask yourself: *which everyday physics principle is underneath?*
