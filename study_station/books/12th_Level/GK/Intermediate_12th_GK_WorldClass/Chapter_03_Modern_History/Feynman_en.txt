# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Modern Indian History

## The Most Confusing Pair: Subsidiary Alliance vs Doctrine of Lapse

Ask any aspirant what trips them up in Modern Indian History, and most will point to these two. Both were weapons the British used to swallow Indian states. Both sound similar. Both appear in the same exam questions — often as two of the four options, deliberately placed side by side to trap you. Let's fix this forever.

### Explain It Like You're 12

Imagine your neighbourhood has many small shops, and one giant supermarket chain wants to own them all. The chain uses two different tricks:

**Trick 1 — The "Bodyguard" Deal (Subsidiary Alliance, Lord Wellesley, 1798):**
The supermarket tells a shopkeeper: "I'll give you free security guards to protect your shop. But you must pay for their salaries, you can't hire any other guards, and you can't even talk to other shopkeepers without my permission." Sounds helpful, right? But soon the shopkeeper can't afford the guards' salaries, falls into debt, and the supermarket quietly takes over the shop. The shopkeeper is still sitting behind the counter — but he owns nothing.

That is the **Subsidiary Alliance**. The Indian ruler keeps his throne, but loses his army, his foreign policy, and eventually his money. The state is not annexed on paper — it is hollowed out from inside. Hyderabad (1798) was the first to accept it, followed by Mysore, Tanjore, Awadh, Peshwa, Scindia, Gaekwad and others.

**Trick 2 — The "No Heir, No Shop" Rule (Doctrine of Lapse, Lord Dalhousie, 1848–56):**
Now imagine the supermarket announces: "If a shopkeeper dies without a *biological* son, the shop automatically becomes mine. Adopted sons don't count." In India, adopting an heir was a centuries-old, perfectly legal tradition. The British simply refused to recognise it — and grabbed the state.

That is the **Doctrine of Lapse**. Satara (1848) was the first victim, then Jaitpur, Sambalpur, Baghat, Udaipur, Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854). Rani Lakshmibai's adopted son was rejected — one of the sparks of 1857.

### The One-Line Difference

- **Subsidiary Alliance** = control the *living* ruler (alive, but a puppet).
- **Doctrine of Lapse** = swallow the state when the ruler *dies* (without a natural heir).

### The Unforgettable Trick

**"Wellesley Watches, Dalhousie Devours."**
- **W**ellesley → **W**atches over the ruler like a "guardian" → Subsidiary Alliance.
- **D**alhousie → **D**evours the state after **D**eath → Doctrine of Lapse.

Memory hook for order: **"SATara JAITpur SAMbalpur — JHANSI NAGPUR"** — Satara first, Jhansi-Nagpur last, all under Dalhousie.

### Examiner's Trap Alert

- Awadh was annexed in 1856 on charges of **misgovernance (maladministration)** — NOT under the Doctrine of Lapse. This is the favourite trap.
- The Subsidiary Alliance did not annex states immediately; it made them dependent. Don't mark "annexation" as its direct result.
- Don't confuse **Jaitpur** (annexed under Lapse) with **Jaipur** — examiners love placing both in the options.

### Quick Question for You

If a question says *"This policy allowed the British to annex a state when its ruler died without a natural male heir"* — which policy is it, and which Governor-General used it? If you answered "Doctrine of Lapse — Dalhousie" in under 5 seconds, this concept is yours forever.
