Card 1
Front: Which gas makes up about 78% of the atmosphere, and why do plants still need help to use it?
Back: Nitrogen. Plants cannot absorb atmospheric N₂ directly; it must be "fixed" into nitrates by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium in legume roots) or lightning.

Card 2
Front: What is the difference between "ecology" and "ecosystem"?
Back: Ecology is the scientific study of interactions between organisms and their environment (term coined by Ernst Haeckel, 1866). An ecosystem is the functional unit itself — biotic + abiotic components interacting (term coined by A.G. Tansley, 1935).

Card 3
Front: In a food chain, why does energy drop by about 90% at each level?
Back: The 10% law (Lindeman, 1942): only about 10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level; the rest is lost as heat, respiration, and undigested matter. This is why food chains rarely exceed 4–5 levels.

Card 4
Front: Which pyramid can NEVER be inverted — number, biomass, or energy?
Back: The pyramid of energy is always upright. Pyramids of number and biomass can be inverted (e.g., a single tree supporting many insects; aquatic ecosystems where phytoplankton biomass is less than zooplankton at a moment).

Card 5
Front: Memory trick: How do you remember the order of ecological organisation — organism to biosphere?
Back: Mnemonic: "OPCEB" — Organism → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biosphere. ("Old People Can Eat Bread.")

Card 6
Front: What is the ozone layer made of, where is it found, and which rays does it block?
Back: Ozone (O₃), concentrated in the stratosphere (roughly 15–35 km altitude). It absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation from the Sun.

Card 7
Front: Examiner's trap: Is the greenhouse effect itself bad for Earth?
Back: No — the natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm enough for life (without it, average temperature would be about −18°C). The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect from excess CO₂, methane, etc., causing global warming. Exams often twist this into "greenhouse effect is harmful" — a false statement.

Card 8
Front: Which gas is the biggest contributor to global warming, and which has the highest warming potential per molecule among common gases?
Back: CO₂ contributes the most overall (mainly from burning fossil fuels). Per molecule, CFCs and methane trap far more heat than CO₂ — methane is roughly 25+ times more potent over 100 years.

Card 9
Front: What is biomagnification, and which famous pollutant demonstrates it?
Back: The increasing concentration of a non-degradable substance (like DDT or mercury) at successively higher trophic levels. DDT sprayed on water bodies concentrates from plankton → fish → birds, causing eggshell thinning in birds of prey.

Card 10
Front: What are the two types of ecological succession, and how do they differ?
Back: Primary succession begins on bare surfaces with no soil (bare rock, new lava, sand dunes). Secondary succession begins where soil already exists after a disturbance (abandoned farmland, burnt forest) — it is faster.

Card 11
Front: Memory trick: How do you quickly recall the major biogeochemical cycles?
Back: Mnemonic: "CHNOPS" — Carbon, Hydrogen, Nitrogen, Oxygen, Phosphorus, Sulphur. Remember: phosphorus cycle has NO gaseous phase (sedimentary cycle), unlike carbon and nitrogen (gaseous cycles).

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: Which is the largest ecosystem of the world — forest or ocean?
Back: The ocean (marine ecosystem) is the largest ecosystem, covering about 71% of Earth's surface. Exams offer "tropical rainforest" as a tempting wrong option — that is only the most biodiverse terrestrial ecosystem.

Card 13
Front: What is the difference between a food chain and a food web?
Back: A food chain is a single linear sequence of who-eats-whom. A food web is the network of many interlinked food chains — it gives an ecosystem stability, since organisms have alternative food sources.

Card 14
Front: What do producers, consumers, and decomposers each do — and which group is missing from a simple grazing food chain diagram?
Back: Producers (green plants) make food via photosynthesis; consumers eat other organisms; decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter, recycling nutrients. Decomposers are often left out of diagrams but are essential — a favourite exam gap.

Card 15
Front: What is an "ecological niche"?
Back: The functional role and position of a species in its ecosystem — what it eats, where it lives, how it interacts. Habitat is the "address"; niche is the "profession."

Card 16
Front: Which day is World Environment Day, and which UN body leads it?
Back: 5 June, led by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP). It marks the start of the 1972 Stockholm Conference — the first major UN conference on the environment.

Card 17
Front: What is eutrophication?
Back: Nutrient enrichment (nitrates, phosphates from fertilisers/sewage) of a water body → algal bloom → oxygen depletion → death of aquatic life. The oxygen-depleted zones are called "dead zones."

Card 18
Front: What is the difference between in-situ and ex-situ conservation?
Back: In-situ: conserving species in their natural habitat (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves). Ex-situ: conserving outside the natural habitat (zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks).

Card 19
Front: What is the "edge effect" in an ecotone?
Back: An ecotone is the transition zone between two ecosystems (e.g., forest–grassland). It often has greater species diversity and density than either adjoining ecosystem — this richness is called the edge effect.

Card 20
Front: Which organisms are called "pioneer species" in primary succession on bare rock?
Back: Lichens (and mosses). They secrete acids that weather rock, trap dust, and slowly form the first thin soil, allowing larger plants to follow.
