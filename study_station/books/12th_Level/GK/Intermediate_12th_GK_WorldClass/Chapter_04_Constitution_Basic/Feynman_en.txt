# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Indian Constitution (Basic)

## The Trickiest Concept: Fundamental Rights vs Directive Principles (FR vs DPSP)

Students lose the most marks here — not because the concept is hard, but because the two look like twins. Let's fix that forever.

### Explain it like you're 12

Imagine your house has two lists stuck on the fridge:

**List 1 — "Things Mom MUST give me, no excuses"**
Food when I'm hungry, a school to go to, freedom to speak at the dinner table. If Mom refuses, I can complain to Grandpa (the Judge), and Grandpa can *order* Mom to fix it.

**List 2 — "Things Mom SHOULD try to arrange when possible"**
A bigger house, a bicycle for everyone, saving money for the future. These are good goals, but if the bicycle doesn't come this year, I *cannot* drag Mom to Grandpa's court. Grandpa will say, "She's trying her best — this is a wish-list, not a command."

That's the whole chapter:

- **Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)** = List 1. **Enforceable** — you can go directly to the Supreme Court (Article 32) or High Court (Article 226). Dr. Ambedkar called Article 32 the *"heart and soul of the Constitution."*
- **Directive Principles (Part IV, Articles 36–51)** = List 2. **Non-enforceable** — guidelines for the government to make a welfare state. Borrowed from the **Irish Constitution**. No court can force the government to follow them.

### The daily-life memory hook

> **"FR = FIR, DPSP = Diary"**
> - **FR → FIR**: If your Fundamental Right is violated, it's serious enough to file a complaint — the court *must* act.
> - **DPSP → Diary**: Directive Principles are like goals written in a diary — nice to achieve, but nobody gets punished for not finishing them.

### The examiner's favourite traps (so you never fall)

1. **"DPSPs are enforceable by courts"** — FALSE. Only Fundamental Rights are.
2. **"DPSPs are useless"** — FALSE. Article 37 says they are *fundamental in the governance of the country*; many laws (MGNREGA, minimum wages) come from them.
3. **Source confusion**: FRs ← inspired by **USA**; DPSPs ← **Ireland**; Emergency provisions ← **Germany**. Examiners swap these.
4. **Which wins in a fight?** Generally FRs get priority, but after the **42nd Amendment (1976)** and the *Minerva Mills case (1980)*, the rule is: **harmony and balance** — neither is superior; both must be read together.
