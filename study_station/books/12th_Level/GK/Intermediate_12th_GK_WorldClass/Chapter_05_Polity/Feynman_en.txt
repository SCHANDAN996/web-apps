# 🧒 The Feynman Corner — Indian Polity

## The Concept That Confuses Everyone: The Basic Structure Doctrine

Ask any aspirant: *"Can Parliament change anything in the Constitution?"* Most will shout "Yes! Article 368 gives Parliament the power to amend!" — and walk straight into the examiner's trap. Let's fix this forever, the Feynman way.

---

## Explain It Like I'm 12

Imagine your school has a rulebook. The school council (like Parliament) is allowed to **change the rules** — they can change the lunch menu, the sports day date, even the uniform colour. That's their legal power.

But one day, the council passes a new rule: *"From now on, the council can never be removed, and students can never vote again."*

Wait. Is that allowed? They DO have the power to change rules... but this change **destroys the very idea of a school council**. It changes the *soul* of the rulebook, not just a rule.

That's exactly what the **Basic Structure Doctrine** says:

- **Article 368** gives Parliament the power to amend the Constitution. ✅
- But the Supreme Court (in the **Kesavananda Bharati case, 1973**) said: Parliament can amend anything — *except* the **basic structure** of the Constitution. ❌
- You can repaint the house, change the furniture, even add a floor — but you **cannot demolish the foundation**.

---

## A Daily-Life Example

Think of a **cricket team**. The captain can change the batting order, the bowlers, the field placements — that's "amendment power". But if the captain says *"From today, there will be no batting at all"* — that's not a change, that's **killing the game itself**. The umpire (Supreme Court) steps in and says: *"Not allowed. You've destroyed the basic structure of cricket."*

---

## What Counts as "Basic Structure"?

The Court never gave one fixed list (that's deliberate — it keeps the doctrine flexible). But over the years, judgements have named these as part of the basic structure:

- Supremacy of the Constitution
- Republic and democratic form of government
- Secularism
- Federal character
- Separation of powers (Legislature, Executive, Judiciary)
- Judicial review
- Free and fair elections
- Rule of law
- Fundamental Rights (core essence)

---

## The Unforgettable Trick 🪄

**"KESAVANANDA = the LOCK on Article 368's door."**

Remember it as a sentence:
> *"Parliament has the key (Article 368), but Kesavananda Bharati (1973) put a lock on the foundation — the Basic Structure."*

Memory hook for the year: **1973** — the same decade as the Emergency debate on Parliament's power. And the follow-up: **Minerva Mills case (1980)** — the Court struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment that tried to give Parliament *unlimited* amending power. Trick: **"Minerva mined the 42nd."**

---

## The Examiner's Trap 🪤

| Trap Statement | Truth |
|---|---|
| "Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including the basic structure." | ❌ False — Kesavananda Bharati (1973) forbids this. |
| "The basic structure is written in Article 368." | ❌ False — it is a **judicial doctrine**, not written anywhere in the Constitution. |
| "The doctrine came from the Golaknath case." | ❌ False — Golaknath (1967) was about Fundamental Rights; the doctrine was born in **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)**. |
| "The 24th Amendment (1971) created the basic structure doctrine." | ❌ False — it only said Parliament *can* amend Fundamental Rights; the *limit* came in 1973. |
