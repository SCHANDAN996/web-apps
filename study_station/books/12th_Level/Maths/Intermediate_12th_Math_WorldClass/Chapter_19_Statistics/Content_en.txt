## The Hook: The Average That Almost Killed the Air Force

In the 1950s, the US Air Force designed cockpits based on the **average** dimensions of thousands of pilots — average height, average arm length, average leg length. The result? Not a single pilot actually fit the "average" cockpit. When researcher Gilbert Daniels measured 4,063 pilots on 10 dimensions, **zero** pilots were average on all ten.

The lesson is powerful: **an average alone can lie to you.** You also need to know how spread out the data is. That single idea — central value *plus* spread — is the entire soul of this chapter, and of almost every Statistics question in SSC CGL, IBPS PO, and RRB NTPC.

## 1. What Is Data?

**Data** is a collection of facts — numbers, measurements, observations.

- **Raw (ungrouped) data:** values listed as collected. Example: marks of 5 students — 45, 62, 58, 70, 62.
- **Grouped data:** values organised into class intervals. Example: 0–10, 10–20, 20–30 with frequencies.
- **Discrete data:** countable values (number of children: 0, 1, 2, 3…).
- **Continuous data:** measurable values that can take any value in a range (height: 162.3 cm, 162.35 cm…).

**Frequency** = how many times a value occurs. **Cumulative frequency (c.f.)** = running total of frequencies, built by adding each frequency to the sum of all previous ones.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what is the difference between discrete and continuous data? Give one example of each. Say it out loud before reading on.

## 2. Central Tendency — The "Centre" of Data

A measure of central tendency is a single value that represents the whole dataset. There are three heroes here: **Mean, Median, Mode.**

### Mean (Arithmetic Average)

**Mean = Sum of all observations ÷ Number of observations**

x̄ = Σx / n

Example: marks 45, 62, 58, 70, 62 → Sum = 297, n = 5 → Mean = 297/5 = **59.4**

For grouped data (frequency distribution):

x̄ = Σfᵢxᵢ / Σfᵢ

where xᵢ is the value (or class mid-point) and fᵢ its frequency.

**Shortcut — Assumed Mean method:** pick a convenient number *a* near the middle, compute deviations dᵢ = xᵢ − a, then:

x̄ = a + (Σfᵢdᵢ / Σfᵢ)

This keeps numbers small and saves precious exam seconds.

### Median — The Middle Value

Arrange data in **ascending order** first — this step is non-negotiable.

- If n is **odd**: Median = value of the ((n+1)/2)th observation.
- If n is **even**: Median = average of the (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th observations.

Example: 12, 15, 18, 22, 30 → n = 5 (odd) → Median = 3rd value = **18**.
Example: 12, 15, 18, 22 → n = 4 (even) → Median = (15 + 18)/2 = **16.5**.

For grouped data: find the **median class** (where cumulative frequency first reaches or crosses N/2), then:

Median = L + [(N/2 − C) / f] × h

where L = lower limit of median class, N = total frequency, C = c.f. before the median class, f = frequency of median class, h = class width.

### Mode — The Most Frequent Value

**Mode = the value that appears most often.**

Example: 45, 62, 58, 70, 62 → Mode = **62** (appears twice).

For grouped data, the class with the highest frequency is the **modal class**, and:

Mode = L + [(f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] × h

where f₁ = frequency of modal class, f₀ = frequency of the class before it, f₂ = frequency of the class after it.

🧠 **Active Recall:** A dataset has 8 values. Which two positions do you average to get the median? And what is the mode of 3, 7, 7, 9, 9, 9, 12? (Answers: 4th & 5th; mode = 9.)

### The Golden Relationship

For a moderately skewed distribution:

**Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean**

This empirical relation is a favourite of SSC and Banking examiners — memorise it cold.

**Memory Trick 🎪 — "The M³ Family":** Imagine three brothers at a dinner table. **MEAN** brother *shares everything equally* (total ÷ count). **MEDIAN** brother always sits in the *middle seat*. **MODE** brother is the *most popular* — everyone copies him. Mean = equal share, Median = middle, Mode = most. And the family formula: the popular one (Mode) = 3 × middle brother − 2 × sharing brother.

## 3. Quartiles and Percentiles

Just as the median splits data into two halves, **quartiles** split it into four equal parts:

- **Q₁ (first quartile):** 25% of data lies below it — the median of the lower half.
- **Q₂:** the median itself.
- **Q₃ (third quartile):** 75% of data lies below it — the median of the upper half.

**Percentiles** divide data into 100 parts. The 90th percentile means 90% of values lie below it. Competitive exams mostly ask Q₁, Q₃, and the **interquartile range = Q₃ − Q₁**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If Q₃ = 80 and Q₁ = 50, what is the interquartile range? What does the 75th percentile mean in plain words?

## 4. Dispersion — How Spread Out Is the Data?

Two classes can both have a mean of 60, yet in one class everyone scored 58–62, and in the other, scores range from 10 to 100. Same centre, totally different story. **Dispersion** measures that story.

### Range

**Range = Maximum value − Minimum value**

Simple, but crude — it ignores everything in between.

### Mean Deviation

The average of the **absolute** distances of each value from the mean (or median):

Mean Deviation = Σ|xᵢ − x̄| / n

We use absolute values because negative and positive deviations would cancel out.

### Variance and Standard Deviation — The Exam Royalty

**Variance (σ²)** = average of the *squared* deviations from the mean:

σ² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n

**Standard Deviation (σ)** = √Variance

Squaring does two jobs: it removes negative signs *and* punishes large deviations more heavily. Standard deviation brings the unit back to the original (from "marks²" back to "marks").

**Handy computation formula:**

σ² = (Σx² / n) − x̄²

i.e., *mean of squares minus square of mean*. This avoids computing every deviation separately.

### Coefficient of Variation (CV) — Comparing Apples with Oranges

**CV = (σ / x̄) × 100%**

Since CV is unit-free, it compares consistency across datasets. **Lower CV = more consistent.** If a batsman has a lower CV of scores than another, he is more consistent — even if his average is lower. This is a classic IBPS/SSC question.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Write the formula for CV from memory. If Team A: mean 50, σ = 5 and Team B: mean 40, σ = 6 — which is more consistent? (CV_A = 10%, CV_B = 15% → Team A.)

## 5. Properties Examiners Love to Test

1. **Adding a constant** to every value: mean, median, mode all increase by that constant; **σ and variance stay unchanged.**
2. **Multiplying every value by k:** mean, median, mode multiply by k; **σ multiplies by |k|; variance multiplies by k².**
3. If all values are equal, σ = 0.
4. Mean is affected by **extreme values (outliers)**; median and mode are not. That's why "average income" reports often prefer the median.

**Trap alert:** "Variance doubles when each observation is doubled" — FALSE. Variance becomes **4 times** (k² = 4). Examiners love this trap.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Every student's marks are increased by 5 grace marks. What happens to the mean? To the standard deviation? (Mean +5; σ unchanged.)

## Before You Close This Chapter

Statistics is not about memorising symbols — it is about asking two questions of any data: **"Where is the centre?"** and **"How far does it spread?"** Master mean–median–mode for the first question and variance–SD–CV for the second, and 90% of exam questions fall into your lap.
