# Geometry — The Art of Seeing Shapes

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

2. Conquer triangles — congruence, similarity, Pythagoras, and special centres.

## The Hook: A Shape That Fooled the World

In 1958, a mathematician named Roger Penrose drew a triangle that **cannot exist** — the famous "Penrose Triangle." It looks perfectly real on paper, yet no carpenter could ever build it. Millions of people stare at it and swear it's possible.

That's the secret of geometry: **your eyes can lie, but the rules never do.**

Every year, lakhs of SSC and Banking aspirants lose marks in geometry not because the questions are hard, but because they *trust their eyes* instead of trusting the theorems. A figure that "looks like" an isosceles triangle may not be one. An angle that "looks like" 90° may be 80°. The examiners know this — and they set traps exactly there.

This chapter will train you to stop guessing and start *proving*. And here's the good news: geometry is the **most rule-based** part of maths. Learn roughly 20 theorems cold, and 90% of exam questions become mechanical.

## Part 1: Lines and Angles — The Alphabet of Geometry

Everything in geometry is built from three undefined ideas: **point, line, and plane**. From these, we build angles.

**Key facts you must know cold:**

- A **straight angle** = 180°. A **complete angle** = 360°. A **right angle** = 90°.
- **Complementary angles** add to 90°. **Supplementary angles** add to 180°.
- When two lines intersect, **vertically opposite angles are equal**.
- **Angles around a point** add up to 360°.

Now the exam favourite: a **transversal** cutting two parallel lines. This single diagram generates hundreds of questions. Remember three pairs:

1. **Corresponding angles** are equal (they sit in the same "corner" position).
2. **Alternate interior angles** are equal (they form a "Z" shape).
3. **Co-interior (same-side interior) angles** are supplementary (they form a "C" or "U" shape).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One angle is 65°. What are its corresponding angle, alternate interior angle, and co-interior angle? (Answers: 65°, 65°, 115°.)

### 🎵 Mnemonic: "Z-F-C-U"

Trace letters on the diagram:
- **Z** → Alternate angles are equal.
- **F** → Corresponding angles are equal.
- **C/U** → Co-interior angles add to 180°.

If you can spot a Z, F, or C hiding in the figure, the question is already solved. Examiners love rotating the diagram so the Z is upside down — train your eyes to hunt for the letters, not the orientation.

## Part 2: Triangles — The King of Geometry

If geometry were a kingdom, the triangle would wear the crown. Roughly half of all exam geometry questions involve triangles directly or indirectly.

### 2.1 Angle Sum and Exterior Angle

- The three interior angles of a triangle always add to **180°**.
- An **exterior angle** equals the **sum of the two opposite interior angles**. (This one line solves dozens of "find x" questions instantly.)

### 2.2 Congruence — When Two Triangles Are Identical Twins

Two triangles are congruent if they match by any of these tests:

| Test | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **SSS** | All three sides equal |
| **SAS** | Two sides + included angle equal |
| **ASA / AAS** | Two angles + a side equal |
| **RHS** | Right angle + hypotenuse + one side equal |

⚠️ **Trap alert:** "AAA" (three equal angles) does **NOT** prove congruence — it only proves *similarity*. And "SSA" (two sides + a non-included angle) is not a valid test either. Examiners use both as wrong options constantly.

### 2.3 Similarity — Same Shape, Different Size

Two triangles are similar if their angles match (AA is enough). Then:

- Corresponding sides are in the **same ratio** (say k).
- Ratio of **areas = k²** (the square of the side ratio).

That last point is a goldmine. If two similar triangles have sides in ratio 2:3, their areas are in ratio **4:9** — not 2:3. This is one of the most-tested traps in SSC CGL.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Triangle ABC ~ Triangle DEF with AB:DE = 3:5. If area of ABC is 27 cm², what is area of DEF? (Answer: 27 × 25/9 = 75 cm².)

### 2.4 Pythagoras and Friends

In a right-angled triangle: **a² + b² = c²** (c = hypotenuse).

Memorise the common **Pythagorean triplets** — they appear in exams with the numbers barely disguised:

- (3, 4, 5) and multiples: (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15)…
- (5, 12, 13)
- (8, 15, 17)
- (7, 24, 25)

If you see legs 12 and 16, don't calculate — recognise (3,4,5) × 4 → hypotenuse = 20. This saves a full minute.

### 2.5 The Four Centres of a Triangle

This is where most students panic. Don't. Here's the clean version:

| Centre | Made by | Special property |
|---|---|---|
| **Centroid (G)** | Medians | Divides each median in **2:1** (vertex side is longer) |
| **Incentre (I)** | Angle bisectors | Centre of incircle; inradius r = Area/s |
| **Circumcentre (O)** | Perpendicular bisectors of sides | Centre of circumcircle; equidistant from all vertices |
| **Orthocentre (H)** | Altitudes | In a right triangle, it sits **at the right-angle vertex** |

🎵 **Mnemonic: "G-I-O-H = Good Idiots Order Hot-chai"** — Centroid, Incentre, Circumcentre, Orthocentre. Silly? Yes. Unforgettable? Also yes.

**Key exam facts:**
- In an **equilateral triangle**, all four centres coincide at one point.
- In a **right-angled triangle**, the circumcentre is the **midpoint of the hypotenuse** (so circumradius R = hypotenuse/2).
- Centroid's 2:1 division is asked again and again.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a right triangle with hypotenuse 10 cm, what is the circumradius? (Answer: 5 cm — no calculation needed.)

## Part 3: Circles — Where Angles Bend

Circles look friendly but hide the most elegant theorems in the syllabus.

### 3.1 The Core Theorems

1. The **angle at the centre** is **double** the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc. (∠centre = 2 × ∠circumference)
2. **Angles in the same segment** are equal.
3. The **angle in a semicircle is 90°** (Thales' theorem).
4. Opposite angles of a **cyclic quadrilateral** are supplementary (add to 180°).

### 3.2 Chords and Tangents

- The perpendicular from the centre to a chord **bisects the chord**.
- Equal chords are **equidistant from the centre**.
- A tangent is **perpendicular to the radius** at the point of contact.
- **Tangents from an external point are equal** in length.
- **Alternate Segment Theorem:** the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

🧠 **Active Recall:** O is the centre, and arc AB subtends 80° at the centre. What angle does it subtend at any point on the remaining circumference? (Answer: 40°.)

### 3.3 Two Circles Touching

- Touching **externally**: distance between centres = r₁ + r₂.
- Touching **internally**: distance between centres = r₁ − r₂.
- Direct common tangents and transverse common tangents are classic formula-based questions — keep the formulas handy (see the Formulas section).

## Part 4: Quadrilaterals and Polygons

### 4.1 The Quadrilateral Family

- **Parallelogram:** opposite sides equal and parallel; diagonals **bisect** each other.
- **Rectangle:** parallelogram + all angles 90°; diagonals **equal**.
- **Rhombus:** all sides equal; diagonals bisect at **90°**.
- **Square:** rectangle + rhombus combined — diagonals equal AND perpendicular.
- **Trapezium:** exactly one pair of parallel sides.

🎵 **Mnemonic for the family tree:** "**P**apa **R**am **R**eads **S**tories **T**onight" — Parallelogram → Rectangle → Rhombus → Square → Trapezium. Remember: every square is a rectangle and a rhombus, but not vice versa. Exams love "which statement is NOT true" questions on exactly this.

### 4.2 Polygons

For a regular polygon with **n sides**:

- Sum of interior angles = **(n − 2) × 180°**
- Each interior angle = **(n − 2) × 180° / n**
- Each exterior angle = **360° / n**
- Number of diagonals = **n(n − 3)/2**

🧠 **Active Recall:** A regular polygon has each exterior angle = 40°. How many sides? (Answer: 360/40 = 9 sides — a nonagon.)

## Part 5: Coordinate Geometry — Geometry with Numbers

Coordinate geometry converts shapes into algebra. Three formulas run the whole show:

1. **Distance:** √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
2. **Section formula (internal division in ratio m:n):** ((mx₂ + nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m+n))
3. **Slope:** m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)

Handy consequences:

- **Midpoint** = average of the coordinates (section formula with m = n = 1).
- Two lines are **parallel** if slopes are equal; **perpendicular** if the product of slopes = −1.
- **Collinearity test:** area of the triangle formed by three points = 0.
- **Centroid** of a triangle with vertices (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃) = ((x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, (y₁+y₂+y₃)/3).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Find the midpoint of (2, 6) and (8, −2). (Answer: (5, 2).)

## Part 6: The Examiner's Favourite Traps

Before the summary, internalise these — they decide ranks:

1. **"Looks like" is not "is."** Never assume equal sides or right angles from the figure unless stated or marked.
2. **Area ratio of similar figures = square of side ratio.** Not the ratio itself.
3. **AAA proves similarity, not congruence.**
4. **Angle in a semicircle = 90°** — hidden right angles are everywhere in circle questions.
5. **Exterior angle = sum of opposite interior angles** — often faster than using the 180° sum twice.
6. In "which is NOT true" questions about quadrilaterals, check the family tree: square ⊂ rectangle, square ⊂ rhombus.
