# The Hardest Idea in Number System — Explained Like You're 12

## The Concept: Remainders and Cyclicity (Why 7¹⁰⁰ is not scary at all)

Ask any exam student what scares them most in Number System, and they'll say: *"Find the remainder when 7¹⁰⁰ is divided by 5."* It looks impossible. 7¹⁰⁰ is a number with 85 digits! Nobody can multiply that out.

But here's the secret: **you never need the actual number. You only need the leftover.**

## The Story: Ravi and the Roti Basket

Imagine Ravi works at a dhaba. Every day he bakes rotis and packs them into baskets of 5. The boss doesn't ask "how many rotis?" — he only asks, *"How many rotis are left over after filling the baskets?"*

That leftover is the **remainder**. That's all division ever asks in these questions.

Now here's the fun part. Ravi notices something magical about 7:

- 7 rotis → 1 full basket of 5, **2 left over**
- 7 × 7 = 49 rotis → 9 baskets, **4 left over**
- 7 × 7 × 7 = 343 → **3 left over**
- 7⁴ = 2401 → **1 left over**
- 7⁵ → back to **2 left over**!

The leftovers go **2, 4, 3, 1, 2, 4, 3, 1…** — like four friends taking turns in a game, forever. This repeating loop is called **cyclicity**. The cycle length here is 4.

## So what's 7¹⁰⁰ ÷ 5?

Just find where 100 lands in the cycle of 4:
100 ÷ 4 = 25 exactly, remainder 0 → so it lands on the **last** position of the cycle → leftover = **1**.

Done. No 85-digit monster. Just a merry-go-round of four numbers.

## The Never-Forget Trick 🎡

**"Powers are Ferris wheels — find the seat count, then count the rounds."**

1. Find the cycle of remainders (usually 1 to 4 seats long).
2. Divide the power by the cycle length.
3. Remainder tells you the seat; remainder 0 means the last seat.

Bonus shortcut: when dividing by 5, only the **unit digit** matters. Powers of 7 end in 7, 9, 3, 1, 7, 9, 3, 1… — same Ferris wheel, same answer!
