# Number System (Advanced)

## 1. The Family Tree of Numbers

Every number you'll ever meet in an exam belongs to a family:

- **Natural numbers (N):** 1, 2, 3, ... — the counting numbers.
- **Whole numbers (W):** 0, 1, 2, 3, ... — natural numbers plus zero.
- **Integers (Z):** ..., −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, ...
- **Rational numbers (Q):** numbers expressible as p/q, where q ≠ 0. Their decimals either *terminate* (0.25) or *repeat* (0.333...).
- **Irrational numbers:** cannot be written as p/q — √2, √3, π. Their decimals never end and never repeat.
- **Real numbers (R):** rational + irrational — the complete number line.

**Exam trap:** Is 0.999... (repeating) rational? Yes! It equals exactly 1. Any repeating decimal is rational.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. Is √9 irrational? (No — √9 = 3, a rational number. The root must be *non-terminating* to be irrational.) Is every integer a whole number? (No — negative integers are not whole numbers.)

## 2. Prime, Composite, and Co-prime — The VIP Club

- **Prime number:** has exactly two factors — 1 and itself. Examples: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13...
- **Composite number:** more than two factors. Examples: 4, 6, 8, 9...
- **1 is neither prime nor composite.** (Examiners love this trap!)
- **2 is the only even prime.** Every other even number is divisible by 2.

**Co-prime numbers** are pairs whose HCF is 1 — they share no common factor except 1. Example: (8, 15) are co-prime even though neither is prime. Co-prime ≠ prime!

**How many primes below 100?** Exactly **25**. Memorize this — it appears directly in exams.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking — is 91 prime? (No! 91 = 7 × 13. This is the most common "fake prime" in exams. Others: 51 = 3 × 17, 57 = 3 × 19, 87 = 3 × 29.)

## 3. Divisibility Rules — Including the Ones Nobody Teaches

You know 2, 3, 5, 10. Here is the complete arsenal:

| Divisor | Rule |
|---|---|
| 2 | Last digit even |
| 3 | Sum of digits divisible by 3 |
| 4 | Last two digits divisible by 4 |
| 5 | Last digit 0 or 5 |
| 6 | Divisible by both 2 and 3 |
| 7 | Double the last digit, subtract from the rest; result divisible by 7 |
| 8 | Last three digits divisible by 8 |
| 9 | Sum of digits divisible by 9 |
| 11 | (Sum of odd-place digits) − (sum of even-place digits) = 0 or multiple of 11 |
| 12 | Divisible by both 3 and 4 |
| 13 | 4 × last digit + rest, divisible by 13 |

**Example (7):** Is 203 divisible by 7? Take 20 − (2×3) = 20 − 6 = 14. Yes, 14 is divisible by 7. ✓

**Example (11):** Is 918082 divisible by 11? Odd places: 9+8+8 = 25. Even places: 1+0+2 = 3. Difference = 22, a multiple of 11. ✓

🧠 **Active Recall:** Test 1001 for divisibility by 7, 11, and 13. (Surprise: 1001 = 7 × 11 × 13 — divisible by all three! This fact alone solves many exam questions.)

## 4. Factors — Counting Them Like a Pro

This is where toppers separate from the crowd. For any number, write its **prime factorization**:

**N = a^p × b^q × c^r** (where a, b, c are primes)

Then:

- **Number of factors** = (p+1)(q+1)(r+1)
- **Sum of factors** = (a⁰+a¹+...+a^p)(b⁰+...+b^q)(c⁰+...+c^r)
- **Number of even factors** = p × (q+1)(r+1) — if a = 2
- **Number of odd factors** = (q+1)(r+1) — just ignore the power of 2
- **Product of factors** = N^(number of factors / 2)

**Example:** N = 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹
- Total factors = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4×3×2 = **24**
- Odd factors = (2+1)(1+1) = **6**
- Even factors = 24 − 6 = **18**

**Perfect squares** always have an **odd** number of factors (one factor pairs with itself). This is a favourite statement-based question.

🧠 **Active Recall:** How many factors does 72 have? (72 = 2³ × 3² → (3+1)(2+1) = 12.) Does 144 have an odd or even count of factors? (Odd — it's a perfect square, 12².)

## 5. Unit Digit and Cyclicity — The 15-Second Superpower

Remember the opening story? Here is the trick. The unit digits of powers repeat in cycles:

| Digit | Cycle of unit digits | Cycle length |
|---|---|---|
| 0, 1, 5, 6 | Always the same | 1 |
| 2 | 2, 4, 8, 6 | 4 |
| 3 | 3, 9, 7, 1 | 4 |
| 4 | 4, 6 | 2 |
| 7 | 7, 9, 3, 1 | 4 |
| 8 | 8, 4, 2, 6 | 4 |
| 9 | 9, 1 | 2 |

**Method:** Divide the exponent by the cycle length. Use the remainder to pick the position. (If remainder = 0, take the *last* position of the cycle.)

**Example:** Unit digit of 7^103? Cycle length 4. 103 ÷ 4 → remainder 3. Third position in (7, 9, 3, 1) = **3**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Unit digit of 2^100? (100 ÷ 4 → remainder 0 → last position of 2,4,8,6 → **6**.)

## 6. Remainders — The Heart of Advanced Number System

**Basic rule:** Remainders behave like the numbers themselves under +, −, ×.

If a ÷ n leaves remainder r₁ and b ÷ n leaves remainder r₂, then:
- (a + b) ÷ n leaves remainder of (r₁ + r₂) ÷ n
- (a × b) ÷ n leaves remainder of (r₁ × r₂) ÷ n

**Negative remainders:** If remainder is −1, treat it as (divisor − 1). This makes huge problems tiny.

**Example:** Remainder when 8^100 is divided by 7?
8 ≡ 1 (mod 7), so 8^100 ≡ 1^100 = **1**. Done in 5 seconds.

**Example:** Remainder when 2^100 ÷ 7? Powers of 2 mod 7: 2, 4, 1, 2, 4, 1... cycle of 3. 100 ÷ 3 → remainder 1 → answer **2**. (This was the opening story's question!)

**Fermat's Little Theorem:** If p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then a^(p−1) ÷ p leaves remainder **1**. Example: 2^6 ÷ 7 → remainder 1.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Remainder when 9^50 ÷ 8? (9 ≡ 1 mod 8 → answer 1.) Remainder when 5^3 ÷ 7? (125 ÷ 7 = 17×7 + 6 → **6**.)

## 7. HCF and LCM — Beyond the Basics

- **HCF × LCM = Product of the two numbers** (works only for *two* numbers — a classic trap!).
- HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators.
- LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators.
- If two numbers are in ratio a : b (co-prime) with HCF = h, the numbers are ah and bh, and LCM = abh.

**Word-problem decoder:**
- "Greatest number that divides..." → **HCF**
- "Smallest number divisible by..." → **LCM**
- "Bells toll together again" → **LCM**
- "Maximum size of equal pieces" → **HCF**

🧠 **Active Recall:** HCF × LCM for three numbers — does the product formula work? (No! Only for two numbers. Examiners test exactly this.)

## 8. Base Systems — The Silent Scorer

We live in base 10, but exams visit other bases:

- **Decimal → other base:** Divide repeatedly by the base, collect remainders bottom-to-top.
- **Other base → decimal:** Multiply each digit by base^position and add.

**Example:** (1011)₂ = 1×2³ + 0×2² + 1×2¹ + 1×2⁰ = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = **(11)₁₀**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Convert 25 to binary. (25 = 16+8+1 → **11001**.)

## 🎭 The Mnemonic You'll Never Forget

**"Lazy Elephants Jump Slowly"** — for the unit-digit cycle of 2: **2, 4, 8, 6** — imagine 2 Lazy elephants, then 4, then 8, then 6, jumping slowly in a loop forever. Weird? Yes. Unforgettable? Also yes.

And for the fake primes: **"91 is a LIAR"** — it looks prime, smells prime, but 91 = 7 × 13. Whenever a number *feels* prime in an exam, interrogate it!
