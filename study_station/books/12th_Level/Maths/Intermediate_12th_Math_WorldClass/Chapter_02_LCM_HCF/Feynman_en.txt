# LCM & HCF — Explained Like You're 12

## The Two Friends Who Could Never Meet

Imagine two friends, **Ravi** and **Meena**, who run around a circular park.

Ravi takes **4 minutes** to complete one round. Meena takes **6 minutes**. They start together at the gate, high-five, and run off at their own speeds.

Now here's the question that confused them for weeks: *"When will we both be at the gate at the SAME time again?"*

Ravi is at the gate at: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, **24**, 28…
Meena is at the gate at: 6, 12, 18, **24**, 30…

Look! They both appear at the gate at minute **12**… and then again at **24**. The FIRST time they meet is **12** — that's the **LCM (Least Common Multiple)**. It's the smallest number that sits in *both* their timetables.

**LCM is a "meeting time" story.** Whenever a problem says "bells ring together," "lights flash together," "runners meet again" — it's secretly asking for the LCM.

---

## Now Flip the Story: Sharing the Pizza Dough

A baker has **24** chocolate cookies and **36** vanilla cookies. He wants to pack them into identical gift boxes — every box the same, no cookies left over, and he wants the **biggest possible** box size.

What sizes work? He could put 1 per box, or 2, or 3, or 4, or 6, or **12**. But 12 is the *largest* number that divides both 24 and 36 perfectly.

That biggest shared size — **12** — is the **HCF (Highest Common Factor)**.

**HCF is a "cutting/sharing" story.** Whenever a problem says "largest piece," "greatest length of tape to measure both," "maximum number of identical groups" — it's secretly asking for the HCF.

---

## The One Trick You'll Never Forget 🎯

> **LCM grows UP, HCF cuts DOWN.**
>
> - **L**CM = **L**arger answer (it's a multiple — multiples only get bigger). Think: **L**ift goes **up**.
> - **H**CF = **H**alving/Cutting (it's a factor — factors chop numbers down). Think: **H**ammer **breaks** things into pieces.

And here's the magic glue connecting them:

> **Product of two numbers = LCM × HCF**

Two numbers, 4 and 6: LCM = 12, HCF = 2. Check: 4 × 6 = 24, and 12 × 2 = 24. ✔ It *always* works — like a seesaw that never unbalances.

---

## Why This Matters in Your Exam

The exam never asks "find the LCM of 4 and 6." It hides the concept inside a story — bells, runners, tiles, ropes. Your job is just to ask one question:

**"Is this a MEETING story (→ LCM) or a CUTTING story (→ HCF)?"**

Answer that, and half the battle is won before you even calculate.
