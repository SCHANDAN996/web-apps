## The Core Dilemma: When to Take HCF vs LCM in Applied Problems?

Students frequently encounter this exam paralysis: *"A word problem talks about bells ringing, milk containers being measured, or runners on a track. I know how to calculate LCM and HCF of raw numbers, but in an applied question, I cannot immediately tell whether I need to take the HCF or the LCM!"*

The golden diagnostic key is:
- **HCF (Highest Common Factor)** is always **<= the given numbers**. If you need to cut, divide, distribute, or find the largest container/tile to measure quantities without leaving a leftover, you are looking for a **Factor (HCF)**.
- **LCM (Least Common Multiple)** is always **>= the given numbers**. If events are repeating, steps are pacing, bells are tolling, or runners are looping until they synchronize in the future, you are looking for a **Multiple (LCM)**.

Once you see this division between cutting down (HCF) and multiplying forward (LCM), the confusion vanishes forever.

## 1. Fundamentals: HCF, LCM, and the Euclidean Algorithm

### Prime Factorization View
Let two numbers $A$ and $B$ be factored into prime powers:
$$A = p_1^{a_1} p_2^{a_2} p_3^{a_3} \dots, \quad B = p_1^{b_1} p_2^{b_2} p_3^{b_3} \dots$$
- **HCF:** Product of the **lowest exponents** of all common prime factors:
  $$\text{HCF}(A, B) = p_1^{\min(a_1, b_1)} \cdot p_2^{\min(a_2, b_2)} \dots$$
- **LCM:** Product of the **highest exponents** of all prime factors appearing in either number:
  $$\text{LCM}(A, B) = p_1^{\max(a_1, b_1)} \cdot p_2^{\max(a_2, b_2)} \dots$$

### The Euclidean Division Algorithm for Rapid HCF
For very large numbers (e.g. 1430 and 1859), prime factorization is slow. Use Euclid's subtraction/division property:
$$\gcd(A, B) = \gcd(B, A \pmod B)$$
Divide the larger number by the smaller, replace the larger by the remainder, and repeat until the remainder is 0. The final non-zero divisor is the HCF.
- $1859 = 1430 \times 1 + 429$
- $1430 = 429 \times 3 + 143$
- $429 = 143 \times 3 + 0$
The HCF is **143**.

## 2. The Ratio Form: $a = Hx, b = Hy$

Let $H = \text{HCF}(a, b)$. By definition, $H$ is the greatest common divisor. Factoring out $H$, we write:
$$a = Hx, \quad b = Hy$$
where $\gcd(x, y) = 1$ ($x$ and $y$ are mutually co-prime).

From this foundation:
1. **LCM ($L$):**
   $$L = H \cdot x \cdot y$$
2. **Product Law:**
   $$a \times b = (Hx)(Hy) = H(Hxy) = H \times L$$
3. **Ratio of LCM to HCF:**
   $$\frac{L}{H} = xy$$
4. **Sum and Difference:**
   $$a + b = H(x + y), \quad a - b = H(x - y)$$

## 3. LCM and HCF of Fractions

When dealing with fractions in simplest terms:
$$\text{HCF}\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{\text{HCF}(a, c)}{\text{LCM}(b, d)}$$
$$\text{LCM}\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{\text{LCM}(a, c)}{\text{HCF}(b, d)}$$

## 4. The Remainder Models

### LCM Models
- **Model 1 (Constant Remainder $r$):**
  $$N = \text{LCM}(d_1, d_2, d_3) \times k + r$$
- **Model 2 (Constant Difference $K = d_i - r_i$):**
  $$N = \text{LCM}(d_1, d_2, d_3) \times k - K$$

### HCF Models
- **Model 1 (Known Remainder $r$):**
  $$\text{Divisor} = \text{HCF}(A - r, B - r, C - r)$$
- **Model 2 (Same Unknown Remainder):**
  $$\text{Divisor} = \text{HCF}(|A - B|, |B - C|, |C - A|)$$

## 5. Industrial and Practical Applications

### Synchronization Models (Bells, Lights, Steps)
Whenever different entities complete cycles at different intervals $t_1, t_2, \dots$:
- They synchronize at interval $T_{\text{cycle}} = \text{LCM}(t_1, t_2, \dots)$.
- In a duration $T$, total simultaneous occurrences $= \lfloor T / T_{\text{cycle}} \rfloor + 1$ (including the simultaneous start at $t = 0$).

### Floor Paving with Minimum Identical Square Tiles
For a floor of length $L$ and breadth $B$:
- To minimize the number of tiles, tile side must be maximum: $S = \text{HCF}(L, B)$.
- Minimum number of tiles $= (L \times B) / S^2 = (L / S) \times (B / S)$.
