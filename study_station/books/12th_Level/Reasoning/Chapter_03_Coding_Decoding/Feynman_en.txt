# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Coding-Decoding Explained Like You're 12

## The Secret Language of Two Friends

Imagine this. Riya and Aman sit in the same class, and the teacher has a strict rule: **no passing notes in class.** But Riya *really* wants to tell Aman: "Meet me at the canteen."

So they invent a secret rule between them:

> **Every letter becomes the NEXT letter of the alphabet.**
> A → B, B → C, C → D … and Z wraps around to A.

Riya writes on her chit: **NFFU**

Aman smiles. He just shifts every letter one step *backwards*:
N→M, F→E, F→E, U→T = **MEET** ✅

The teacher picks up the chit, reads "NFFU", shrugs, and throws it away. Mission accomplished.

**Congratulations — you just did Coding-Decoding.** That's the *entire* chapter. Everything else is just fancier versions of Riya and Aman's trick.

---

## The Two Halves of Every Question

Every coding-decoding question has exactly two jobs:

1. **CODING** = locking the message (English word → secret code). Riya's job.
2. **DECODING** = unlocking the message (secret code → English word). Aman's job.

The exam gives you a *sample pair* — like "CAT is written as DBU" — and asks you to find the **rule** (the secret agreement), then apply it to a new word. Your only real task is: **catch the rule.**

---

## The Hardest Part: "How Do I Find the Rule Fast?"

Here's where students panic. The word is coded, but *how*? Letter shift? Reverse? Jumbled? Here's the trick that makes it child's play:

### 🔑 The Unforgettable Trick: **"Write the Numbers, Watch the Dance"**

Letters are shy. Numbers are honest. The moment you convert letters to their **place values** (A=1, B=2, … Z=26), the hidden rule starts dancing in front of you.

**Example:** In a code, DOG is written as FQI. How is CAT written?

Step 1 — Write place values:

| D | O | G |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 15 | 7 |

| F | Q | I |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 17 | 9 |

Step 2 — Compare each pair:
- 4 → 6 (**+2**)
- 15 → 17 (**+2**)
- 7 → 9 (**+2**)

The dance is obvious: **every letter jumps +2.** 🕺

Step 3 — Apply to CAT: C(3)→E, A(1)→C, T(20)→V = **ECV** ✅

That's it. Numbers never lie. If the difference isn't constant, check these usual suspects, in this order:

1. **Constant shift** (+2, −3, etc.) — most common
2. **Increasing shift** (+1, +2, +3…)
3. **Reverse order** (word written backwards: DOG → GOD)
4. **Opposite letters** (A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X — pairs adding to 27)
5. **Vowels vs consonants treated differently** (vowels +1, consonants −1, say)
6. **Letter positions swapped** (1st↔3rd letter, etc.)

### 🧠 Memory Hook: **"EJOTY"** — your alphabet GPS

To recall place values instantly, remember the word **EJOTY**:
**E**=5, **J**=10, **O**=15, **T**=20, **Y**=25 — the multiples of 5.

Need K? J is 10, so K is 11. Need S? T is 20, so S is 19. No more counting on fingers in the exam hall!

And for opposite letters, remember: **"Sum to 27"** — A(1)+Z(26)=27, M(13)+N(14)=27. If a letter and its code add to 27, it's an opposite-letter code.

## One More Story: The Number Code

Sometimes the code isn't letters at all. "If PEN = 35, what is INK?"

Don't panic — numbers again! P(16)+E(5)+N(14) = **35**. The rule was just *add the place values*. So INK = 9+14+11 = **34**. The exam loves this because students assume it must be complicated. It rarely is.
