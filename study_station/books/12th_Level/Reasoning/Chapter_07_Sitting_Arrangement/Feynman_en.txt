# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Sitting Arrangement (Intermediate)

## The Hardest Part, Made Simple

The toughest thing in this chapter is not the puzzle itself — it is **holding 8 people's positions in your head at once while the clues keep twisting** ("A sits second to the left of B, who faces away from the centre..."). Your brain drops pieces. So let's learn the one skill that fixes everything: **turn words into a picture, immediately.**

## A Story: The Birthday Dinner

Imagine 12-year-old Riya is planning her birthday dinner. Six friends, one round table. Her mom says: "Seat them so nobody fights!"

The rules come out like exam clues:

- "Meera won't sit next to Karan."
- "Ali must sit directly opposite Riya."
- "Karan wants to be on Riya's immediate right."

Riya tries to remember all this in her head — and fails in 10 seconds. Then she grabs a paper plate, draws a circle, marks 6 chairs, and writes initials. Suddenly it's easy: **Ali goes opposite her, Karan on her right, Meera anywhere far from Karan.** Done.

That paper plate is your exam answer sheet. **Every sitting arrangement question is just Riya's dinner with more guests and grumpier rules.**

## The One Unforgettable Trick: **"Fix One, Chain the Rest"**

Never start with the clue that says "P is somewhere to the left of Q" — that's floating. Start with the clue that **nails one person to one exact chair**:

1. **Fix** — Find the strongest clue ("A sits at the extreme left end" / "C faces B directly"). Place that person first. In circular puzzles with no fixed point, place *anyone* at the top — the circle has no "first chair," so you may choose it.
2. **Chain** — Use clues that connect to the person you just placed ("second to the right of A"). Each placement unlocks the next.
3. **Branch** — If a clue gives two possibilities, draw both mini-diagrams. One will die when a later clue contradicts it. Kill it, keep the survivor.
4. **Facing matters** — In a circle, "left" and "right" flip depending on whether people face the centre or away. Write a small arrow on each head in your diagram. Facing centre → their right is *your* clockwise. Facing away → reversed (their right is *your* anticlockwise).

**Memory hook: "Anchor → Chain → Branch → Arrows."** Say it like a mantra before every puzzle.

## The Examiner's Favourite Trap (So You Never Fall)

In circular puzzles, the examiner writes: *"D sits second to the left of E"* — but E faces **outward**. Many candidates place D on the wrong side and lose all the linked questions. The trick: **before placing anyone, mark every person's facing direction on your diagram.** Ten seconds of arrows saves five marks.

Whatever you couldn't recall is exactly what you must revise tonight.

## One Question for You

If 8 people sit in a circle, all facing the centre, and you are told "X sits third to the right of Y" — why does it not matter *which* chair you place Y in first? Explain it to a friend (or to your wall) in your own words.
