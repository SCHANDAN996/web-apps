Card 1
Front: What is the very first thing you should fix before solving any seating arrangement puzzle?
Back: A reference point — usually one person's position or the direction everyone faces. Without fixing "who faces where," every other clue becomes ambiguous.

Card 2
Front: In a circular arrangement where everyone faces the centre, is a person's "right" your right or your left on paper?
Back: Your left on paper. When people face the centre, their right hand points anti-clockwise (your left in the diagram). This reversal is the single biggest source of errors.

Card 3
Front: In a circular arrangement where everyone faces OUTWARD (away from centre), which way does a person's "right" go on paper?
Back: Clockwise — your right on paper. Facing outward flips the direction compared to facing the centre.

Card 4
Front: What does "A sits second to the right of B" mean exactly?
Back: There is exactly ONE person between A and B, and A is on B's right-hand side. "Second to the right" = skip one seat in the right direction.

Card 5
Front: What is the difference between "A is to the immediate right of B" and "A is to the right of B"?
Back: "Immediate right" means adjacent — no one between them. Just "right of" means anywhere on the right side, not necessarily next to B.

Card 6
Front: Which type of clue should you place FIRST: a definite clue or a conditional/possible clue?
Back: Always start with definite clues ("C sits at an extreme end", "D is third to the left of E"). Conditional clues ("P may sit…") are fitted in later.

Card 7
Front: What is the mnemonic trick for remembering direction reversal in circles?
Back: "IN = Anti, OUT = Clock" — facing IN (centre), right goes anti-clockwise; facing OUT, right goes clockwise. Say it before every circular puzzle.

Card 8
Front: In a linear row where some people face North and some face South, how do you handle "right/left"?
Back: For North-facing people, right = your right (east on paper). For South-facing people, right = your left (west on paper). Mark each person's facing direction with an arrow first.

Card 9
Front: What is a "block" or "pair" technique in seating puzzles?
Back: When a clue ties two people together ("M and N sit together"), treat them as one movable unit (block) with two internal orders (MN or NM). Place the block first, then resolve the internal order with later clues.

Card 10
Front: In a two-row arrangement (Row 1 facing South, Row 2 facing North, facing each other), what does "X faces Y" tell you?
Back: X and Y are in opposite rows in the SAME column (directly opposite each other). Also remember: their left-right senses are mirror images of each other.

Card 11
Front: Examiner's trap: "P is third to the left of Q, who is second to the right of R." What mistake do most students make here?
Back: They read the whole chain from P. Actually the chain anchors at R: first place R, then Q (second right of R), then P (third left of Q). Always start from the LAST named anchor person in the chain.

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: In an 8-person circle, "A sits opposite B" — how many seats are between them?
Back: Three seats on each side (opposite = half the circle away, i.e., 4 positions apart). Students often wrongly assume 2 seats between them.

Card 13
Front: What mnemonic helps you remember the order of solving steps?
Back: "F-D-B-C": Fix facing direction → Definite clues → Blocks/pairs → Conditional clues. Never jump to conditional clues first.

Card 14
Front: If a puzzle says "not more than two persons sit between X and Y," what are the possible gaps?
Back: Zero, one, or two persons between them — i.e., X and Y can be adjacent, or 2 or 3 seats apart. "Not more than" includes the boundary value; students often wrongly exclude it.

Card 15
Front: In a rectangular table with 4 people at corners and 4 at the middle of sides, who sits "opposite" a corner person?
Back: The person at the diagonally opposite corner. A side-middle person is opposite the side-middle person on the parallel side. Corner-to-side is never "opposite."

Card 16
Front: When a clue says "A is not an immediate neighbour of B," what should you do with it?
Back: Park it as a negative clue. Negative clues don't place anyone directly — use them at the end to eliminate wrong arrangements among the remaining possibilities.

Card 17
Front: How many distinct cases should you draw when a clue gives two possibilities?
Back: Draw both cases as parallel diagrams (Case 1 and Case 2), and keep eliminating. The moment one case contradicts any clue, drop it and continue with the survivor.

Card 18
Front: In a row of people facing North, "B is at one of the extreme ends" — how many positions does this fix?
Back: Two possibilities: leftmost or rightmost end. Keep both open until another clue (e.g., "B is to the right of A") eliminates one.

Card 19
Front: Why should you never assume the gender or facing direction of a person not mentioned in any clue?
Back: Because examiners deliberately leave some attributes undetermined — questions then ask "which statement CANNOT be determined." Assuming unstated facts is a classic trap that leads to wrong answers.

Card 20
Front: What is the quickest self-check after completing an arrangement?
Back: Re-read every clue once and verify it against your final diagram — especially direction-sensitive clues (left/right) and "between" counts. One violated clue means the whole arrangement is wrong.
