# Sitting Arrangement (Intermediate)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Solve **circular arrangements** where people face inward AND outward (mixed facing).
2. Handle **rectangular/square tables** with people at corners and sides.
3. Crack **two-row parallel arrangements** (people facing each other).
4. Manage **extra variables** — like each person having a different city, colour, or profession.
5. Use a fixed, repeatable method so you never panic in the exam hall.

---

## The Hook: One Diagram Is Worth Ten Lines of Reading

Imagine this. The examiner gives you 8 lines of clues about 8 friends sitting around a table. Most students read the clues again and again — 4 minutes gone, nothing solved. The topper? She draws one circle, marks 8 seats, and starts *placing* people. In 90 seconds, the whole arrangement is on paper, and all 5 questions are free marks.

That is the entire secret of Sitting Arrangement: **stop reading, start drawing.** This chapter teaches you exactly how.

---

## We Know the Struggle

Almost every aspirant says the same three things:

- "The clues contradict each other in my head."
- "I make the diagram, but one clue breaks everything."
- "Facing outward vs inward confuses me completely."

Relax. These problems come from **one root cause**: trying to hold the arrangement in your memory instead of on paper. Once everything lives on the diagram, the confusion disappears. Let's build the skill chunk by chunk.

---

## Chunk 1: The Golden Method (Works for Every Question)

Follow these 5 steps, in this order, every single time:

**Step 1 — Draw the frame first.** Circle, square, or two parallel lines. Mark the seats. Mark the facing direction with small arrows.

**Step 2 — Hunt for the "anchor clue."** An anchor clue fixes someone's exact position: *"A sits at the head of the table"* or *"P is second to the right of Q"* (once Q is placed). Start with the most definite clue.

**Step 3 — Place relatives, not absolutes.** Clues like *"B is third to the left of A"* only work after A is placed. Chain them one by one.

**Step 4 — Use "negative clues" to eliminate.** *"C is NOT next to D"* doesn't place anyone, but it kills wrong possibilities. Write it beside the diagram.

**Step 5 — If two cases are possible, draw both.** Solve until one case breaks a clue. Delete it. Never guess between cases in your head.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking above — what are the 5 steps of the Golden Method? Say them aloud. Which step do most students skip? (Step 5 — and that's where marks are lost.)

## Chunk 2: Circular Arrangement — Mixed Facing (The Intermediate Upgrade)

In basic circles, everyone faces the centre. At the intermediate level, **some face inward, some face outward**. This changes everything about left and right.

**The one rule to memorise:**

- Facing the **centre (inward)** → their **right is clockwise**, their **left is anti-clockwise**.
- Facing **outside (outward)** → their **right is anti-clockwise**, their **left is clockwise**.

**Mnemonic — "IN-CR, OUT-AR":**
> **IN**ward → **C**lockwise **R**ight. **OUT**ward → **A**nti-clockwise **R**ight.

Say it three times: *IN-CR, OUT-AR.* You'll never mix up directions again.

**Worked mini-example:** 6 people around a circle, A faces outward. Who is on A's immediate right? Since A faces outward, A's right = anti-clockwise direction. So the person sitting anti-clockwise next to A is on A's right.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If B faces the centre, is B's left clockwise or anti-clockwise? (Anti-clockwise — because inward means right is clockwise, so left is anti-clockwise.)

## Chunk 3: Rectangular & Square Tables

Typical setup: 8 people — 4 at corners, 4 at the middle of sides. Facing can be inward or outward.

Key facts to remember:

- A person at a **corner** has exactly **2 neighbours**.
- A person at the **middle of a side** has exactly **2 neighbours** too, but "opposite" means the middle of the facing side.
- "Sits opposite" is a strong anchor clue — use it early.
- Corner people facing inward: their left/right still follows IN-CR, OUT-AR along the table's edge.

**Pro tip:** Number the seats 1–8 clockwise on your diagram. Then clues like "third to the right" become simple counting.

---

## Chunk 4: Two Parallel Rows (Facing Each Other)

Setup: Row 1 faces **south**, Row 2 faces **north** (or vice versa), and each person faces exactly one person in the other row.

The trap here is left-right reversal:

- A person facing **south**: their right hand points **west** (your left on paper).
- A person facing **north**: their right hand points **east** (your right on paper).

**Simple hack:** For the row facing south, *flip* your sense of left and right. Many toppers write a small "R→" arrow above each row showing which way "right" goes for that row. Do this — it takes 3 seconds and saves 3 marks.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a row facing south, if X is "second to the right" of Y, does X appear to the left or right of Y on your paper? (To the left — because their right is your left.)

---

## Chunk 5: Extra Variables (Person + City + Colour)

Intermediate questions add a second layer: *"The one who likes red sits second to the left of the doctor."*

Method:

1. Solve the **seating skeleton first** — get every person into a seat.
2. Then attach the extra variable (city/colour/profession) clue by clue, writing it under each name.
3. If a variable clue also fixes a seat (e.g., "the Delhi person sits at a corner"), use it as an anchor earlier.

Never try to solve persons and variables simultaneously — one layer at a time.

---

## Chunk 6: The "Two Cases" Discipline

When a clue gives you a choice ("P sits either second or third to the left of Q"), do this:

- Draw **Case 1** and **Case 2** side by side, small diagrams.
- Keep solving both. Usually within 2–3 clues, one case hits a contradiction.
- Cross out the dead case loudly on paper. Commit fully to the survivor.

Students who keep two cases "in their head" mix them up and answer everything wrong. Paper is cheap; marks are not.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Why should you never merge two possible cases into one diagram? (Because clues from different cases contaminate each other and produce a fake arrangement that "almost" works.)

## Summary Table

| Arrangement Type | Key Rule | Biggest Danger |
|---|---|---|
| Circle, all inward | Right = clockwise | Miscounting positions |
| Circle, mixed facing | IN-CR, OUT-AR | Forgetting to flip for outward facers |
| Square/Rectangle | Corner = 2 neighbours; use "opposite" as anchor | Confusing corner vs side-middle |
| Two parallel rows | South-facing row: flip left-right | Left-right reversal |
| Extra variables | Skeleton first, variables second | Solving both layers at once |
| Two possible cases | Draw both, kill one | Merging cases mentally |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — The "almost right" arrangement.** The examiner designs options so that if you misread even ONE clue (e.g., "second to the right" read as "second to the left"), the whole arrangement still looks neat — and matches a wrong option. **Defence:** After solving, re-check your diagram against every single clue before touching the questions. 20 seconds of checking saves 5 questions.

**Trap 2 — Confusing language and double relationships.** Phrases like *"R sits between the one who faces P and the one who is opposite Q"* are deliberately heavy. **Defence:** Translate each heavy sentence into a small symbol note on your diagram immediately (e.g., write "R: between X & Y"). Never carry a complicated sentence in your head.

**Trap 3 — Mixed facing hidden in plain sight.** The clue *"some face the centre, some face outside"* appears once, early in the paragraph, and students forget it by clue number 4. **Defence:** The moment you read facing directions, draw arrows on every seat FIRST, before placing a single person.
