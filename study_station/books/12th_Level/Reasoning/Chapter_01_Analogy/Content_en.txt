# Analogy (सादृश्यता)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Understand what an analogy question really tests.
2. Identify the **relationship** between a given pair of words.
3. Classify analogies into standard types (synonym, antonym, part-whole, tool-work, cause-effect, etc.).
4. Apply a fixed 3-step method to solve any analogy in seconds.
5. Spot and avoid the examiner's favourite traps.

---

## 1. The Hook: You Already Solve Analogies Every Day

"Doctor is to patient as teacher is to ______?"

You answered *student* in less than a second — without any coaching. That is the whole game of Analogy. The exam does not test your vocabulary alone; it tests whether you can **find the hidden bridge** between two words and build the *same bridge* between two other words.

Think of it like this: an analogy is a **relationship-matching machine**. Two words go in, one relationship comes out. Your job is to find another pair with the identical relationship.

---

## 2. The Common Struggle (And Why It Happens)

Most students lose marks in Analogy not because the question is hard, but because:

- They match the **words** instead of the **relationship**.
- They stop at the first option that "feels right" — and examiners design exactly such tempting options.
- They ignore the **direction** of the relationship (e.g., "egg → bird" is not the same as "bird → egg").

Remember: **relationship first, words later.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** In "Doctor : Patient :: Teacher : ?", what exactly is being matched — the words or the relationship? Say it aloud before reading further.

---

## 3. The 3-Step Method (Your Formula)

For every analogy question, follow these three steps — never skip Step 1:

**Step 1 — Make a sentence.** Connect the given pair in a short sentence.
Example: *Knife : Cut* → "A knife is used to cut."

**Step 2 — Apply the same sentence to each option.**
- (a) Pen : Write → "A pen is used to write." ✔
- (b) Paper : Book → "A paper is used to book." ✘

**Step 3 — Check direction and exactness.** Tool → work, not work → tool. Whole → part, not part → whole. The closest, most precise match wins.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Try it: *Bird : Nest :: Lion : ?* Make the sentence first — "A bird lives in a nest." Now which option fits the same sentence?

---

## 4. Standard Types of Analogies (The Examiner's Menu)

Almost every exam question falls into one of these types:

| # | Type | Example | Relationship |
|---|------|---------|--------------|
| 1 | Synonym | Happy : Joyful | Same meaning |
| 2 | Antonym | Hot : Cold | Opposite meaning |
| 3 | Part–Whole | Wheel : Car | Part to whole |
| 4 | Tool–Work | Pen : Write | Tool to its function |
| 5 | Worker–Workplace | Teacher : School | Person to place of work |
| 6 | Cause–Effect | Disease : Pain | Cause to result |
| 7 | Animal–Young | Dog : Puppy | Adult to young one |
| 8 | Animal–Dwelling | Bee : Hive | Creature to home |
| 9 | Country–Capital/Currency | India : New Delhi | Place to its capital |
| 10 | Study–Subject | Cardiology : Heart | Science to what it studies |
| 11 | Degree/Intensity | Warm : Hot | Lesser to greater intensity |
| 12 | Gender | King : Queen | Masculine to feminine |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Cover the table. Name any five types with one example each. Then check.

---

## 5. A Mnemonic to Remember the Types

**"SAPTACAD"** — a quick memory hook for the most frequent types:

- **S** – Synonym / Antonym
- **A** – Animal (young one, dwelling, sound)
- **P** – Part–Whole
- **T** – Tool–Work
- **A** – Action–Cause–Effect
- **C** – Country–Capital–Currency
- **A** – Academic study (Cardiology : Heart)
- **D** – Degree of intensity (Warm : Hot)

If you can run through SAPTACAD mentally, you can classify most exam analogies in under 10 seconds.

## 6. Worked Examples

**Example 1:** *Book : Pages :: Wall : ?*
- Sentence: "A book is made of pages."
- Options: (a) Bricks (b) Cement (c) Roof (d) Paint
- "A wall is made of bricks." ✔ → **Answer: (a) Bricks**
- Trap check: Cement joins bricks but a wall is not "made of cement" alone — bricks is the exact part-whole match.

**Example 2:** *Moon : Satellite :: Earth : ?*
- Sentence: "The Moon is a satellite."
- "The Earth is a planet." → **Answer: Planet**
- Trap: "Sun" is tempting (both are in space) but the relationship is *category membership*, not location.

**Example 3:** *Thermometer : Temperature :: Barometer : ?*
- Sentence: "A thermometer measures temperature."
- "A barometer measures pressure." → **Answer: Pressure**

🧠 **Active Recall:** In Example 2, why is "Sun" wrong even though the Sun is related to space? Frame your answer in one line.

---

## 7. Direction Matters: The Silent Killer

*Nest : Bird* is **not** the same as *Bird : Nest*.

- Bird : Nest → creature to its home.
- Nest : Bird → home to its creature.

If the question gives *Nest : Bird*, the answer must also be *home : creature* (e.g., *Hive : Bee*), not *Bee : Hive*. Always read the pair **left to right** and keep the same order in your answer.

## 8. Summary Table

| Key Idea | One-line Rule |
|----------|---------------|
| Core skill | Match the **relationship**, not the words |
| Method | Sentence → Apply → Check direction |
| Direction | Left-to-right order must be preserved |
| Types | Use the **SAPTACAD** mnemonic |
| Best option | The most **precise** match, not the first plausible one |
| Time rule | 20–30 seconds per question; skip if no clear sentence forms |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Multiple plausible relationships.**
Options are designed so that two of them seem related to the given word. Example: *Doctor : Patient* — options may include both *Teacher : Student* and *Lawyer : Client*. Both look right! The tie-breaker: a doctor **treats/cures** a patient; a lawyer **represents** a client, while a teacher **teaches** a student. Pick the option whose sentence matches *most exactly*. When in doubt, make your sentence more specific.

**Trap 2 — Difficult words to confuse you.**
Examiners insert uncommon words (*Ornithology, Entomology, Speleology*) so you panic. Don't. The relationship is usually simple — "study of birds", "study of insects". If you know even one common pair of the same type (*Cardiology : Heart*), use it as a template and guess the meaning logically.

**Trap 3 — Double-relationship questions.**
Some pairs carry **two** relationships, and only one option matches both. Example: *Apple : Fruit* — options (a) *Mango : Fruit* and (b) *Carrot : Vegetable*. Both fit the first relationship ("X is a Y" — category membership). Now look for the **second hidden relationship**: in the question pair, the first word is a *specific item* and the second is its *category*, and the item belongs to the plant/food group in the same way. Here both options still tie on category — so sharpen further: an apple **grows on a tree and is eaten as a fruit**, and so does a mango; a carrot is a **root** vegetable. The item-to-category match that preserves the *same sub-type* (tree-grown fruit) is *Mango : Fruit*. The lesson: if two options fit the first relationship, hunt for a **second layer** — sub-category, direction, or function — to decide. Never answer after checking only one layer.
