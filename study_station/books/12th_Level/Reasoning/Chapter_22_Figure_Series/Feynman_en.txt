# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Figure Series & Classification Explained Like You're 12

## The Story: Ravi and the Magic Photo Album

Imagine your friend Ravi has a photo album. On page 1, there's a photo of a flower with **1 petal**. Page 2: **2 petals**. Page 3: **3 petals**. Page 4: **4 petals**.

Now Ravi covers page 5 and asks you: *"What's on this page?"*

You laugh. "Obviously a flower with **5 petals**!"

Congratulations — you just solved a **Figure Series** question. That's ALL it is. A series of pictures that follow a hidden rule, and your job is to find the rule and predict the next picture.

Now imagine Ravi shows you 5 photos: a dog, a cat, a cow, a goat, and... a **car**. "Which one doesn't belong?" Easy — the car! That's **Figure Classification** — finding the odd one out using a rule.

The whole chapter is just these two games: **"What comes next?"** and **"Which one is different?"**

## The Hardest Part: When Pictures Change in 3 Ways at Once

Here's where students panic. In real exam questions, the figures don't just add petals. A single problem might have:

- a dot that **moves** corner to corner,
- an arrow that **rotates** 45° each step,
- a shape that **gains a side** (triangle → square → pentagon).

Three changes happening together! Your brain tries to track everything at once and gives up.

**Feynman trick: Be a detective with 3 notebooks.**

Don't look at the whole picture. Ask three separate questions, one at a time:

1. **COUNT** — Is anything increasing or decreasing? (lines, dots, sides, shapes)
2. **MOVE** — Is anything shifting position? (clockwise? anticlockwise? how many steps?)
3. **TURN** — Is anything rotating or flipping? (45°? 90°? mirror image?)

Check COUNT first across all figures. Then MOVE. Then TURN. Almost every exam figure obeys a rule from these three families. When you isolate one change at a time, the "impossible" problem becomes three easy ones.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "The Thief Only Moves One Pocket"

Think of the figure as a thief carrying loot in different pockets. Between two frames, usually **only one pocket changes** — the dot moves, OR the arrow turns, OR a line disappears. Rarely everything at once.

So when you're stuck comparing figure 1 and figure 2, ask: *"What is the ONE thing that changed?"* Find it, track it across all frames, and the answer reveals itself.

**For Classification (odd one out):** use the **"4 vs 1" rule**. Don't ask "what's special about this figure?" Ask "what do FOUR of them share that the fifth doesn't?" Four shapes might be closed figures while one is open. Four might have an even number of sides while one has odd. The odd one always breaks a rule the other four follow.

## The Examiner's Trap (Read This Twice!)

- **The "almost right" option:** The correct answer has the dot in the top-right AND the arrow rotated. The trap option has the dot right but the arrow wrong. **Check ALL elements before marking** — never mark after matching just one feature.
- **The mirror trap:** A rotated figure and its mirror image look similar but are different. Rotation keeps the "handedness"; a mirror flips it.
- **In classification:** the odd one out must be odd by a rule that the **other four all share** — not by some random unique feature (every figure is unique in *some* way).
