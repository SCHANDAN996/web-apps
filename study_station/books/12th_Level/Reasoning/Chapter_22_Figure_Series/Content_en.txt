# Figure Series & Classification

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Identify the **pattern** in a series of figures (rotation, addition, deletion, movement).
2. Find the **odd figure** in a classification question quickly and confidently.
3. Break any complex figure into simple elements — dots, lines, arrows, shapes.
4. Avoid the traps examiners plant in look-alike figures.
5. Solve each question in under 30 seconds with a fixed checking routine.

---

## The Hook: Your Brain Already Does This

Look at this sequence: 2, 4, 6, 8, ? — you said 10 instantly, right?

Figure series is **exactly the same game**, just played with pictures instead of numbers. A shape rotates, a dot moves, a line disappears — and your job is to spot the rule. The good news: your brain is a pattern-detecting machine. The bad news: examiners know this, so they design figures that *fool* a careless brain. This chapter teaches you to slow down for 10 seconds, check systematically, and never get fooled.

---

## The Common Struggle (And Why It's Normal)

Most students say: *"All five figures look the same to me!"* or *"I can see something is changing, but I can't say what."*

This happens because we look at the **whole figure at once**. The trick is to stop seeing "a figure" and start seeing **parts**: a triangle here, a dot there, an arrow on top. Once you track each part separately, the hidden pattern jumps out. Every topper in non-verbal reasoning does exactly this — nothing more.

---

## Part 1: What Is a Figure Series?

In a **figure series**, 4–5 figures are given in a row (the *problem figures*), and you must pick the next figure from the *answer figures*.

The change from one figure to the next always follows a **rule**. Your only job: find the rule, apply it once more, and match the answer.

### The Five Classic Rules

**Rule 1 — Rotation.** An element rotates by a fixed angle: 45°, 90°, 135°, or 180°, clockwise or anti-clockwise.
*Example: an arrow pointing up → right → down → left → next is up (90° clockwise each step).*

**Rule 2 — Addition or Deletion.** One element is added or removed in each step.
*Example: a square gains one side-line each step, or petals of a flower reduce 5 → 4 → 3 → 2.*

**Rule 3 — Movement.** An element shifts position — corner to corner, or one step along the sides.
*Example: a dot moves corner → corner clockwise while a cross moves anti-clockwise.*

**Rule 4 — Mirror / Water Image.** The figure flips horizontally (mirror) or vertically (water image) in alternate steps.

**Rule 5 — Combination.** Two rules run together — e.g., the outer shape rotates 90° while the inner dot moves one corner. This is the most common type in SSC and Banking exams.

## Part 2: The 4-Step Checking Routine

Never stare at a figure hoping the answer appears. Follow this fixed routine:

**Step 1 — Count.** Count elements: lines, dots, shapes, sides. Is the number changing? (Addition/deletion rule.)

**Step 2 — Track one element.** Pick the most "active" element (usually a dot or arrow) and follow it across all figures. Where does it go? (Movement rule.)

**Step 3 — Check rotation.** Does any shape turn? By how much — 45°, 90°, 180°? Clockwise or anti-clockwise?

**Step 4 — Combine and predict.** Apply the rule(s) to the last figure and match with the options. If two options look close, re-check the *inner* element — that's where the difference hides.

### Mnemonic: **"C-T-R-P"** — *"Can't Track? Rotate & Predict!"*
**C**ount → **T**rack → **R**otate → **P**redict. Say it once before every question.

## Part 3: What Is Figure Classification?

In **classification** (also called "odd one out"), you get 4–5 figures. All except one share a common property. Find the one that doesn't belong.

### The Six Classic Properties to Check

1. **Rotation vs. Reflection:** Four figures are rotations of the same figure; one is a mirror image. *(The most frequent classification trap!)*
2. **Element position:** In four figures, the dot is inside the smaller shape; in one, it's outside.
3. **Number of elements:** Four figures have an even number of lines/dots; one has odd.
4. **Enclosure:** In four figures, one shape is completely inside another; in one, they merely touch or overlap.
5. **Direction:** Four arrows point clockwise; one points anti-clockwise.
6. **Division:** In four figures, a line divides the shape into two equal halves; in one, unequal.

### How to Solve

Don't compare all figures at once. Instead, **pick any two figures and ask: "What is common between them?"** Then check whether the third shares it. The figure that breaks the common property is your answer.

## Part 4: Worked Mini-Examples

**Example 1 (Series):** A square has a dot that moves one corner clockwise in each step, while a small line on the top side rotates 90° clockwise each step. After figure 4, where will the dot be?
*Solution:* Track only the dot: top-left → top-right → bottom-right → bottom-left → next is **top-left**. Ignore everything else until the dot's position is fixed. Then verify the line's rotation to choose between look-alike options.

**Example 2 (Classification):** Five figures show a triangle with a line through it. In four, the line passes through the triangle's vertex; in one, it passes through the middle of a side. The odd one is the last — the property is *"line passes through a vertex."*

Notice the method: **state the common property in words first**, then eliminate. If you can't say the property in one sentence, you haven't found it yet.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Blurry figures on screen.** In computer-based exams (SSC, IBPS), small dots and thin lines look faint. Train your eye to zoom in mentally: always verify whether a dot is *inside* or *outside* a shape before answering. Most "silly mistakes" in this chapter are actually reading mistakes.

**Trap 2 — Difference hidden in look-alike figures.** Two answer options often differ in only one tiny detail — the direction of one arrowhead, or a dot shifted by one corner. Never finalize an answer after matching just one element; confirm the second element too.

**Trap 3 — Slight change in rotation/reflection.** A figure rotated 90° and its mirror image can look nearly identical at a glance. The examiner places the mirror image as a tempting option. Fix: mark one "landmark" (e.g., a black dot on a specific arm) and check whether it lands where pure rotation would put it — if it lands on the wrong side, it's a reflection, not a rotation.
