# The Feynman Way: Clock & Calendar Explained Like You're 12

## The Hardest Concept: "Odd Days" — Why Calendars Repeat

Most students can handle clock angles with practice. But the one idea that makes everyone freeze is **odd days** in calendar problems. Let's crack it with a story.

### The Story of Ravi's Birthday

Imagine your friend Ravi was born on a **Monday**. Next year, you want to know: on which day will his birthday fall?

You might guess "Monday again!" — but no. Here's why.

A normal year has **365 days**. Now, a week has 7 days. Divide:

**365 ÷ 7 = 52 weeks and 1 day left over.**

That "1 day left over" is the whole secret. After 52 full weeks, the calendar comes back to the same day — and then **one extra day pushes everything forward by one**. So Ravi's next birthday falls on **Tuesday**.

That leftover day is called an **odd day**. That's it. That's the entire concept.

- **Normal year (365 days)** → 1 odd day
- **Leap year (366 days)** → 2 odd days (because 366 ÷ 7 leaves 2 extra days)

### Why Leap Years Exist (The Clock Story)

The Earth takes about **365 days and 6 hours** to go around the Sun. We ignore those 6 hours every year… but 6 hours × 4 years = 24 hours = **one full extra day**. So every 4th year we add February 29 to "catch up." That year has 366 days → 2 odd days.

**The trap:** A century year (like 1900, 2100) is a leap year **only if divisible by 400**. So 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not. Examiners love this trap!

### Counting Odd Days Over Many Years

Want the day of the week for a far-off date? Just count total odd days from a known reference:

- **100 years** = 5 odd days (76 normal + 24 leap years → 76×1 + 24×2 = 124 days → 124 ÷ 7 leaves 5)
- **200 years** = 3 odd days
- **300 years** = 1 odd day
- **400 years** = 0 odd days ← this is why the calendar **repeats every 400 years**!

Then take the total odd days, divide by 7, and the remainder tells you how many days to move forward from your reference day. Remainder 0 = same day, 1 = one day ahead, and so on.

## The Unforgettable Trick: "The Extra Guest"

Think of every week as a dinner table with exactly **7 chairs**. Every year, 365 guests arrive. They fill 52 full tables — and **one extra guest** is left standing. That standing guest pushes the next year one chair forward. In a leap year, **two guests** are left standing.

Whenever you see "odd days," just ask: *"How many guests are left standing after filling tables of 7?"* Divide by 7, keep only the **remainder**, and ignore the quotient completely. The quotient is a distraction — the remainder is the answer.

**Memory line:** *"Divide by seven, keep the rest — the remainder passes the test!"*

## The Clock Version of the Same Idea

The clock works the same way, just with 12 instead of 7:

- The minute hand gains **55 minutes** on the hour hand every 60 minutes.
- So the two hands **coincide every 65(5/11) minutes**, not every 60 — another "extra guest" situation!
- Angle formula: **Angle = |30H − (11/2)M|** — if the answer is more than 180°, subtract it from 360°.

Same philosophy: track the *gap*, not the whole journey.
