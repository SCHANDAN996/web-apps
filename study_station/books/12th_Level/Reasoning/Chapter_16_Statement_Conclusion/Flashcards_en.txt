Card 1
Front: What is the golden rule for judging a conclusion in Statement & Conclusion questions?
Back: A conclusion is valid only if it follows definitely and directly from the statement — no outside knowledge, no assumptions, no "probably true" ideas.

Card 2
Front: If a conclusion is "possibly true" but not certain, should you accept it?
Back: No. In these questions, "possibly true" = invalid. Only conclusions that must be true are accepted.

Card 3
Front: What does the mnemonic "DON" stand for in Statement & Conclusion?
Back: D – Definite (must be certain), O – Only from the statement (no outside info), N – No assumptions. If a conclusion fails any letter, reject it.

Card 4
Front: What is the "keyword test" for quickly rejecting a wrong conclusion?
Back: Look for extreme or absolute words in the conclusion — "all", "always", "never", "only", "best" — when the statement doesn't support them. Such conclusions are usually invalid.

Card 5
Front: The statement says "Some students passed." A conclusion says "All students passed." Valid or invalid — and why?
Back: Invalid. "Some" never guarantees "all". Expanding the scope beyond the statement is a classic examiner trap.

Card 6
Front: What is an examiner's favourite trap using real-world facts?
Back: Giving a conclusion that is true in real life but not stated or implied in the statement. Remember: real-world truth is irrelevant — only the statement matters.

Card 7
Front: Statement: "The company launched a new product to increase sales." Conclusion: "Sales will definitely increase." Valid?
Back: Invalid. Intention ("to increase") is not a guarantee of result. Purpose ≠ outcome — a very common trap.

Card 8
Front: How should you treat conclusions that merely restate the statement in different words?
Back: Accept them. A conclusion that is a direct restatement or an obvious paraphrase of the statement definitely follows.

Card 9
Front: What does the mnemonic "SAFE" remind you to check before accepting a conclusion?
Back: S – Stated or directly implied? A – Absolute words avoided? F – Facts limited to the statement? E – Emotion/opinion of your own removed?

Card 10
Front: If a statement is negative ("No employee was late"), can a positive conclusion ("All employees were punctual") follow?
Back: Yes, if it is a logically equivalent restatement. "No one was late" directly means everyone was on time — equivalent conversions are valid.

Card 11
Front: Statement: "The government advised people to save water." Conclusion: "People are wasting water." Does it follow?
Back: No. Advice to save water does not prove people are currently wasting it — the advice could be precautionary. Don't read causes into advice.

Card 12
Front: What is the difference between a "conclusion" and an "assumption" in reasoning questions?
Back: A conclusion comes after the statement (what follows from it); an assumption comes before (what is taken for granted while making the statement). Don't mix the two question types.

Card 13
Front: In "either conclusion I or II follows" cases, what two conditions must be met?
Back: (1) The two conclusions must be contradictory/complementary (one positive, one negative on the same subject), and (2) neither must follow individually. Then "either-or" is the answer.

Card 14
Front: Examiner's trap: a conclusion uses the same words as the statement but adds "therefore it is the only reason." Why reject it?
Back: "Only" makes it absolute. The statement gives one reason; claiming it is the sole reason goes beyond the given information.

Card 15
Front: How much time should you ideally spend on one Statement & Conclusion question in the exam?
Back: About 30–40 seconds. Read the statement once carefully, apply the DON test to each conclusion, and move on — don't debate real-world logic.

Card 16
Front: Statement: "Regular exercise improves health." Conclusion: "Those who don't exercise are unhealthy." Valid?
Back: Invalid. The statement says exercise helps; it says nothing about the condition of those who don't exercise. Denying the premise is a logical fallacy trap.

Card 17
Front: When both conclusions seem partly correct, what should you do?
Back: Test each independently with the DON rule. Accept only the one that definitely follows; if both definitely follow, mark "both follow" — never force an either-or.

Card 18
Front: Why should you ignore your own opinions about topics like politics, economy or society in these questions?
Back: Because conclusions must be judged purely on the given statement. Personal beliefs make you accept emotionally agreeable but logically unsupported conclusions — exactly what the examiner wants.
