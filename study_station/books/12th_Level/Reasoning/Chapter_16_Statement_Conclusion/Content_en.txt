# Statement & Conclusion

## 1. What You Will Learn

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Understand what a **Statement** and a **Conclusion** really mean in exam language.
- Decide, in seconds, whether a conclusion **definitely follows** from a statement or not.
- Handle the standard answer formats: *Only I follows*, *Only II follows*, *Either I or II follows*, *Neither follows*, *Both follow*.
- Spot the traps examiners plant — extreme words, emotional wording, and "almost correct" conclusions.
- Solve these questions in **30–40 seconds** each with full confidence.

---

## 2. The Hook: The Judge Inside Your Head

Imagine you are a judge. A lawyer stands up and says only this:

> "It rained heavily in the city last night."

Now two conclusions are placed before you:

- **Conclusion I:** The streets were wet in the morning.
- **Conclusion II:** The city's drainage system is poor.

Can you honestly declare both guilty of being true? The first one — yes, it follows naturally. The second one — the lawyer never said anything about drainage. Maybe the drainage is excellent and still the streets were wet. A good judge never goes beyond the evidence.

**That judge is you in the exam hall.** The statement is the *only* evidence you have. The moment you use your outside knowledge, opinions, or assumptions — you lose the mark. This single idea is the heart of this entire chapter.

---

## 3. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Almost every student makes the same mistake here: they treat the statement as a *starting point for a debate* instead of a *closed box of facts*.

Typical wrong thinking:

- "Well, in real life this usually happens, so the conclusion must follow."
- "The conclusion sounds sensible and positive, so it must be correct."
- "Both conclusions look related to the topic, so both must follow."

The examiner **knows** you think like this. That is exactly why conclusions are written to sound reasonable, moral, or practical — while not actually following from the statement. In this chapter, we will train you to think like the judge, not like a debater.

---

## 4. Chunk 1 — What Is a Statement?

A **Statement** is a sentence or a short passage given in the question. For the exam, it is:

- **Always assumed to be TRUE** — even if it sounds false in real life.
- The **complete and only** source of information.
- Not to be questioned, doubted, or extended.

**Example:**
> Statement: "All the students of Class X who scored above 90% received a scholarship."

Even if you personally know a school where this didn't happen — forget it. Inside this question, this statement is the law.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In a Statement & Conclusion question, are you allowed to use your real-world knowledge to judge the statement? Why not?

---

## 5. Chunk 2 — What Is a Conclusion?

A **Conclusion** is a statement that must be **definitely and necessarily true** if the given statement is true.

The golden test — ask yourself:

> "If the statement is 100% true, is this conclusion **forced** to be true? Or is it merely *possible*?"

- **Definitely true** → Conclusion **follows** ✅
- **Probably true / possibly true / true in real life** → Conclusion **does NOT follow** ❌

**Example:**
> Statement: "Some doctors in the city hospital are cardiologists."
> - Conclusion I: Some cardiologists work in the city hospital. ✅ (directly follows — "some doctors are cardiologists" means those cardiologists are in that hospital)
> - Conclusion II: Cardiology is the most difficult branch of medicine. ❌ (the statement says nothing about difficulty)

🧠 **Active Recall:** What is the difference between a conclusion that is "possibly true" and one that "definitely follows"? Which one earns the mark?

---

## 6. Chunk 3 — The Golden Rules

### Rule 1: The statement is the whole universe
Nothing exists outside it. No general knowledge, no news, no personal experience.

### Rule 2: "Definite" beats "Probable"
A conclusion must be **certain**, not just likely. Words like *may, might, could, possibly* in a conclusion usually mean it does **not** follow — unless the statement itself supports that possibility.

### Rule 3: Beware of extreme words
Conclusions containing **all, every, always, never, none, only, completely** are usually traps. Unless the statement itself uses such absolute language, an extreme conclusion does not follow.

> Statement: "A few employees of the company were found taking bribes."
> Conclusion: "All employees of the company are corrupt." ❌ (extreme word "all" — classic trap)

### Rule 4: Suggestions and advice are NOT conclusions
If a sentence tells what *should be done* ("The government should…", "People must…"), it is a **course of action**, not a conclusion. It does not follow unless the question asks for courses of action.

### Rule 5: Don't add morality or emotion
"The culprit should be punished severely" — sounds noble, but it is an opinion, not a logical consequence.

### Rule 6: Rephrased statement = valid conclusion
If a conclusion simply restates the statement (or a direct part of it) in different words, it **follows**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** A conclusion says "All X are Y" but the statement only said "Some X are Y." Does it follow? Which rule applies?

## 7. Chunk 4 — The Standard Answer Formats

Most questions give two conclusions (I and II) and these options:

| Option | Meaning |
|---|---|
| (a) | Only Conclusion I follows |
| (b) | Only Conclusion II follows |
| (c) | Either I or II follows |
| (d) | Neither I nor II follows |
| (e) | Both I and II follow |

### When does "Either I or II" apply?
Only when **all three** conditions are met:
1. Both conclusions are individually **possible but not definite** (or one is positive, one negative about the same subject).
2. They are about the **same subject/predicate**.
3. Together they cover **all possibilities** (a complementary pair — e.g., "X will win" / "X will not win").

**Example:**
> Statement: "The match will be played tomorrow if the weather is clear."
> - I: The match will be played tomorrow.
> - II: The match will not be played tomorrow.
>
> Neither is definite (weather is unknown), they are contradictory and cover all cases → **Either I or II follows.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** What three conditions must be satisfied before you mark "Either I or II follows"?

---

## 8. Chunk 5 — Worked Examples

**Example 1**
> Statement: "The company has decided to increase the salaries of all its employees by 15% from next month."
> - I: The employees of the company will get more money from next month. ✅ (direct restatement)
> - II: The company is the best payer in the industry. ❌ (comparison with industry is nowhere in the statement)
>
> **Answer: Only I follows.**

**Example 2**
> Statement: "Regular exercise reduces the risk of heart disease."
> - I: People who never exercise will definitely get heart disease. ❌ (extreme + negative twist; "reduces risk" ≠ "certainly causes")
> - II: Exercise is beneficial for heart health. ✅ (direct, mild restatement)
>
> **Answer: Only II follows.**

**Example 3**
> Statement: "The government has increased the tax on tobacco products."
> - I: The government wants to discourage tobacco consumption. ✅ (a reasonable, direct purpose behind a tax hike — such purpose-based conclusions are accepted when they are the natural, direct implication)
> - II: Tobacco consumption in the country will stop completely. ❌ (extreme word "completely")
>
> **Answer: Only I follows.**

Notice the pattern: the correct conclusion is usually the **calm, mild, directly-connected** one. The wrong one is usually **extreme, emotional, comparative, or off-topic**.

---

## 9. Mnemonic Trick — **"JUDGE"**

Before marking any conclusion, run it through the **JUDGE** filter:

- **J** — *Just the statement*: Is it based only on the given statement?
- **U** — *Unavoidable*: Is it definitely true, not just probably?
- **D** — *Don't add*: No outside knowledge, no assumptions?
- **G** — *Guard against extremes*: No "all/never/always/completely" unless stated?
- **E** — *Emotion-free*: No advice, opinion, or moral judgement?

If a conclusion passes all five — it **follows**. Fail even one — it **does not**.

---

## 10. Summary Table

| Situation in Conclusion | Follows? |
|---|---|
| Direct restatement of the statement | ✅ Yes |
| Mild, direct, unavoidable implication | ✅ Yes |
| "May/might" possibility supported by statement | ✅ Yes |
| Extreme words (all, never, always) not in statement | ❌ No |
| Based on real-world knowledge, not the statement | ❌ No |
| Advice / suggestion / moral judgement | ❌ No |
| Comparison or new information not in statement | ❌ No |
| Contradictory complementary pair, both indefinite | Either I or II |

📌 **Review this chapter after 2 days.** Solve 10 questions then and check whether the JUDGE filter comes to your mind automatically.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — The "Almost Correct" Conclusion:**
The examiner writes a conclusion that is 90% supported by the statement but adds one small extra idea. Example: Statement says "Sales rose after the advertisement." Conclusion says "The advertisement was the sole reason for the rise in sales." The word *sole* makes it wrong. Read every conclusion word by word — the trap is always in one small word.

**Trap 2 — Difficult or Emotional Language to Confuse You:**
Statements are sometimes dressed in heavy vocabulary or emotional themes (poverty, corruption, patriotism) so that you react emotionally instead of logically. Remember: the fancier the language, the calmer you must become. Strip the sentence down to its bare facts, then apply the JUDGE filter.

**Trap 3 — The Double-Relation Question (Fake "Either-Or"):**
Two conclusions look like opposites, so students rush to mark "Either I or II follows." But check carefully — if one of them **definitely follows** or **definitely contradicts** the statement, the either-or rule collapses. "Either–or" works only when **both** conclusions are indefinite **and** together cover all possibilities. Marking (c) blindly is one of the most common ways students lose marks in this chapter.
