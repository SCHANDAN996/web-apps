# 🪄 Short Tricks & Mnemonics — Statement & Conclusion

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 1: "MUST vs MAY"
> **"A conclusion MUST follow, not MAY follow."**

If you can imagine even ONE situation where the conclusion is false while the statement is true — reject it. The word "must" is your gatekeeper; "may" is the trap.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 2: "The 100% Club"
> **"Only 100% sure conclusions enter the club. 99% stands outside."**

A conclusion that is *probably* true is still wrong. Probability ≠ logical certainty.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 3: "SCE — Statement Contains Everything"
> **"SCE: The Statement Contains Everything you know."**

Pretend you were born the moment you read the statement. No outside knowledge, no newspaper facts, no "but in real life…". If it's not in the statement, it doesn't exist.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 4: "EITHER-OR Twins"
> **"Same subject + opposite actions + both can't be true together = EITHER-OR twins."**

When two conclusions are mirror opposites (e.g., "Ram passed" / "Ram failed") and one of them MUST be true, mark "either I or II follows."

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 5: "ALL-EXTREME Alarm"
> **"Words like ALL, EVERY, NEVER, ONLY, ALWAYS ring the alarm bell."**

Extreme conclusions from a mild statement are almost always wrong. Statement says "some students failed" → conclusion "all students are weak" = rejected instantly.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 6: "The Judge Rule"
> **"Be a judge, not an advisor."**

Conclusions that give *suggestions* ("The government should…", "People must…") are advice, not deductions. Advice never "follows" logically — reject it.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 7: "COPY-CAT Check"
> **"A safe conclusion is often a copy-cat — it restates the statement in new words."**

If conclusion II is just the statement paraphrased, it follows. Don't reject it for being "too obvious."

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 8: "NEGATIVE Magnet"
> **"A negative statement attracts only negative conclusions."**

Statement: "No student failed." A positive conclusion ("Everyone is brilliant") overreaches. Stay within the statement's tone and scope.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 9: "The Two-Step Sieve"
> **"Step 1: Is it stated or directly implied? Step 2: Does it add anything extra?"**

Any conclusion failing Step 1 or adding extra in Step 2 falls through the sieve. Two questions, five seconds, done.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 10: "REAL-WORLD Blindfold"
> **"Wear the blindfold: the statement is the whole universe."**

Even if the statement says "The moon is made of cheese," your conclusions must follow from THAT world. Logic > reality inside the exam hall.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 11: "BOTH-FOLLOW Test"
> **"Before marking 'both follow', test each conclusion ALONE."**

Many students mark "both" because conclusion I is right and II *sounds* related. Each conclusion must independently pass the MUST test.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 12: "KEYWORD Handshake"
> **"The conclusion must shake hands with the statement's keywords."**

If the conclusion introduces a brand-new term (new place, new group, new cause) absent from the statement, the handshake fails — reject.

---

## 🧠 Mnemonic 13: "15-Second First Look"
> **"Read statement → predict the answer in your head → then look at conclusions."**

Predicting first stops the options from hypnotising you. Examiners design conclusion II to *sound* smart; your prediction is the shield.

---

## ⏭️ Skip Strategy — When to Skip

**Tip 1 — The 20-Second Rule:**
If after 20 seconds you cannot decide whether a conclusion "must" follow, mark it for review and move on. Statement–Conclusion questions are usually quick scorers; a confusing one eats the time of two easy questions.

**Tip 2 — Skip the "Double-Negative Either-Or":**
If both conclusions are negative, use words like "not / cannot / no," AND look like an either-or pair, the analysis gets tricky. If you can't resolve it in one clean pass, skip and return later — these are the highest error-rate questions in this chapter.

**Tip 3 — Skip Long Paragraph Statements (temporarily):**
If the statement is 3–4 lines long with multiple facts, don't attempt it first. Finish all one-line statement questions, bank those marks, then come back to the paragraph ones with a fresh mind.
