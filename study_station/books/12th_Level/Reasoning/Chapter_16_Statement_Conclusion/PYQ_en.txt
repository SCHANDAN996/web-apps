# Statement & Conclusion — PYQ Analysis (English)

## What examiners actually ask

Statement & Conclusion is a regular feature of SSC CGL, CHSL, Banking and RRB exams. It is one of the most predictable chapters in Verbal Reasoning: the format barely changes, and the traps repeat.

**Sub-topic weightage (pattern-based, not counts):**
- Direct conclusion questions (statement → which conclusion follows) form the bulk of the questions.
- "Which conclusion logically follows" with two conclusions (I and II) is the most common format.
- "Only conclusion I follows / Only II follows / Both follow / Neither follows" — this option set is the standard trap zone.
- Banking exams (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI) tend to ask slightly longer statements with social or economic themes; SSC tends to ask short, crisp statements.

**Exam-wise distribution (qualitative):**
- SSC CGL/CHSL: usually a small number of questions in the reasoning section, moderate difficulty.
- IBPS/SBI: questions often embedded in longer statements with "if only I follows" options.
- RRB NTPC: direct, short statements, generally easier.
- The 15-second rule applies: if the statement is short and the conclusions are direct, attempt it; if the statement involves multiple conditions and both conclusions look plausible, skip.

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with solutions and trap analysis)

**Q1.** Statement: All pens are books. All books are chairs.
Conclusions: I. All pens are chairs. II. All chairs are pens.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
Answer: (a)
Solution: All pens → books → chairs, so all pens are chairs (I follows). All chairs need not be pens — the reverse does not follow.
Trap: Students often pick "both follow" assuming symmetry. The hook is the "reverse conclusion" trap — just because A⊂B⊂C does not mean C⊂A.

**Q2.** Statement: Some students are players. All players are singers.
Conclusions: I. Some students are singers. II. Some singers are students.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
Answer: (c)
Solution: Some students are players, and all players are singers, so some students are singers (I). Since some students are singers, some singers are students (II) — conversion of "some" works both ways.
Trap: Students think "some A are B" does not convert. The hook is the conversion rule — "some" statements are reversible.

**Q3.** Statement: No cat is a dog. Some dogs are animals.
Conclusions: I. No cat is an animal. II. Some animals are not dogs.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
Answer: (d)
Solution: "No cat is a dog" does not extend to "no cat is an animal" (I fails). "Some dogs are animals" does not imply "some animals are not dogs" (II fails).
Trap: The negative statement "no cat is a dog" lures students into thinking "no cat is an animal" — the hook is the "negative statement" trap.

**Q4.** Statement: All engineers are graduates. Some graduates are doctors.
Conclusions: I. Some engineers are doctors. II. Some graduates are engineers.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
Answer: (b)
Solution: "All engineers are graduates" converts to "some graduates are engineers" (II). "Some graduates are doctors" does not connect engineers to doctors (I fails).
Trap: Students pick I because "some graduates are doctors" seems to link engineers and doctors — but the link is missing. The hook is the "middle term" trap.

**Q5.** Statement: All politicians are corrupt. Some corrupt people are caught.
Conclusions: I. Some politicians are caught. II. All corrupt people are politicians.
(a) Only I follows (b) Only II follows (c) Both follow (d) Neither follows
Answer: (d)
Solution: "Some corrupt people are caught" does not connect politicians to being caught (I fails). "All politicians are corrupt" does not mean all corrupt people are politicians (II fails).
Trap: The hook is the "some" trap — "some corrupt people are caught" does not imply "some politicians are caught."

## Skip Strategy (15-second rule)

- **Check the middle term:** when the statement has "some" and a conclusion also has "some," verify that the middle term actually connects the two groups. If it does not connect, the conclusion fails — and if both conclusions depend on such a link, skip quickly.
- **Check negative statements:** when the statement has "no," a negative conclusion follows only if the exclusion is directly stated or forced. If the "no" does not extend to the term in the conclusion, reject it.
- **Check reverse conclusions:** when the statement has "all A are B" and a conclusion says "all B are A," the reverse never follows — reject it immediately.
- **Skip** if the statement involves multiple conditions and both conclusions look plausible at first glance — these are the time-eaters.

## Time Rule Review

- **15-second rule:** If the statement is short and the conclusions are direct, attempt it.
- **45-second rule:** If the statement involves multiple conditions and both conclusions look plausible, skip it and return later if time permits.
- **Middle-term check:** If the statement has "some" and a conclusion has "some," confirm the middle term connects the two groups; if it does not, that conclusion fails.
- **Negative check:** If the statement has "no," confirm the exclusion actually covers the term in the conclusion; if it does not, that conclusion fails.
