126. Statement: Should the government make it mandatory for all private companies to reserve a fixed percentage of jobs for economically weaker sections?
Arguments:
I. Yes. Economic weakness, not caste alone, is the real barrier to opportunity, and reservation based on it will ensure social justice.
II. No. Reservation in the private sector will discourage merit-based hiring and reduce the competitiveness of Indian industry.
(a) Only argument I is strong (b) Only argument II is strong (c) Both arguments I and II are strong (d) Neither argument I nor argument II is strong
Answer: (c)
Solution: The trap is to pick only the side you personally agree with; in Statement–Argument questions both sides can be strong simultaneously when each rests on a substantial public-interest reason. Argument I is strong because it invokes social justice and equal opportunity, a recognised policy ground. Argument II is strong because merit and industrial competitiveness are also legitimate national concerns. Since both raise weighty, relevant considerations, the answer is both arguments I and II are strong.
Source: PYQ-style

127. Statement: Should India ban the use of all single-use plastics immediately across the country?
Arguments:
I. Yes. Single-use plastics are a major source of pollution and harm marine life, so an immediate ban is the only effective solution.
II. No. An immediate ban without affordable alternatives in place would hurt small traders, vendors and millions of livelihoods dependent on the plastic industry.
(a) Both arguments I and II are strong (b) Only argument II is strong (c) Only argument I is strong (d) Neither argument I nor argument II is strong
Answer: (a)
Solution: The tempting error is to treat "only effective solution" in Argument I as making it automatically strong and Argument II as mere excuse-making; but Argument II raises a genuine implementation concern — livelihoods and absence of alternatives — which is a standard strong-argument ground. Argument I is strong on environmental protection, a matter of public interest. Both are substantial and relevant, so the answer is both arguments I and II are strong.

128. Statement: Should the voting age in India be reduced from 18 years to 16 years?
Arguments:
I. Yes. Sixteen-year-olds are mature enough today due to education and exposure to information, and many countries allow voting at 16.
II. No. At 16, most individuals are still in school and lack the political understanding and independent judgement needed to vote responsibly.
(a) Only argument I is strong (b) Only argument II is strong (c) Neither argument I nor argument II is strong (d) Both arguments I and II are strong
Answer: (d)
Solution: The trap here is the phrase "many countries allow voting at 16" — students often reject Argument I as a weak "others do it" argument, but it is backed by the maturity-and-education reasoning, which is substantive. Argument II is strong because political maturity and independent judgement are directly relevant to the right to vote. Both sides present defensible, weighty grounds, so the answer is both arguments I and II are strong.

(a) Only argument I is strong (b) Only argument II is strong (c) Neither argument I nor argument II is strong (d) Both arguments I and II are strong
Answer: (d)
Solution: The tempting mistake is to reject Argument II because the deterrent value of the death penalty is debated; however, deterrence and retributive justice are standard strong grounds in such questions, and the "rarest of rare" doctrine is part of Indian law. Argument I is strong because irreversibility and judicial error are serious, logical concerns. Both are weighty, so the answer is both arguments I and II are strong.
