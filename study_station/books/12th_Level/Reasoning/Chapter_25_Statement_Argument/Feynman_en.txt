# 🧒 The Feynman Way: Statement & Argument Explained Like You're 12

## The Cricket Captain Story

Imagine you're the captain of your school cricket team. Tomorrow is the big final. Your coach makes a **statement**:

> **"The team should practise for 3 extra hours tonight."**

Now two of your teammates give **arguments**:

- **Ravi says:** "Yes, because more practice always helps."
- **Meena says:** "No, because the players are already exhausted, and tired players will perform worse tomorrow."

Which argument is *strong*? Think like a detective, not a fan.

- Ravi's argument sounds nice, but "more practice always helps" is just a feel-good line. Always? Even at midnight before a final? It's **weak** — it's vague, emotional, and ignores the real situation.
- Meena's argument points to a **real consequence** (exhaustion → poor performance) that directly connects to the decision. That's **strong**.

**That's the whole chapter in one story.** A Statement & Argument question gives you a proposal, and you must judge whether each argument is *strong* (specific, relevant, factual, looks at real consequences) or *weak* (emotional, vague, extreme, just an example, or attacks a person).

## The One Rule That Solves 80% of Questions

> **A strong argument answers: "What actually happens if we do this — and why does it matter?"**
> A weak argument answers: "How do I *feel* about this?"

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"JUDGE, Don't CHEER"** ⚖️

Before marking any argument strong, ask these 4 questions. If it fails even one, it's **weak**:

1. **J** — *Just an opinion?* ("It would be nice/good/bad…") → Weak
2. **U** — *Unrealistic or extreme?* ("always", "never", "everyone", "completely") → Weak
3. **D** — *Disconnected from the statement?* (True fact, but about something else) → Weak
4. **G** — *Gives a real reason or consequence?* (Cause → effect, linked to the issue) → **Strong!**
5. **E** — *Example or person-attack only?* ("Sachin practised a lot…" / "The coach is stupid") → Weak

**Memory hook:** A judge doesn't cheer for a side — a judge weighs consequences. So should you.

## The Traps Examiners Love (So You Don't Fall)

- **The "true but irrelevant" trap:** The argument is a correct fact, but doesn't address *this* statement. Truth ≠ strength.
- **The "emotional appeal" trap:** "Think of the poor children!" — touching, but weak unless it gives a concrete reason.
- **The "both can't be strong" trap:** If Argument I says "Yes, because X" and Argument II says "No, because not-X", usually **only one** is strong. "Both strong" is rare — check twice before choosing it.
- **The "example = proof" trap:** One example never proves a general rule.
