Card 1
Front: What is the very first test every argument must pass in a Statement & Argument question?
Back: Relevance — the argument must directly address the statement. An argument that is true but talks about something else is always weak.

Card 2
Front: True or False: A strong argument must be a proven fact.
Back: False. A strong argument can be an opinion, but it must be logical, relevant, and address the core issue of the statement.

Card 3
Front: Which mnemonic reminds you of the 4 tests of a strong argument?
Back: "R-I-D-E": Relevant, Important (significant), Direct (not vague), Established (based on facts/accepted principles). If an argument fails any letter, it is weak.

Card 4
Front: An argument says "It would be good if…" or "It is desirable that…" — strong or weak?
Back: Weak. Mere wishful thinking or desirability without a reason is never a strong argument.

Card 5
Front: What is the examiner's favourite trap with "emotional appeal" arguments?
Back: Arguments like "Think of the poor farmers!" or "For the honour of the nation!" sound powerful but appeal to emotion, not logic. Unless backed by a concrete reason, they are weak.

Card 6
Front: If an argument simply restates the statement in different words, is it strong?
Back: No. Repetition of the statement adds no new reasoning — it is a weak argument.

Card 7
Front: What does the mnemonic "B-A-D" stand for when spotting weak arguments?
Back: B — Based on assumption only; A — Ambiguous/vague; D — Disproportionate (too extreme or trivial compared to the issue). B-A-D arguments are weak.

Card 8
Front: An argument cites an example of one person or one city to justify a national policy. Strong or weak?
Back: Weak. A single example or isolated case cannot establish a general rule — this is the "hasty generalization" trap.

Card 9
Front: Should your personal opinion about the statement influence your answer?
Back: Never. Judge only the logical strength of the given arguments, not whether you agree with the statement.

Card 10
Front: In "Only argument I is strong" type questions, what must you check first?
Back: Read each argument independently. Do not compare the two arguments against each other — each is tested on its own merit.

Card 11
Front: An argument uses words like "all", "always", "never", "completely". What should you suspect?
Back: Extreme or absolute claims are usually weak — real-world policies rarely work in absolutes. Check carefully before accepting.

Card 12
Front: What is the trap when both arguments look strong but contradict each other?
Back: Both CAN be strong even if they oppose each other (one "for", one "against"). If each is relevant and logical, the answer is "both are strong" — don't assume only one side can win.

Card 13
Front: An argument appeals to authority: "A famous actor supports this." Strong or weak?
Back: Weak. Appeal to authority or celebrity is not logical evidence unless the authority's expertise is directly relevant to the issue.

Card 14
Front: How many seconds should you ideally spend on one Statement & Argument question in the exam?
Back: About 30–40 seconds. Read the statement once, apply R-I-D-E to each argument, and mark. Don't re-read repeatedly.

Card 15
Front: What makes an argument "strong" even when it is short and simple?
Back: If it gives a concrete, relevant reason (economic, social, legal, practical) directly tied to the statement's core issue, even a one-line argument is strong.

Card 16
Front: An argument says "This is against our culture and tradition." Strong or weak?
Back: Usually weak. Tradition alone is not a logical justification unless the argument shows a concrete harm or benefit. Examiners use this as a classic emotional trap.

Card 17
Front: In questions with three or more arguments, what is the safe elimination strategy?
Back: Test each argument separately with R-I-D-E, mark each as strong/weak, then match your combination with the options. Never decide by "overall feel".

Card 18
Front: What is the difference between a Statement & Argument and a Statement & Assumption question?
Back: In Assumption, you find what is hidden/implied in the statement; in Argument, you judge whether the given reasons FOR or AGAINST an action are logically strong.
