# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Statement & Argument (English)

## 1. How Exams Ask This Chapter

Statement & Argument questions appear regularly in SSC CGL, SSC CHSL, RRB NTPC, IBPS PO/Clerk and State PCS prelims. The pattern is remarkably stable across years:

- A **statement** (a proposal, policy, or question) is given.
- Two or three **arguments** follow, labelled I, II (and III).
- You must decide which argument(s) is/are **"strong"** — i.e., logically relevant, factually grounded, and addressing the core issue.

The standard answer options are:
(a) Only I is strong (b) Only II is strong (c) Either I or II is strong (d) Neither I nor II is strong (e) Both I and II are strong

## 2. Sub-topics That Repeat Most Often

Examiners draw statements from a small, predictable pool:

1. **Government policy / bans** — "Should the government ban X?" (smoking, strikes, private tuition, loudspeakers)
2. **Education** — compulsory subjects, exams vs. grades, medium of instruction
3. **Economy & employment** — privatisation, reservations, minimum wage, foreign investment
4. **Social issues** — dowry, population control, voting age, capital punishment
5. **Technology & environment** — mobile phones in schools, plastic bans, nuclear energy

Within these, the **"Should…? Yes/No"** format dominates. Three-argument questions (I, II, III) are more common in Banking exams; two-argument questions dominate SSC and Railway.

## 3. Exam-wise Tendencies (in words, not numbers)

- **SSC CGL / CHSL**: Two arguments, moderate length statements, one argument usually clearly weak (emotional or irrelevant). Tests speed.
- **RRB NTPC / Group D**: Simple, everyday statements; the weak argument is often an extreme or absolute claim ("all", "never", "completely").
- **IBPS PO / Clerk**: Three arguments, longer statements, subtler traps — an argument may be *true* but not *relevant*, or *relevant* but based on an assumption.
- **State PCS**: Policy-heavy statements; arguments often hinge on constitutional or practical feasibility.

## 4. The Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

1. **The emotionally appealing argument** — "Yes, because it will harm our culture." Sounds strong, but it is vague and unverifiable. Weak.
2. **The true-but-irrelevant argument** — A factually correct statement that does not address the proposal. Truth ≠ strength.
3. **The extreme argument** — Contains "all", "always", "never", "completely". Almost always weak.
4. **The personal-opinion argument** — "I think…", "It is not desirable…" Opinions without reasons are weak.
5. **The both-sides-look-right trap** — Two arguments that are both reasonable but **contradictory** (one says Yes with logic, one says No with logic). The answer is usually **"either I or II"**, not "both" — this is the most-missed pattern.
6. **The assumption-based argument** — Assumes facts not in evidence ("No, because no country has ever done this"). Weak unless verifiable.

## 5. Top 10 High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions

**Q1.** Statement: Should the government ban the use of plastic bags completely?
Arguments: I. Yes, plastic bags are a major source of environmental pollution and are non-biodegradable.
II. No, the ban will inconvenience shoppers who are used to carrying goods in plastic bags.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is strong — it cites a verifiable, core reason (environmental harm). II is weak: temporary inconvenience to shoppers is trivial compared to the issue, and alternatives exist.
**Trap:** II appeals to everyday sympathy — the "emotional convenience" hook.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q2.** Statement: Should strikes be banned in essential services like hospitals?
Arguments: I. Yes, strikes in hospitals endanger patients' lives, and the right to life is paramount.
II. No, employees have a fundamental right to protest for their demands.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is strong — it addresses the core issue (patient safety) with a sound principle. II is weak as stated: the right to protest is not absolute and does not justify endangering lives; it ignores the "essential services" qualifier.
**Trap:** II sounds constitutionally noble — the "true-but-not-decisive" hook.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q3.** Statement: Should India make voting compulsory for all eligible citizens?
Arguments: I. Yes, compulsory voting will increase participation and make democracy more representative.
II. No, forcing people to vote violates their freedom of choice.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Both
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Both arguments are logical, relevant, and grounded in genuine principles — but they support **opposite** conclusions. When two strong arguments contradict each other, the answer is "either I or II", not "both".
**Trap:** The classic "both-sides-look-right" trap. Most students wrongly pick (d).
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q4.** Statement: Should private tuition be banned?
Arguments: I. Yes, it creates inequality between rich and poor students.
II. No, banning anything is always undemocratic.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is strong — it identifies a real, relevant harm. II is weak: the word "always" makes it an extreme, sweeping claim; many bans (e.g., on child labour) are clearly democratic and justified.
**Trap:** The extreme-word hook ("always"). Train yourself to reject absolutes.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q5.** Statement: Should the minimum age for voting in India be reduced from 18 to 16?
Arguments: I. Yes, 16-year-olds are mature enough to understand political issues.
II. No, 16-year-olds are easily influenced and lack adequate political awareness.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Both are reasoned, relevant positions on maturity — a genuinely debatable question — and they contradict each other. Hence "either I or II".
**Trap:** Students pick the argument they personally agree with. The test rewards recognising *balanced* debates, not taking sides.
**Source:** PYQ-style
**⏱ Skip-rule note:** If you cannot quickly see that both sides are balanced, mark and skip — this type costs more than 45 seconds when overthought.

**Q6.** Statement: Should English be made the compulsory medium of instruction in all schools in India?
Arguments: I. Yes, English is essential for higher education and global employment.
II. No, because I personally feel mother tongue is better for young children.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is strong — practical, relevant, verifiable. II is weak: "I personally feel" signals an unsupported opinion, even though the underlying idea could have been argued strongly with evidence.
**Trap:** The personal-opinion hook. The *content* may be defensible, but the *form* makes it weak.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q7.** Statement: Should the government privatise all public sector undertakings (PSUs)?
Arguments: I. Yes, privatisation always improves efficiency.
II. No, some PSUs serve strategic and social objectives that profit-driven private firms may neglect.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** I is weak — "always" is an extreme claim, and the statement's own word "all" is equally sweeping. II is strong — it gives a concrete, relevant reason (strategic/social roles) against blanket privatisation.
**Trap:** Two absolutes ("all", "always") in one question — examiners test whether you notice both.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q8.** Statement: Should capital punishment be abolished in India?
Arguments: I. Yes, no judicial system is infallible, and an innocent person once executed cannot be brought back.
II. No, it acts as a deterrent against the most heinous crimes.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Both
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Both arguments are weighty, relevant, and logically grounded — and they pull in opposite directions. This is a textbook "either/or" case.
**Trap:** Emotionally charged topic; students pick the side they believe in instead of checking balance.
**Source:** PYQ-style
**⏱ Skip-rule note:** If your personal view is strong on this topic, you are *more* likely to err — decide by structure (balanced + contradictory = either), or skip within 45 seconds.

**Q9.** Statement: Should mobile phones be allowed in schools?
Arguments: I. Yes, mobile phones are useful in emergencies for contacting parents.
II. No, mobile phones were invented recently.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Both (d) Neither
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** I is strong — a concrete, relevant benefit. II is weak: the recency of invention is true but completely irrelevant to whether phones belong in schools.
**Trap:** The true-but-irrelevant hook — II is factually correct, which tempts careless readers.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10.** Statement: Should there be a uniform civil code in India?
Arguments: I. Yes, it would ensure equal treatment of all citizens regardless of religion.
II. No, it may interfere with the personal laws and religious freedom of communities.
(a) Only I (b) Only II (c) Either I or II (d) Neither
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Both arguments rest on genuine constitutional values (equality vs. religious freedom) and contradict each other — a balanced debate, so "either I or II".
**Trap:** Politically sensitive topic; the examiner counts on your personal stance overriding structural analysis.
**Source:** PYQ-style

## 6. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Reviewed Against PYQ Patterns

- **15-second questions** (attempt immediately): One argument is clearly weak — extreme word ("always/never/all"), personal opinion ("I feel"), or obviously irrelevant (Q4, Q6, Q9). These are the majority in SSC/Railway papers.
- **45-second questions** (attempt with care): Balanced contradictory pairs where the answer is "either I or II" (Q3, Q5, Q8, Q10). You must verify that *both* sides are genuinely reasoned before choosing "either".
- **Skip candidates**: (1) Emotionally or politically charged topics where your own bias slows you down (Q8, Q10); (2) three-argument Banking-style questions where all three arguments look plausible — mark for review and return if time permits.

**Golden rule from the pattern:** When in doubt between "both" and "either", check whether the arguments reach the *same* conclusion (→ both) or *opposite* conclusions (→ either). This single check resolves the most frequently missed question type in this chapter.
