# Statement & Argument

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Understand what a "Statement" and an "Argument" really mean in exam language.
2. Judge whether an argument is **strong** or **weak** using clear tests.
3. Spot the examiner's favourite traps before they trap you.
4. Solve Statement–Argument questions in under 30 seconds with confidence.

---

## 1. A Hook: The Courtroom Inside Your Exam Paper

Imagine a courtroom. A lawyer stands up and says, *"My client is innocent!"* — that is a **statement**, a claim. But the judge doesn't care about claims. The judge asks: *"Why? Give me a reason."* The reason the lawyer gives is the **argument**.

Now here is the twist: in your exam, **you are the judge**. The paper gives you a statement (a proposal, an opinion, a policy idea) and then two or more arguments. Your job is not to agree or disagree personally — your job is to decide which arguments are **strong** (solid, relevant, logical) and which are **weak** (emotional, vague, irrelevant, or extreme).

Master this one skill, and Statement–Argument questions in SSC, Banking, and Railway exams become some of the quickest marks you can earn.

---

## 2. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Almost every student makes the same mistake here: they judge arguments by **their own opinion**.

Example: *Statement: Should smoking be banned in all public places?*
A student who hates smoking marks every anti-smoking argument as "strong" — even a silly one like *"Yes, because smokers are bad people."* That argument is **weak** (it attacks people, not the issue).

The golden rule: **Leave your opinion outside the exam hall.** Only logic enters with you.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what is the difference between a statement and an argument? Say it aloud in one line.

## 3. What Is a Strong Argument?

An argument is **STRONG** if it passes these tests:

| Test | Question to Ask |
|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Does it directly address the statement? |
| **Logic** | Is the reason sensible and fact-based? |
| **Depth** | Does it touch a real consequence (social, economic, legal, health)? |
| **Practicality** | Is it realistic, not wishful thinking? |

**Example:**
*Statement: Should the government make primary education compulsory and free?*
- *Argument I: Yes, because education is a fundamental right and reduces poverty.* → **Strong** (relevant, logical, deep).
- *Argument II: No, because children should enjoy their childhood.* → **Weak** (emotional, ignores the real issue).

---

## 4. What Is a Weak Argument? (The 5 Weaknesses)

Memorise these five — most weak arguments fall into one of them:

1. **Emotional** – appeals to feelings, not facts. *("No, because it would be very sad.")*
2. **Vague / Superficial** – says nothing concrete. *("Yes, because it is a good idea.")*
3. **Extreme / Absolute** – uses "all", "never", "completely" unrealistically. *("Yes, this will end all corruption forever.")*
4. **Irrelevant** – talks about something else. *(Statement about education; argument about cricket.)*
5. **Personal attack / Prejudice** – attacks a person or group instead of the idea.

### 🎯 Mnemonic Trick: **"E-V-E-I-P" → "Every Vague Excuse Is Pathetic"**
**E**motional, **V**ague, **E**xtreme, **I**rrelevant, **P**ersonal attack — if an argument smells of any of these, mark it **weak**.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. List the 5 weaknesses using the E-V-E-I-P mnemonic.

---

## 5. Special Case: "Either I or II" Questions

Sometimes two arguments are **mutually exclusive** — both cannot be strong together, but each is individually reasonable.

*Statement: Should India have a single national language?*
- *I: Yes, it would strengthen national unity.*
- *II: No, India is a diverse country and imposing one language would hurt regional identities.*

Both are strong, but they contradict each other → Answer: **Either I or II is strong.**

**Test:** If Argument I says "Yes because X" and Argument II says "No because not-X", and both reasons are logical — choose "either".

---

## 6. Special Case: Desirability vs. Possibility

A classic trap: an argument can be **desirable** (nice to have) but not **practical**, or vice versa.

*Statement: Should every village in India be connected by air travel?*
- *"Yes, because fast travel is good for everyone."* → Desirable-sounding, but **impractical and vague** → Weak.

Always ask: *Is this actually workable, or just a dream?*

🧠 **Active Recall:** In "either I or II" questions, what single feature of the two arguments tells you the answer?

---

## 7. The 30-Second Solving Method

1. **Read the statement twice.** Identify the core issue (policy? ban? new rule?).
2. **Read each argument separately.** Never compare them first.
3. **Apply E-V-E-I-P.** Any hit → weak.
4. **Check relevance.** Off-topic → weak.
5. **Check for contradiction.** Two strong but opposite arguments → "either".
6. **Mark and move.** Do not re-read. Your first logical judgement is usually right.

## 8. Summary Table

| Argument Type | Verdict | Example Signal Words |
|---|---|---|
| Fact-based, relevant, practical | **Strong** | "because it reduces…", "as per law…" |
| Emotional | Weak | "sad", "heartless", "wonderful" |
| Vague | Weak | "good idea", "bad thing" |
| Extreme | Weak | "all", "never", "completely", "forever" |
| Irrelevant | Weak | talks about a different subject |
| Personal attack | Weak | blames a person/group |
| Two logical but opposite | **Either** | "Yes because X" vs "No because not-X" |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — Multiple plausible relationships in the options.**
The examiner designs options so that *both* arguments look strong at first glance. One will secretly be vague or emotional. Read each argument **word by word** — the weakness usually hides in a single word like "all", "always", or "everyone".

**Trap 2 — Difficult, fancy words to confuse you.**
Words like "ameliorate", "stringent", or "egalitarian" are inserted to make a weak argument *sound* intellectual and strong. Remember: **fancy vocabulary ≠ strong logic.** Translate the argument into simple Hindi/English in your head, then judge it.

**Trap 3 — Double-relationship (either/or) questions.**
When two arguments oppose each other, students panic and mark "both strong" or "neither strong". First check: are they **contradictory**? If yes, and each is individually logical, the answer is **"either I or II"** — not "both". Marking "both" for contradictory arguments is the single most common error in this chapter.
