# Inequality (असमानता)

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Read and understand the five inequality symbols instantly.
2. Decode coded inequalities (where symbols are replaced by words or signs).
3. Find the definite relationship between two elements using a chain.
4. Handle "either-or" (complementary pair) conclusions confidently.
5. Solve a standard 5-question inequality set in under 3 minutes.

---

## The Hook: One Symbol Can Cost You a Government Job

In SSC CGL, IBPS PO, and RRB NTPC, inequality questions look like the easiest marks in the reasoning section — and they are, *if* you respect them. Many aspirants lose marks here not because they didn't know the rules, but because they read `>` as `<` in a hurry, or forgot the "either-or" case. This chapter will make sure that never happens to you. Inequality is pure logic — no formulas to memorise, no calculations. Just symbols, a chain, and discipline.

---

## The Common Struggle (You're Not Alone)

Most students struggle with inequality for three reasons:

- **Symbol confusion under pressure** — `>` and `<` look similar when the clock is ticking.
- **Broken chains** — when the chain goes `A > B < C`, students force a relationship that doesn't exist.
- **The either-or panic** — when neither conclusion follows individually, students don't know what to mark.

We will fix all three, one chunk at a time.

---

## Chunk 1: The Five Symbols — Your Alphabet

| Symbol | Meaning | Memory Trick |
|---|---|---|
| `>` | Greater than | The crocodile's mouth opens toward the **bigger** number |
| `<` | Less than | Mouth opens toward the bigger number (so the smaller one is at the pointed end) |
| `≥` | Greater than or equal to | `>` with an "equal" line underneath |
| `≤` | Less than or equal to | `<` with an "equal" line underneath |
| `=` | Equal to | Both sides are the same |

**The Crocodile Rule 🐊:** A hungry crocodile always opens its mouth toward the bigger meal. So in `A > B`, the mouth faces A — A is bigger. In `A < B`, the mouth faces B — B is bigger.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking above — in `P < Q`, which element is bigger? Which way does the crocodile's mouth open?

---

## Chunk 2: The Priority Order (The Golden Rule)

When two symbols fight, the stronger one wins. Remember this priority:

> **`>` and `<` (strict) beat `≥` and `≤` (flexible), which beat `=`**

Think of it like a family hierarchy:

- **Strict symbols (`>`, `<`)** = the strict father — his decision is final.
- **Flexible symbols (`≥`, `≤`)** = the adjustable mother — she allows equality too.
- **Equal (`=`)** = the child — adjusts to everyone.

**Example:** If `A ≥ B` and `B > C`, then `A > C` (strict `>` wins over flexible `≥`).

🧠 **Active Recall:** If `X > Y` and `Y ≥ Z`, what is the definite relation between X and Z — strict or flexible?

---

## Chunk 3: Building the Chain

Inequality questions give you **statements** and ask you to check **conclusions**. The method:

**Step 1:** Write all statements in one line, joining common elements.

**Step 2:** Trace a path from the first element of the conclusion to the second.

**Step 3:** Apply the priority rule along the path.

**Example:**
Statements: `A > B ≥ C = D`
Conclusion I: `A > D` → Path: A > B ≥ C = D. The strict `>` dominates → **True.**
Conclusion II: `B > D` → Path: B ≥ C = D. Only flexible symbols → `B ≥ D`, not `B > D` → **False.**

**The Gate Rule 🚪:** If the path between two elements has **opposite-facing signs** (like `A > B < C`), the gate is closed — **no definite relationship exists**. Never force one.

🧠 **Active Recall:** In `M > N < O ≥ P`, can you find any definite relation between M and O? Why or why not?

---

## Chunk 4: The Either-Or (Complementary Pair) Case

This is where examiners trap the most students. Sometimes **neither conclusion follows individually**, but together they cover all possibilities. Then the answer is "either I or II follows."

**Three conditions must ALL be true:**

1. Both conclusions have the **same two elements**.
2. **Neither** conclusion is definitely true on its own.
3. The two conclusions together form a **complementary pair** — one of these combinations:
   - `>` and `≤`
   - `<` and `≥`
   - `=` and `≠` (equal vs. not equal)

Note: `>` and `<` alone are **not** a complementary pair, because they leave out the possibility of equality. A valid pair must cover *every* possible relationship between the two elements.

**Example:**
Statement: `A = B`
Conclusion I: `A > B` — False.
Conclusion II: `A ≤ B` — True (since A = B satisfies ≤).
Here II follows directly, so no either-or needed.

**Truer either-or example:**
Statement: `A ≥ B`
Conclusion I: `A > B` — not definite.
Conclusion II: `A = B` — not definite.
Both same elements, neither true, and together (`>` or `=`) they cover everything under `≥` → **Either I or II follows.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Statement: `P ≤ Q`. Conclusions: I. `P < Q`  II. `P = Q`. What is the answer?

---

## Chunk 5: Coded Inequalities

Here symbols are disguised in words:

> 'A @ B' means 'A is greater than B'
> 'A # B' means 'A is less than or equal to B'

**Strategy:** Before solving, make a small **translation table** on your rough sheet:

| Code | Real Symbol |
|---|---|
| @ | > |
| # | ≤ |

Then rewrite every statement and conclusion in real symbols, and solve normally. Never try to solve in coded form in your head — that's exactly where silly mistakes happen.

🧠 **Active Recall:** If 'A $ B' means 'A ≥ B' and 'A % B' means 'A < B', translate: `P $ Q % R`.

---

## Mnemonic Trick: "Crocodile Eats Big, Strict Dad Decides, Opposite Gates Close"

- 🐊 **Crocodile eats big** → mouth opens toward the bigger element.
- 👨 **Strict dad decides** → `>`/`<` override `≥`/`≤`.
- 🚪 **Opposite gates close** → `A > B < C` means no relation between A and C.

Say this line to yourself before every practice set.

## Summary Table

| Situation | Rule | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Same-direction signs in chain | Strict sign dominates | Definite relation exists |
| Mixed strict + flexible, same direction | Strict wins (`A ≥ B > C` → `A > C`) | Definite relation exists |
| Opposite-facing signs (`A > B < C`) | Gate closed | No relation |
| Only flexible signs (`A ≥ B ≥ C`) | Result stays flexible (`A ≥ C`) | Strict conclusion fails |
| Neither conclusion true, same elements, complementary pair | Either-Or | "Either I or II follows" |
| Coded symbols | Translate first, solve second | Avoids symbol confusion |

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1: Multiple possible relations shown in options.**
The examiner designs conclusions where a *possible* relation looks like a *definite* one. Example: from `A ≥ B`, the conclusion `A > B` is *possible* but not *definite*. Unless the question says "possibility," mark only what is 100% certain. When in doubt, ask: "Can I find even one case where this fails?" If yes, it's false.

**Trap 2: Confusing language to rattle you.**
Phrases like "A is not smaller than B" simply mean `A ≥ B`, and "A is not greater than B" means `A ≤ B`. Examiners use double negatives to slow you down. Translate every phrase into a symbol immediately — never reason in words.

**Trap 3: Double-relation (either-or) questions.**
Students see that neither conclusion is true and mark "neither follows" — forgetting to check the either-or conditions. Always run the 3-point checklist: same elements? neither true? complementary pair? If all three are yes, the answer is "either I or II follows." Missing this check is the single most common reason for losing marks in this chapter.
