# Statement & Course of Action — The Feynman Way

## The Hardest Idea, Explained Like You're 12

Imagine you are the captain of your school cricket team. One morning, the coach tells you: **"The ground is flooded with rainwater."**

That's it. That's the **Statement** — just a fact, a problem, a situation. The coach is NOT asking you a question. He is simply telling you what happened.

Now you, as captain, must decide what to **DO** about it. Your teammates shout out ideas:

- **Ravi:** "Let's cancel today's practice and inform all players."
- **Meena:** "Let's buy a new cricket bat."
- **Arjun:** "Let's ask the ground staff to drain the water and check if the ground dries by evening."
- **Sohan:** "Let's complain that the rain god is unfair."

Which ideas make sense? Ravi's and Arjun's — because they **directly attack the problem** (flooded ground). Meena's new bat doesn't remove water. Sohan's complaint changes nothing.

**That is the entire chapter.** A Statement & Course of Action question gives you:
1. A **Statement** = the flooded ground (a problem or situation).
2. Some **Courses of Action** = Ravi, Meena, Arjun, Sohan's suggestions.

Your job: pick only the suggestions that are **practical, logical, and directly solve the problem stated**.

## The Three Tests Every Action Must Pass

Before accepting any course of action, ask these three questions — like a strict umpire:

1. **Does it attack THIS problem?** (Relevance) — A new bat doesn't dry a ground. Reject.
2. **Can it actually be done?** (Practicality) — "Stop the rain forever" is impossible. Reject.
3. **Is it legal, fair, and sensible?** (Ethics/Logic) — "Punish the ground staff without enquiry" is unfair. Reject.

An action that fails even ONE test is out. Remember: **extreme actions** ("dismiss all employees", "shut down the railway") and **emotional actions** ("the government is useless") are almost always wrong. Also, an action that merely **repeats the statement** ("The ground is flooded, so we should note that the ground is flooded") is a trap — it does nothing.

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"CAPTAIN R-A-D"**

Whenever you see a Statement & Course of Action question, become the **Captain** and run **RAD**:

- **R — Relevant?** Does it solve the exact problem in the statement?
- **A — Achievable?** Can a real authority actually do it?
- **D — Direct & Decent?** Is it a direct step (not a vague promise) and fair/legal?

**RAD check passed = Follows. RAD check failed = Does not follow.**

One more captain's secret: if the statement is a **positive suggestion or a simple fact with no problem**, sometimes "none follows" is correct — don't force an action where none is needed. And watch for the word **"only"** in options — examiners love hiding a wrong action beside a right one.
