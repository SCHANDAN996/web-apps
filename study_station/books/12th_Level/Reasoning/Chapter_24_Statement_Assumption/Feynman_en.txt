# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Statement & Assumption Explained Like You're 12

## The Story of Ravi and the Umbrella

Imagine your friend Ravi looks out of the window and says:

> **"The sky is full of dark clouds. I should take an umbrella to school."**

Now here's a fun game. Ravi never *said* "it might rain." He never *said* "umbrellas protect from rain." But did he *think* these things? Of course he did! Without believing "clouds may bring rain" and "an umbrella keeps me dry," his sentence makes no sense at all.

That hidden thought — the thing Ravi **believes in his head but doesn't say out loud** — is called an **Assumption (पूर्वधारणा)**.

And that's the whole chapter. Seriously. A **Statement** is what is *spoken*. An **Assumption** is what the speaker *must be silently believing* for the statement to make sense.

## The Detective Rule: "Would the sentence collapse without it?"

Here's how a 12-year-old detective solves every question. Take the assumption, **delete it from the speaker's brain**, and ask: *does the statement still make sense?*

- Statement: *"Join our coaching institute to pass the SSC exam."*
- Test assumption: *"Coaching can help people pass."* Delete it → the speaker is inviting you to something useless. The statement **collapses**. ✅ Valid assumption.
- Test assumption: *"Our institute is the best in India."* Delete it → the speaker can still honestly invite you even if it's just *good*, not *best*. The statement **survives**. ❌ Not an assumption.

**If removing the thought breaks the sentence, it was really there. If the sentence survives, it was never assumed.**

## The Three Traps (Where Everyone Slips)

1. **The "Too Much" Trap** — Words like *best, only, all, always, 100%* in an assumption are suspicious. Ravi taking an umbrella doesn't mean he believes umbrellas *always* work in *every* storm. Extreme claims are rarely assumed.
2. **The "Restatement" Trap** — If the assumption just repeats the statement in new words, it's not hidden — it's a copy. An assumption must be *behind* the statement, not *inside* it.
3. **The "My Opinion" Trap** — Don't ask "Is this assumption *true in real life*?" Ask "Did *the speaker* believe it?" Even if the speaker is wrong, their belief still counts as an assumption.

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"The Invisible Backpack" 🎒**

Every speaker walks into the exam hall wearing an **invisible backpack** full of beliefs. Your only job: peek inside and check — *was THIS belief in the bag?*

- Belief needed to say the sentence? → **In the bag → Assumption is implicit. ✅**
- Extra decoration (best/only/all)? → **Not in the bag → ❌**
- Just a photocopy of the sentence? → **Stuck on the outside, not inside → ❌**

Say it aloud once: *"Statement is the speech, assumption is the backpack."* You'll never forget it.

## One Worked Mini-Example

**Statement:** "The principal announced: 'Students should reach school by 7:30 AM for the extra classes.'"

- Assumption I: *Students can reach by 7:30 AM.* → Delete it → the announcement is pointless. **In the backpack ✅**
- Assumption II: *All students love extra classes.* → Delete it → the announcement still stands (they may come unwillingly!). **Not in the backpack ❌**
