# Statement & Assumption

## 1. Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- Understand what a **Statement** and an **Assumption** really mean.
- Identify whether an assumption is **implicit** (hidden inside the statement) or not.
- Spot the difference between an assumption and a conclusion.
- Avoid the 3 most common traps examiners set in SSC, Banking, Railway and State exams.
- Solve any Statement–Assumption question in under 40 seconds with a fixed method.

---

## 2. The Hook: The Invisible Sentence

Read this sentence:

> "The Principal announced: 'All students must wear the new uniform from Monday.'"

Now answer honestly — did the Principal *say* that students are able to buy the new uniform? No. Did he *say* that a new uniform exists? No.

But wait… if students could not get the new uniform, would his announcement make any sense? Never.

So even though he never said it, he **assumed** it. That invisible, unsaid sentence hiding behind his words — that is an **Assumption**. And finding it is the entire game of this chapter.

Every day, people around you speak half-sentences and assume the other half. Advertisers do it. Politicians do it. And in your exam, the paper-setter does it — and asks *you* to catch it.

---

## 3. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Most students lose marks here for one simple reason: **they use their heart instead of the statement.**

They read: *"Buy Shine Soap for glowing skin"* and think — "Well, soap doesn't really make skin glow, so the assumption is false." Wrong approach! The question is not whether the assumption is *true in real life*. The question is whether the **speaker believed it** while making the statement.

The second struggle: students think "more assumptions = better answer." No. An assumption is valid only if the statement **collapses without it**. We will master this test soon.

Take a breath. This chapter has no formulas to memorise — only one skill to build: *reading the speaker's mind.*

## 4. Chunk 1: What Is a Statement? What Is an Assumption?

**Statement (कथन):** A sentence that declares something — an order, advice, suggestion, notice, or fact.

**Assumption (पूर्वधारणा):** Something the speaker **takes for granted** — accepts as true *before* speaking — without actually saying it.

Golden definition to remember:

> **An assumption is the unsaid foundation on which the statement stands.**

Example:
- **Statement:** "Join our coaching institute to clear SSC CGL."
- **Assumption I:** Coaching institutes help in clearing SSC CGL. ✅ (Implicit — the whole advertisement is built on this belief.)
- **Assumption II:** Our institute is the best in India. ❌ (Not implicit — the speaker never claimed to be the *best*; "best" is an extreme word.)

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking up, complete this: "An assumption is the _______ on which the statement _______." Now check. If you got it, move on. If not, re-read the golden definition once.

## 5. Chunk 2: The Collapse Test — Your Main Weapon

How do you check if an assumption is implicit? Use the **Collapse Test (also called the Negation Test)**:

1. Take the assumption.
2. **Negate it** (make it opposite).
3. Ask: *Does the statement still make sense?*
   - If the statement **collapses / becomes meaningless** → the assumption is **implicit**. ✅
   - If the statement **still survives** → the assumption is **not implicit**. ❌

**Example:**
- Statement: "The teacher told the students, 'Submit your homework by Friday.'"
- Assumption: "Students are capable of submitting homework by Friday."
- Negate it: "Students are NOT capable of submitting by Friday." → Then why would the teacher give the deadline? The statement collapses. ✅ **Implicit.**

**Counter-example:**
- Assumption: "All students love homework."
- Negate it: "Students don't love homework." → Does the teacher's order collapse? No! Orders don't need love. ❌ **Not implicit.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Statement — "Please switch off the fan when you leave." Test this assumption: "Someone will leave the room." Negate it. Does the request collapse? (Answer: Yes — if nobody leaves, the instruction is pointless. Implicit ✅)

## 6. Chunk 3: Assumption vs Conclusion — Don't Mix Them!

This confusion costs marks every year:

| Point | Assumption | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Comes **before** the statement (the cause/foundation) | Comes **after** the statement (the result) |
| Question to ask | "What did the speaker **already believe**?" | "What can we **derive** from this?" |
| Time | Past/hidden belief | Future/logical outcome |

**Memory trick:** **A comes before C** — in the alphabet AND in logic. **A**ssumption is the **A**ncestor (before); **C**onclusion is the **C**hild (after).

Example:
- Statement: "It is raining heavily, so the match has been cancelled."
- Assumption (before): Heavy rain can disrupt a match. ✅
- Conclusion (after): The players will go home. (Possible, but that's a different chapter's job.)

🧠 **Active Recall:** Say the mnemonic aloud three times: *"A before C — Assumption is the Ancestor, Conclusion is the Child."*

## 7. Chunk 4: The 5 Types of Statements You Will See

Examiners build questions from these five moulds. Recognise the mould, and the assumption reveals itself:

**Type 1 — Advertisement / Appeal:**
"Buy X, use Y, join Z."
→ Hidden assumption: *People will respond to this appeal; the product/service actually delivers the promised benefit.*

**Type 2 — Notice / Order / Instruction:**
"Employees must reach office by 9:30 AM."
→ Hidden assumption: *The instruction can be followed; people generally obey such orders.*

**Type 3 — Advice / Suggestion:**
"You should practise daily to succeed."
→ Hidden assumption: *Practice leads to success; the advice will be taken seriously.*

**Type 4 — Cause–Effect Statement:**
"Due to fog, trains are running late."
→ Hidden assumption: *Fog affects train movement.*

**Type 5 — Comparison / Claim:**
"Our bike gives more mileage than any other bike."
→ Hidden assumption: *Mileage matters to buyers; such a comparison is possible.*

Notice the pattern? In every type, ask: **"What must be true for the speaker to bother saying this?"**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Cover the table above. Name the 5 types. (Advertisement, Notice, Advice, Cause–Effect, Comparison.) Uncover and check.

## 8. Chunk 5: Words That Almost Always Make an Assumption WRONG

The examiner hides poison in sweet words. When an assumption contains these, be suspicious:

- **Extreme words:** *only, all, always, never, best, completely, every, none*
  - "Only our institute can make you successful." → "Only" is too extreme. Usually ❌.
- **Words beyond the statement's scope:** The statement talks about one school; the assumption talks about *all schools in India*. ❌
- **Restating the statement:** An assumption that just repeats the statement in new words is NOT an assumption — it's a paraphrase. ❌
- **Moral judgements:** "It is wrong to…", "One should never…" — unless the statement itself is moral, these are usually ❌.

**Mnemonic — "EARS" for rejecting assumptions:**
- **E**xtreme words (only, all, best)
- **A**lready stated (just repeats the statement)
- **R**emote / out of scope (goes far beyond the statement)
- **S**ubjective judgement (moral opinions, likes/dislikes)

If an assumption has EARS, don't listen to it! 👂❌

🧠 **Active Recall:** What does each letter of EARS stand for? Write it on a rough sheet now — this is your first blurt.

## 9. Chunk 6: The 4-Step Solving Method (Use in Exam Hall)

**Step 1 — Read the statement twice.** Identify its type (advertisement, notice, advice…).

**Step 2 — Ask the magic question:** *"What did the speaker take for granted?"*

**Step 3 — Apply the Collapse Test** to each assumption: negate it. Statement dies → implicit. Statement survives → reject.

**Step 4 — Run EARS** on the survivors. Extreme, repeated, remote, or subjective? Reject.

**Worked Example:**
> **Statement:** The government has advised people to use public transport to reduce air pollution.
> **Assumption I:** People may follow the government's advice.
> **Assumption II:** Public transport is the only cause of reduced pollution.

- Step 2: The government assumes people will listen. 
- Step 3: Negate I — "People will never follow advice." Then why advise? Collapse. ✅ I is implicit.
- Step 3+4: II has "only" (EARS — Extreme). Negate II — "Other measures also reduce pollution." Does the advice collapse? No, advising public transport still makes sense. ❌ II is not implicit.
- **Answer: Only Assumption I is implicit.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** Recite the 4 steps in order, eyes closed. Then write them once on paper.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1 — The "Both Look Possible" Illusion:**
The examiner writes two assumptions that *both* seem related to the statement. Only one passes the Collapse Test; the other is merely *possible*, not *necessary*. Remember: an assumption must be **necessary** for the statement, not just **connected** to it. When in doubt, negate both — the one whose negation kills the statement is your answer.

**Trap 2 — Difficult / Fancy Words to Confuse You:**
Statements dressed in heavy vocabulary ("The municipal corporation has promulgated a directive…") are just simple sentences in disguise. Translate the statement into your own simple words *first*, then hunt for the assumption. Never let a big word scare you into a wrong answer — the logic underneath is always Class-6 level.

**Trap 3 — The Double-Relation Question:**
Some questions hide a chain: Statement → Assumption → another mini-assumption. The examiner offers the *second-level* assumption as an option (e.g., statement about coaching → option about "every student wants a government job"). That deeper layer is usually **remote** (the R in EARS). Pick the assumption *closest* to the statement — the first brick of the foundation, not the basement.

**Trap 4 — Your Real-Life Opinion:**
You may personally disagree with a statement ("Cricketers should advertise paan masala"). Irrelevant! Judge only from the **speaker's** point of view. The moment you bring your own opinion into the exam hall, the examiner wins.
