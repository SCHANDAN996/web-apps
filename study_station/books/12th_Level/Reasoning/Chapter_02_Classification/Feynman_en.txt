# 🧒 The Feynman Way — Classification Explained Like You're 12

## The Toy Box Story

Imagine your little cousin dumps his whole toy box on the floor: cars, a teddy bear, building blocks, a cricket ball, and… one of your school socks (don't ask how it got there).

You say, "Let's clean up! Keep only the toys."

In two seconds, he picks up the sock and throws it out. Why? Because the sock is **not a toy**. It doesn't belong to the group.

**Congratulations — you just solved a Classification question.** That's literally all this chapter is: four or five things are given, they all belong to one family, and one outsider is hiding among them. Your job? **Find the outsider.**

## The Hardest Part (And Why Smart Students Get Trapped)

Finding the odd one out is easy when the family is obvious — like Apple, Mango, Banana, **Potato**. (Potato is a vegetable; the rest are fruits. Done.)

But examiners are sneaky. They give you options where **more than one logic seems to work**. Look at this:

**Rose, Lotus, Marigold, Lily, Jasmine**

- Logic 1: "Lotus grows in water, others on land!" → Answer: Lotus?
- Logic 2: "Rose has thorns, others don't!" → Answer: Rose?
- Logic 3: "Marigold is… hmm."

Now you're stuck, because **three answers feel right**. This is the real monster of Classification.

### The Golden Rule That Slays the Monster

> **The correct logic is the one that applies to the MAXIMUM number of options and is based on a fixed, factual property — not your opinion.**

"Grows in water" is a fact. "Has thorns" is a fact. But the examiner designs the question so that **only one** property cleanly separates exactly one item from all the others in the way the official answer intends — usually the most standard, textbook-level property (like the Lotus being the National Flower and aquatic, while the rest are common land garden flowers).

So when two logics fight, ask: *"Which property is more definite, more factual, and leaves exactly one clear outsider?"* Pick that one. Never pick a logic based on taste ("I like roses") or vague ideas ("marigold smells strong").

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"FAMILY PHOTO"** 📸

Imagine all five options are posing for a **family photo**. Ask each one:

> **"What is your LAST NAME?"**

Four of them will shout the same surname — "Fruit!", "River!", "Odd Number!", "Musical Instrument!" One will mumble something different. **That mumbler is your answer.**

Before marking, do the **Reverse Check**: say the family name out loud and point at each option one by one — "Fruit? Yes. Fruit? Yes. Fruit? Yes. Fruit? **NO — gotcha!**"

If your "family name" fits only 2 out of 5, your logic is wrong. A real family must include **all except one**.

## The Three Families Examiners Love

1. **Word families** — meaning-based (fruits, rivers, planets, professions).
2. **Letter families** — pattern-based (vowel positions, +2/+3 gaps, reverse pairs like AZ, BY).
3. **Number families** — math-based (odd/even, prime, perfect squares, divisibility, digit sums).

The trick is the same for all three: **name the family, find the mumbler, reverse-check.**
