# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Classification (Odd One Out)

## 1. How Exams Ask This Chapter

Classification is one of the most reliable scoring areas in Verbal Reasoning. Across SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS, GD), Banking (IBPS, SBI), and Railway (RRB NTPC, Group D) exams, questions from this chapter appear in almost every reasoning paper, usually in the opening or middle portion of the section. The questions are short, need no calculation, and can be solved in seconds — which is exactly why examiners load them with traps.

The standard formats asked are:

- **Word-based odd one out** — four or five words are given; find the one that does not belong to the group.
- **Number-based odd one out** — find the number that breaks a mathematical property (prime, square, cube, divisibility, digit-sum).
- **Letter-pair / alphabet-based odd one out** — find the pair or group that breaks a positional or gap pattern.
- **Pair-based classification** — four pairs share a relationship (synonym, tool–function, country–capital); one pair does not.

## 2. Sub-topics That Repeat Most Often

Based on the pattern of questions asked over the years, the following sub-topics dominate:

1. **General knowledge words** — animals, birds, fruits, vegetables, rivers, planets, currencies, sports, musical instruments, professions. The trap is usually a near-member (e.g., "Bat" among birds, "Tomato" among vegetables).
2. **Number properties** — prime vs composite, perfect squares/cubes, even/odd, divisibility by a fixed number, digit-sum patterns.
3. **Alphabet positions** — vowels vs consonants, letters with fixed gaps (+2, +3), mirror positions from the end of the alphabet.
4. **Relationship pairs** — synonym/antonym pairs, country–capital, instrument–measurement, animal–young one.
5. **Science-based words** — planets, elements, units of measurement, diseases caused by virus vs bacteria.

## 3. Exam-wise Tendencies

- **SSC CGL / CHSL:** Balanced mix of word, number, and letter classification. Letter-gap and number-property questions are frequent. Difficulty is easy to moderate.
- **SSC MTS / GD:** Mostly word-based general knowledge classification with simple vocabulary. Number questions use only basic properties (even/odd, prime).
- **RRB NTPC / Group D:** Word classification dominates; pairs like country–currency and animal–habitat appear often.
- **Banking (IBPS/SBI Clerk/PO):** Fewer direct questions, but when asked, they favour number patterns and letter-pair logic with a twist.

## 4. Examiner's Traps (Psychological Hooks)

- **The near-member trap:** An option that *looks* like a group member but technically is not — "Whale" among fish (it is a mammal), "Bat" among birds (it is a mammal), "Tomato" among vegetables (botanically a fruit).
- **The double-logic trap:** Two valid groupings seem possible; the examiner intends the *stronger, more specific* property. Always check whether three items share a precise property (e.g., "prime numbers") rather than a vague one (e.g., "odd numbers").
- **The surface-similarity trap:** Words that sound alike or are related in daily life but belong to different categories — "Pen, Pencil, Eraser, Paper" (Paper is written *on*, the rest are writing tools).
- **The digit-sum illusion:** In number questions, three numbers may share an obvious property (all even) while the intended logic is deeper (digit sum, square ±1). Check at least two properties before answering.
- **The alphabet-end trap:** Letter questions sometimes use positions from the *end* of the alphabet (Z=1), not the start. Students who only memorise A=1…Z=26 fall for this.

## 5. Top 10 High-Yield PYQ-Style Questions

**Q1.** Find the odd one out:
(a) Sparrow (b) Pigeon (c) Crow (d) Bat
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Sparrow, Pigeon, and Crow are birds. Bat is a mammal, though it flies — the classic near-member trap.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q2.** Find the odd one out:
(a) 121 (b) 144 (c) 169 (d) 225
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 121 = 11², 144 = 12², 169 = 13² — squares of consecutive numbers. 225 = 15² breaks the sequence (14² = 196 is missing). The trap: all four are perfect squares, so surface logic fails.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q3.** Find the odd one out:
(a) Apple (b) Mango (c) Banana (d) Potato
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Apple, Mango, and Banana are fruits; Potato is a vegetable (a tuber). Easy question — meant to be solved in under 15 seconds.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q4.** Find the odd one out:
(a) 17 (b) 23 (c) 37 (d) 51
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 17, 23, and 37 are prime numbers. 51 = 3 × 17 is composite. Trap: 51 *looks* prime to students who only test divisibility by 2 and 5.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q5.** Find the odd one out:
(a) Mercury (b) Venus (c) Moon (d) Mars
**Answer:** (c)
**Solution:** Mercury, Venus, and Mars are planets; the Moon is a satellite. A frequently repeated GK-based classification.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q6.** Find the odd letter-pair:
(a) CE (b) FH (c) KM (d) QT
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** C(3)→E(5) is +2, F(6)→H(8) is +2, K(11)→M(13) is +2. But Q(17)→T(20) is +3, so QT breaks the uniform +2 gap. Trap: all four pairs "look" like short-gap pairs — always count the exact gap instead of guessing from appearance.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q7.** Find the odd one out:
(a) Rupee (b) Dollar (c) Yen (d) Delhi
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** Rupee, Dollar, and Yen are currencies; Delhi is a city. Skip-level easy — solve within 15 seconds.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q8.** Find the odd one out:
(a) 8 (b) 27 (c) 64 (d) 100
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 8 = 2³, 27 = 3³, 64 = 4³ are perfect cubes; 100 is a perfect square (10²), not a cube. Trap: 64 is both a square (8²) and a cube (4³), which confuses students into doubting the cube logic.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q9.** Find the odd pair:
(a) Doctor : Patient (b) Teacher : Student (c) Lawyer : Client (d) Carpenter : Wood
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** In (a), (b), (c) the relationship is *professional : person served*. In (d), wood is a material, not a person. The surface-similarity trap makes all four look like "profession pairs."
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10.** Find the odd one out:
(a) 63 (b) 80 (c) 99 (d) 122
**Answer:** (d)
**Solution:** 63 = 8²−1, 80 = 9²−1, 99 = 10²−1 — all follow the n²−1 pattern. 122 = 11²+1, which breaks it. Trap: 122 sits right next to 120 (= 11²−1), so a hurried student assumes it fits the pattern. Always verify the exact square and the sign.
**Source:** PYQ-style

## 6. The 15-Second / 45-Second Rule — Review

- **15-second questions:** Word-based GK classification with familiar vocabulary (Q1, Q3, Q5, Q7 above). If the category is obvious, answer instantly and bank the time.
- **45-second questions:** Number and letter-pattern questions (Q2, Q6, Q8, Q10). Give them one focused pass: test prime/square/cube, then digit-sum, then gaps. If nothing clicks in 45 seconds, mark for review and move on — never let one odd-one-out question eat the time of three easy ones.
- **Skip candidates:** Q6 and Q10 above are the type to attempt carefully or skip on the first pass — they hide the logic behind a surface pattern, and forcing them under time pressure invites exactly the trap the examiner set.

## 7. Key Takeaways

- Classification questions are almost always present and almost always solvable — treat them as guaranteed marks.
- Master the checklist: category → prime/square/cube → digit-sum → alphabet position/gap → relationship type.
- Beware the near-member (Bat, Whale, Tomato) and the double-logic trap; the more specific property wins.
- Easy word questions: 15 seconds. Pattern questions: 45 seconds, then skip and return.
