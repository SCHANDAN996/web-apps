# Classification — Finding the Odd One Out

## Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

1. Understand what "Classification" questions really test.
2. Identify the 6 major types of classification questions asked in SSC, Banking, Railway and State exams.
3. Apply a 3-step method to solve any odd-one-out question in under 30 seconds.
4. Spot and escape the examiner's favourite traps.
5. Build the habit of checking *why* three items belong together — not just why one is different.

---

## 1. The Hook: Four Friends, One Spy

Imagine four friends: **Apple, Mango, Banana, Potato**.

Three of them meet every Sunday. One of them is an imposter — a spy who doesn't belong to the group. Who is it?

You said **Potato** in less than two seconds, didn't you? Why? Because three are *fruits* and one is a *vegetable*.

**Congratulations — you just solved a Classification question.** That is all this chapter is: a group of items where three (or four) share a common property, and one breaks the rule. Your job is to catch the spy.

SSC, Banking and Railway exams ask these questions regularly because they test something precious: **can you find the hidden rule?** And the beautiful part? You already have this skill. We just need to sharpen it.

---

## 2. The Common Struggle (You Are Not Alone)

Most students lose marks here for one simple reason: **they find a difference, but not the *right* difference.**

Look at this: **Dog, Cat, Cow, Snake**

- Student A says: "Snake — because it has no legs." ✔️
- Student B says: "Cow — because it gives milk." ✔️
- Student C says: "Dog — because it guards the house." ✔️

All three found *a* difference! But the examiner wants the difference based on the **strongest, most general category**. Here, Dog, Cat and Cow are all **domestic mammals**, while Snake is a **reptile**. The biological class is the deepest rule, so Snake is the intended answer.

> **Golden Lesson:** In classification, many differences exist. The examiner's answer is based on the **most standard, category-level difference** — usually the one you learned in school (science, geography, grammar).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what is the one golden rule about "which difference" to choose? Say it aloud before reading on.

## 3. The 6 Types of Classification Questions

### Type 1: Word Classification (Odd Word Out)

Four/five words are given; one doesn't belong to the group.

**Example:** Rose, Lotus, Marigold, Mango
- Rose, Lotus, Marigold → **flowers**
- Mango → **fruit** ✅ Answer

The categories come from school-level knowledge:
- **Biology:** fruits/vegetables, mammals/reptiles/birds, herbs/shrubs/trees
- **Geography:** rivers/cities/states/countries, continents, oceans
- **Science:** metals/non-metals, planets, units of measurement
- **Language:** synonyms, parts of speech
- **Everyday life:** sports, musical instruments, professions, vehicles

### Type 2: Number Classification (Odd Number Out)

**Example:** 16, 25, 36, 50
- 16 = 4², 25 = 5², 36 = 6² → **perfect squares**
- 50 is not a perfect square ✅ Answer

Common number rules to check **in this order**:
1. Even / Odd
2. Prime / Composite
3. Perfect square / Perfect cube
4. Divisibility (by 3, 5, 7, 11…)
5. Sum of digits
6. Digits in ascending/descending order
7. Relationships like n²+1, n²−1, ×2+3

### Type 3: Letter Classification (Odd Letter Group Out)

**Example:** ACE, BDF, HJL, MNP
- ACE: A(1) +2 → C(3) +2 → E(5) — gap of 2 ✔️
- BDF: B(2) +2 → D(4) +2 → F(6) — gap of 2 ✔️
- HJL: H(8) +2 → J(10) +2 → L(12) — gap of 2 ✔️
- MNP: M(13) +1 → N(14) +1 → O… no, N(14) +1 → P? N+2 = P. So MNP has gaps of **1 and 2** — it breaks the rule ✅ **Answer: MNP**

Notice the clean **3 vs 1 split**: three groups follow "+2, +2", one doesn't. That is what a valid classification question looks like.

Always convert letters to positions: **A=1, B=2 … Z=26.** Then check gaps, vowels, and symmetry.

### Type 4: Pair Classification (Odd Pair Out)

**Example:** (Doctor : Patient), (Teacher : Student), (Lawyer : Client), (Farmer : Plough)
- First three pairs: **professional : person served**
- Farmer : Plough → person : tool ✅ Answer

### Type 5: Number-Pair Classification

**Example:** (8, 64), (3, 9), (5, 25), (7, 48)
- 8→64 (square), 3→9 (square), 5→25 (square), 7→48 ✗ ✅ Answer: (7, 48)

### Type 6: Image/Figure Classification

Same logic with pictures — three figures share a property (number of sides, shading, rotation), one doesn't. The verbal method stays identical.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Close the book. Name all 6 types. Which type needs alphabet positions? Which type checks "sum of digits"?

## 4. The 3-Step Method (Your 30-Second Weapon)

**Step 1 — OBSERVE (5 seconds):** Glance at all options. Are they words, numbers, letters, or pairs?

**Step 2 — HUNT THE RULE (15 seconds):** Ask: "What do *three* of them share?" Run through the checklist for that type (for numbers: even/odd → prime → square → divisibility → digit-sum).

**Step 3 — VERIFY (10 seconds):** Confirm the rule fits **exactly three** items and fails for **exactly one**. If your rule splits them 2–2, your rule is wrong — hunt again.

> ⚠️ Never finalize an answer where your rule makes two items odd. A valid classification rule always gives a **3 vs 1** split.

🧠 **Active Recall:** What are the three steps? What split must a valid rule produce?

## 5. Mnemonic Trick: **"SPEND"** for Number Questions

When numbers confuse you, **SPEND** a few seconds checking:

- **S** — Square / cube?
- **P** — Prime?
- **E** — Even / odd?
- **N** — Number divisibility (3, 5, 7, 11)?
- **D** — Digit sum / digit order?

Remember: *"Don't just look at numbers — SPEND time on them!"*

For words, use **"FROGS"**: **F**ood, **R**ealm (science/geography), **O**ccupation, **G**roup (animals/sports), **S**ound (synonyms/grammar).

## 6. Worked Examples (Think Along!)

**Example 1:** 121, 144, 169, 180
- SPEND: 121=11², 144=12², 169=13² → squares! 180 is not. ✅ **Answer: 180**

**Example 2:** Guitar, Sitar, Flute, Tabla
- All musical instruments… deeper: Guitar and Sitar are string instruments, Flute is a wind instrument — all three produce *melody*; Tabla is a **percussion** (struck) instrument used for *rhythm*. ✅ **Answer: Tabla**

**Example 3:** BD, FH, JL, NP — find the odd one out.
- Internal gaps: BD (B+2=D), FH (F+2=H), JL (J+2=L), NP (N+2=P) — all +2. First test passes for all four!
- Now check the starting letters: B=2, F=6, J=10, N=14 — a sequence with gaps of 4. All four fit, so this set has no odd one.
- But if one option were **KM**: K+2=M ✔️ (internal gap fine), yet K=11 breaks the +4 starting sequence (10 → 11 instead of 10 → 14). Then **KM** would be the odd one.
- **Lesson: check BOTH the internal gap AND the sequence of starting letters.**

🧠 **Active Recall:** In letter pairs, which TWO things must you check?

## 7. Summary Table

| Type | What You Check | Quick Tool |
|---|---|---|
| Odd Word | Category (science, geography, daily life) | FROGS |
| Odd Number | Square, prime, even/odd, divisibility, digits | SPEND |
| Odd Letters | Alphabet positions, gaps, vowels | A=1…Z=26 |
| Odd Pair | Relationship between the two items | "What connects them?" |
| Odd Number-Pair | Mathematical operation in the pair | ×, ², +, ÷ |
| Odd Figure | Sides, shading, rotation, elements | Same 3-step method |

**The Universal Law:** A correct rule always separates the group into **3 vs 1** — never 2 vs 2.

## ⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

**Trap 1: Multiple Possible Relationships**
The examiner deliberately designs options where *two* differences seem valid. Example: "Apple, Banana, Grape, Cauliflower" — you might pick Grape (grows on vines), but the intended answer is Cauliflower (vegetable vs fruits). **Escape:** Always prefer the broadest school-level category (fruit/vegetable, mammal/reptile, metal/non-metal) over a clever, narrow property.

**Trap 2: Difficult/Unfamiliar Words to Confuse You**
Words like "Thermometer, Barometer, Ammeter, Odometer" look scary. Students panic and guess. **Escape:** Difficult words usually share a *simple* rule — break each word down and ask what it *measures* (temperature, atmospheric pressure, electric current, distance travelled). Never skip a question out of fear of long words.

**Trap 3: Double-Relationship Questions**
Some questions have a rule inside a rule — e.g., letter groups where the *internal gap* is the same for all, but the *starting-letter sequence* eliminates one. **Escape:** If all options pass your first test, don't celebrate — apply a second layer of checking (positions, digit sums, sub-categories). The examiner hides the real answer one level deeper.
