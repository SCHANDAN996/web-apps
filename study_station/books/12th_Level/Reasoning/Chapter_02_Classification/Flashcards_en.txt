Card 1
Front: In a classification question, what is the very first thing you should check before comparing options?
Back: The direction of the question — "find the odd one out" vs "find the one that belongs". Read twice; most silly errors happen here.

Card 2
Front: What are the four main types of classification asked in SSC/Bank/Railway exams?
Back: (1) Word classification, (2) Number classification, (3) Letter/Alphabet classification, (4) Pair classification.

Card 3
Front: Three options are 121, 144, 169 and the fourth is 132. What property makes 132 the odd one out?
Back: 121, 144, 169 are perfect squares (11², 12², 13²); 132 is not a perfect square.

Card 4
Front: In letter classification, which two checks solve most questions instantly?
Back: (1) Position of letters in the alphabet (A=1…Z=26), (2) Vowel/consonant pattern and the gap (difference) between letters.

Card 5
Front: Mnemonic trick: How do you quickly recall the positions of letters like E, J, O, T, Y?
Back: Use "EJOTY" — E=5, J=10, O=15, T=20, Y=25. Multiples of 5, easy anchor points for any letter position.

Card 6
Front: Mnemonic trick: What is the reverse-position shortcut for letters?
Back: Reverse position = 27 − normal position. Example: C is 3rd, so from the end it is 27−3 = 24th. Sum of a letter and its opposite is always 27 (A+Z, B+Y, C+X…).

Card 7
Front: Options: Apple, Mango, Banana, Potato. Why is Potato the odd one out — and what trap is hidden here?
Back: Potato is a vegetable (grows underground); the rest are fruits. Trap: don't classify by colour or taste — classify by the standard category (fruit vs vegetable).

Card 8
Front: Examiner's trap: All four options are prime numbers. Can the question still have an odd one out?
Back: Yes. Look deeper — e.g., 2 is the only even prime, or three are two-digit primes and one is single-digit. Never stop at the first common property.

Card 9
Front: In number classification, list the five properties you should test, in order.
Back: (1) Prime/composite, (2) Odd/even, (3) Perfect square/cube, (4) Divisibility, (5) Digit-sum or digit pattern.

Card 10
Front: Options: 8, 27, 64, 100. Which is odd and why?
Back: 100 — the others are perfect cubes (2³, 3³, 4³); 100 is a perfect square, not a cube.

Card 11
Front: In pair classification like (3, 9), (4, 16), (5, 25), (6, 34), what is the rule and the odd pair?
Back: Rule: second number = square of the first. (6, 34) is odd because 6² = 36, not 34.

Card 12
Front: Examiner's trap: Options are Rose, Lotus, Marigold, Mango. A student picks Lotus as "national flower". Is that the right logic?
Back: No. Classification needs a property shared by exactly three. Rose, Lotus, Marigold are flowers; Mango is a fruit/tree — Mango is the odd one out. "National" status applies to only one item, so it can't form a group of three.

Card 13
Front: What is the difference between "odd one out" and "analogy-based classification"?
Back: Odd one out: find the item that doesn't share the group's property. Analogy-based: first identify the relation in a given pair, then find which option follows (or breaks) the same relation.

Card 14
Front: Options: 17, 23, 37, 51. Which is the odd one out?
Back: 51 — it is composite (3 × 17); the others are prime numbers.

Card 15
Front: In word classification, name four common category bases examiners use.
Back: (1) Category/type (fruit–vegetable, river–mountain), (2) Function (tools, instruments), (3) Gender or living/non-living, (4) Part–whole or size/degree relations.

Card 16
Front: Options: BD, FH, JL, NP — all have a gap of +2. If one option were JN, why would it be odd?
Back: J(10)→N(14) is a gap of +4, while B→D, F→H, J→L, N→P are all +2. Always compute the alphabet gap, don't judge by appearance.

Card 17
Front: What should you do when two options both seem "odd" for different reasons?
Back: Choose the property that groups exactly three items together under one clear, standard rule. The strongest answer is the one where three share a property and only one lacks it.

Card 18
Front: Time rule for classification questions in the exam hall?
Back: 15–20 seconds per question. If no property clicks within 20 seconds, mark and skip — return later; classification questions are quick marks, not worth a long fight.
