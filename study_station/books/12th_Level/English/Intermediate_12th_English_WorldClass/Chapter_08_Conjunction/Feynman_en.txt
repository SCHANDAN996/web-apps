# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Conjunctions Explained Like You're 12

## The Big Idea: Conjunctions Are Just "Glue Words"

Imagine you have two toy trains. Each train is a complete sentence — it has an engine (subject) and coaches (verb and object). Now, if you want to join two trains into one long train, what do you need? A **coupler** — that small hook between two coaches.

A **conjunction is exactly that coupler**. It joins two words, two phrases, or two complete sentences so they travel together as one.

- "Ravi is tired." + "Ravi is working." → "Ravi is tired **but** he is working."
- The word **but** is the coupler. Without it, the two sentences crash into each other.

## The Three Types of Couplers (The Hardest Part, Made Easy)

**1. Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS)** — These join two *equal* trains. Both sides can stand alone.
Think of two friends shaking hands — neither is the boss.
*For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.*

**2. Subordinating conjunctions** — These join a *boss* train to a *servant* train. The servant part cannot stand alone.
"**Because** it rained, the match stopped."
Try saying only "Because it rained." — Sounds incomplete, right? That's the servant clause. It needs its boss ("the match stopped").

**3. Correlative conjunctions** — These are **twin couplers**. They always come in pairs, like a pair of shoes. You never wear just one.
*Either…or, Neither…nor, Not only…but also, Both…and.*
"**Neither** Ravi **nor** his brother came." — Both twins must appear.

## The Daily-Life Example You'll Never Forget

Think of your school assembly line:

- **Coordinating** = two students standing side by side, equal height. (Ram **and** Shyam)
- **Subordinating** = a student standing *behind* the class monitor — the monitor leads, the student depends. (**Although** he is short, he plays well.)
- **Correlative** = twins holding hands — if one twin is missing, everyone notices. (**Either** you study **or** you fail.)

## The Unforgettable Trick 🎯

**"If you can delete it and both halves still make full sense — it's coordinating. If one half sounds like a broken sentence — it's subordinating."**

Test it:
- "He was ill, **so** he stayed home." → Delete *so*: "He was ill." ✔ "He stayed home." ✔ → Coordinating!
- "**Although** he was ill, he came." → Delete *although*: "He was ill" ✔, but "Although he was ill" alone ✘ → Subordinating!

## The One Mistake Everyone Makes

Twins must hold the **same kind of hands**. After "not only…but also," both sides must carry the same grammar:
- ❌ "He not only plays cricket but also football." (plays cricket ≠ football)
- ✔ "He plays not only cricket but also football." (cricket = football — same kind!)
