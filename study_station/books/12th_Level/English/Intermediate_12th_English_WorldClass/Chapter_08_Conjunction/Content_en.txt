# Conjunction — The Glue of English Sentences

## 🔗 Why This Chapter Matters

Imagine two railway coaches without a coupling — each coach is fine on its own, but the train cannot move. Sentences are the same. *"He worked hard. He failed."* is two lonely coaches. *"He worked hard, **yet** he failed."* is a train — and that tiny word **yet** is exactly what SSC CHSL, RRB NTPC and Bank exams test, again and again. Master conjunctions and you unlock error-spotting, sentence improvement, fill-in-the-blanks and cloze tests — four question types from one chapter.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Define a conjunction and identify it in any sentence.
2. Distinguish the **three types**: Coordinating, Subordinating and Correlative.
3. Apply the **12 Golden Rules** (pairs like *either…or*, *no sooner…than*, *hardly…when*).
4. Spot the classic traps examiners set around *until/unless*, *since/because*, and *though/but*.
5. Solve a conjunction question in under 15 seconds using pair-matching.

## 1. What Is a Conjunction?

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words, phrases or clauses.

- Words: *tea **and** coffee*
- Phrases: *in the morning **or** in the evening*
- Clauses: *She sang **while** he played the flute.*

Think of a conjunction as a **bridge**. It never carries meaning alone; its whole job is to connect two banks of meaning and show the *relationship* between them — addition, contrast, choice, reason, time or condition.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what three things can a conjunction join? Give one example of each.

## 2. Type 1 — Coordinating Conjunctions (The FANBOYS)

Coordinating conjunctions join **equal** elements — two words, two phrases, or two independent clauses. Remember them with the mnemonic:

> **FANBOYS** = **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o

| Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| For | reason | He stayed home, **for** he was ill. |
| And | addition | Ram **and** Shyam came. |
| Nor | negative addition | He neither called **nor** wrote. |
| But | contrast | She is poor **but** honest. |
| Or | choice | Run fast, **or** you will miss the train. |
| Yet | contrast (surprise) | He is rich, **yet** unhappy. |
| So | result | It rained, **so** the match was cancelled. |

⚠️ *For* as a conjunction is formal and means "because" — do not confuse it with the preposition *for* ("a gift **for** you").

🧠 **Active Recall:** Recite FANBOYS. Which two FANBOYS show contrast? Which one shows result?

## 3. Type 2 — Subordinating Conjunctions (The Dependent-Makers)

A subordinating conjunction joins a **dependent (subordinate) clause** to an **independent (main) clause**. The dependent clause cannot stand alone.

- ***Because** it rained*, the match was cancelled. ✅
- *Because it rained.* ❌ (incomplete — leaves the listener waiting)

**Group them by job:**

| Job | Conjunctions |
|---|---|
| Time | when, while, before, after, until, since, as soon as, no sooner…than, hardly…when |
| Reason | because, since, as |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, in case |
| Contrast/Concession | though, although, even though, whereas, while |
| Purpose | so that, in order that, lest |
| Comparison | than, as…as |

**Key behaviour:** if the subordinate clause comes **first**, use a comma: *"**Although** he is rich, he is humble."* If it comes **second**, usually no comma: *"He is humble **although** he is rich."*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Turn this into one sentence using *unless*: "Work hard. You will fail otherwise." (Answer: *Unless you work hard, you will fail.*)

## 4. Type 3 — Correlative Conjunctions (The Fixed Pairs)

These work **in pairs**, and exams attack the pairing mercilessly. Memorise the pairs as one unit:

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| either … or | **Either** come in, **or** go out. |
| neither … nor | **Neither** Ram **nor** Shyam came. |
| not only … but also | She is **not only** wise **but also** kind. |
| both … and | **Both** his father **and** his mother are doctors. |
| whether … or | I don't know **whether** he will come **or** not. |
| no sooner … than | **No sooner** did she arrive **than** it rained. |
| hardly/scarcely … when | **Hardly** had he slept **when** the phone rang. |
| such … that | It was **such** a hot day **that** we stayed inside. |
| the more … the more | **The more** you read, **the more** you learn. |

**Parallelism rule:** whatever follows the first half of the pair must grammatically match what follows the second half.
- ❌ *He not only plays cricket but also football.*
- ✅ *He plays not only cricket but also football.* (both halves followed by a noun)

🧠 **Active Recall:** Fill the blanks: *No sooner ___ the bell ring ___ the students rushed out.* (did … than)

## 5. The 12 Golden Rules of Conjunctions ⭐

1. **Either…or / Neither…nor** — never mix them (*either…nor* ❌).
2. **No sooner takes *than***; **hardly/scarcely take *when*** — never swap.
3. **Although/Though** never pairs with **but** — use *yet* or nothing: *Though he is ill, (yet) he works.* ❌ *Though he is ill, but he works.*
4. **Because/Since/As** never pairs with **therefore/so**: *Because he was ill, he stayed home.* ❌ *Because he was ill, so he stayed home.*
5. **Unless** = "if…not" — it is already negative, so never add *not*: ❌ *Unless you do not work…* ✅ *Unless you work…*
6. **Until** refers to **time**; **unless** refers to **condition**: *Wait **until** I return.* / *Don't go **unless** I call you.*
7. **Since** (reason) vs **because**: *since* is fine at the start of a sentence; *because* answers "why?" directly.
8. **Lest** is followed by **should** (or the bare verb), never by *not*: *Walk fast **lest** you **should** miss the train.*
9. **Both…and** cannot take a negative verb: ❌ *Both Ram and Shyam did not go.* ✅ *Neither Ram nor Shyam went.*
10. **As well as / along with / together with** are **not** true conjunctions — the verb agrees with the **first** subject: *Ram, **as well as** his brothers, **is** going.*
11. **Rather than / sooner than** are followed by the **bare infinitive**: *I would die **rather than beg**.*
12. **The reason…is that**, not *the reason…is because*: ✅ *The reason he failed is **that** he didn't study.*

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Double Conjunction:** Examiners love *"Though he is poor, but he is honest"* and *"Because he was late, so he missed the bus."* Your eye must scream: **two conjunctions for one join = wrong.** Delete one.

**Trap 2 — The Broken Pair:** *"No sooner had he entered **when** the lights went off."* Sounds smooth, reads wrong — *no sooner* demands **than**. Say the pair aloud as one word: *nosooner…than*, *hardly…when*.

**Trap 3 — Unless + Not:** *"Unless you do not apologise, I will not forgive you."* Double negative = the sentence now means the opposite of what was intended. **Unless already contains the "not".**

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi से English

| हिंदी में हम सोचते हैं | गलत English | सही English |
|---|---|---|
| "यद्यपि वह गरीब है, **परंतु** वह ईमानदार है" (दोनों शब्द स्वाभाविक लगते हैं) | Though he is poor, **but** he is honest. | Though he is poor, he is honest. |
| "क्योंकि वह बीमार था, **इसलिए** वह नहीं आया" | Because he was ill, **so** he didn't come. | Because he was ill, he didn't come. |
| "जब तक तुम मेहनत **नहीं** करोगे…" (unless को *not* चाहिए लगता है) | Unless you do **not** work hard… | Unless you work hard… |
| "न तो राम **और न** श्याम" | Neither Ram **or** Shyam | Neither Ram **nor** Shyam |

Hindi happily uses double connectors (यद्यपि…परंतु, क्योंकि…इसलिए); English allows only **one** connector per join. When in doubt, count your connectors.

## 6. Mnemonic to Lock the Pairs 🔑

> **"No Sooner THAN, Hardly WHEN — E-N pairs, E-O friends."**
> *No sooner* marries *than*; *hardly/scarcely* marry *when*; **N**either marries **N**or (N-N); **E**ither marries **O**r (E-O).
