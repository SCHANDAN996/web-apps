# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Pronoun Explained to a 12-Year-Old

## The Hardest Concept: Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement (and the "Everyone" Trap)

Imagine your classroom. The teacher says:

> "**Everyone** should bring **their** notebook tomorrow."

Sounds fine, right? Almost everyone in India says this. But in a competitive exam, this sentence is **wrong**. Here's why, in the simplest way possible.

### Think of pronouns as "name-tags"

A pronoun is just a name-tag you stick on a noun so you don't repeat the name. If the name-tag says **he**, it must be stuck on **one boy**. If it says **they**, it must be stuck on **many people**. The tag and the person must match — always.

Now, words like **everyone, everybody, someone, each, nobody** are tricksters. They *feel* like a crowd ("everyone" = all the students!), but grammatically each one is a **single person standing alone**. The word itself tells you: **every + ONE**, **some + ONE**, **no + BODY** (one body).

So the rule is:

> **ONE-words are always singular → they take he/she/his/her, never "they/their" (in exam grammar).**

✅ Everyone should bring **his** notebook. (exam-correct)
❌ Everyone should bring **their** notebook. (daily speech, but exam-wrong)

### The Daily-Life Example You'll Never Forget

Think of a school assembly line. The principal announces: "**Each of the students** must wear **his** ID card."

Even if there are 500 students in the line, "each" picks them up **one by one**, like a teacher checking copies one at a time. One student = one ID card = **his/her**, never "their."

### The Unforgettable Trick: 🔑 "ONE BODY, ONE TAG"

Whenever you see **every-, some-, any-, no- + one/body**, or **each / either / neither**, shout in your head:

> **"ONE BODY, ONE TAG!"** → singular verb + singular pronoun (is, was, has, he, she, his, her).

- Each of the girls **has** done **her** homework. ✅
- Neither of the boys **has** brought **his** book. ✅
- Everybody **is** present and has brought **his** admit card. ✅

### Bonus Trap: The Intervening Phrase

Examiners hide the real subject behind a long phrase:

> "The quality of the mangoes **was** good." (not *were* — the subject is *quality*, not *mangoes*)

Same trick with pronouns: "One of my friends has lost **his** wallet." — *friends* is a distraction; the subject is **One**.
