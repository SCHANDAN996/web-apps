# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Idioms & Phrases

## 1. How Exams Test This Chapter (Pattern Analysis)

Idioms & Phrases is one of the most **predictable** vocabulary areas in SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS, GD), RRB (NTPC, Group D), Banking (IBPS, SBI) and State exams. The pattern has stayed remarkably stable over the years:

**What examiners repeatedly ask:**

- **Direct meaning questions** — "What is the meaning of *to burn the midnight oil*?" This is the single most common format. Four options are given; three are literal or near-miss meanings, one is the figurative meaning.
- **Reverse questions** — the meaning is given in the question, and you must pick the correct idiom: "To work hard late into the night" → (a) burn the midnight oil.
- **Sentence-based usage** — the idiom is underlined in a sentence and you choose its meaning *from context*. Slightly harder, because the sentence can mislead you toward the literal meaning.
- **One idiom, many exams** — the same core set of idioms keeps rotating across SSC, Railway and Bank papers. Classics like *a blessing in disguise, once in a blue moon, to let the cat out of the bag, at the drop of a hat, to bite the dust, a red herring, to hit the nail on the head* appear again and again in different years and shifts.

**Sub-topics that recur most:**

1. Colour idioms (*red tape, blue blood, green with envy, white lie, black sheep*)
2. Body-part idioms (*to have a big heart, to turn a blind eye, by word of mouth, to wash one's hands of*)
3. Animal idioms (*to kill two birds with one stone, a wild goose chase, to rain cats and dogs*)
4. Death/difficulty idioms (*to bite the dust, to kick the bucket, in deep waters, a hard nut to crack*)
5. Money/success idioms (*to make both ends meet, to bring home the bacon, a feather in one's cap*)

**The examiner's favourite traps (in words, not numbers):**

- **Literal-meaning trap:** the wrong option translates the idiom word by word. *To kick the bucket* ≠ "to hit a bucket"; it means "to die". This is the most common style of wrong option in this chapter.
- **Near-synonym trap:** two options look almost correct; only one matches the *exact* figurative sense. *A wild goose chase* = a useless/hopeless search, not merely "a long journey".
- **Sound-right trap:** an option that "sounds like good English" but is not the idiom's accepted meaning. *To read between the lines* = to find the hidden meaning, not "to read carefully".
- **Similar-idiom confusion:** pairs like *at sixes and sevens* (in disorder) vs *on cloud nine* (very happy); *a bed of roses* (easy situation) vs *a bed of thorns* (full of difficulties). Examiners love swapping these.

**Weightage (qualitative):** In SSC and Banking English sections, idioms appear as a small but dependable cluster of questions — you can expect at least a couple in almost every paper. Because the question bank is finite and repetitive, this is a **high return-on-effort** area: mastering the core set of frequently asked idioms covers the overwhelming majority of what is asked.

## 2. Top 10 High-Yield Questions (with Solutions & Trap Classification)

**Q1.** What is the meaning of the idiom **"to burn the midnight oil"**?
(a) To waste time at night
(b) To work or study late into the night
(c) To light lamps in the evening
(d) To stay awake due to worry

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom comes from the days of oil lamps — working past midnight meant burning lamp oil. It means to work/study late. Option (c) is the **literal-meaning trap**; (d) is a **sound-right trap** (staying awake ≠ working).
**Trap type:** Literal-meaning | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style (recurs across SSC exams)

---

**Q2.** **"A blessing in disguise"** means:
(a) A hidden curse
(b) A good thing that seemed bad at first
(c) A religious blessing
(d) A gift from a stranger

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "Disguise" = hidden appearance. Something that looks unfortunate but turns out beneficial. Option (a) reverses the meaning — a classic **near-synonym/opposite trap**.
**Trap type:** Near-synonym | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q3.** Choose the correct meaning of **"to let the cat out of the bag"**:
(a) To free a trapped animal
(b) To reveal a secret, usually by mistake
(c) To create chaos
(d) To start a quarrel

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to disclose a secret unintentionally. Option (a) is the **literal trap**; (c) and (d) are **sound-right traps** — chaos may *result* from the revelation, but that is not the idiom's meaning.
**Trap type:** Literal-meaning | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q4.** **"Once in a blue moon"** means:
(a) Very rarely
(b) Every month
(c) On a full-moon night
(d) During a festival

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** A "blue moon" (second full moon in a calendar month) is rare, so the idiom means "very rarely". Option (c) is the **literal trap**.
**Trap type:** Literal-meaning | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q5.** The manager's speech was **"a red herring"**. This means it was:
(a) Very inspiring
(b) A misleading distraction from the real issue
(c) Full of anger
(d) Extremely boring

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** A red herring is something that diverts attention from the main issue. Option (c) is a **sound-right trap** — "red" suggests anger, but the idiom has nothing to do with anger.
**Trap type:** Sound-right | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q6.** **"To wash one's hands of something"** means:
(a) To clean up after work
(b) To refuse to take any further responsibility for it
(c) To complete a task successfully
(d) To forgive someone

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom (from Pontius Pilate) means to abandon responsibility. Option (a) is the **literal trap**; (c) is the **near-synonym trap** — finishing a task is not the same as disowning it.
**Trap type:** Literal + Near-synonym | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q7.** **"At sixes and sevens"** means:
(a) In a state of confusion or disorder
(b) Very punctual
(c) Extremely lucky
(d) Divided equally

**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** The idiom means in disorder/confusion. Do not confuse with *on cloud nine* (very happy) — a favourite **similar-idiom confusion** pair. Option (d) is a **sound-right trap** built on the numbers.
**Trap type:** Similar-idiom confusion | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q8.** **"To hit the nail on the head"** means:
(a) To hurt someone badly
(b) To say or do exactly the right thing
(c) To work as a carpenter
(d) To make a small mistake

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** Striking a nail squarely = being exactly accurate. Option (a) is the **literal trap**; (d) is the **opposite trap** — the idiom means precision, not error.
**Trap type:** Literal-meaning | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q9.** He lost his job and now finds it hard **"to make both ends meet"**. The underlined phrase means:
(a) To connect two wires
(b) To manage expenses within one's income
(c) To meet old friends
(d) To work two jobs

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "Ends" = income and expenditure. The idiom means to live within one's earnings. Option (d) is the **over-thinking trap** — working two jobs might be *a way* to make ends meet, but it is not the meaning. Option (a) is the **literal trap**.
**Trap type:** Over-thinking + Literal | **Time rule:** Sentence gives context, but still Fact-based → 15–20 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

---

**Q10.** **"To bite the dust"** means:
(a) To eat in a hurry
(b) To suffer defeat, failure or death
(c) To clean a dirty room
(d) To behave rudely

**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** The idiom means to fail, be defeated, or die. Option (a) is the **literal trap**; (d) is a **sound-right trap** with no connection to the idiom.
**Trap type:** Literal-meaning | **Time rule:** Fact-based → 15 sec
**Source:** PYQ-style

## 3. Applying the 15/45-Second Rule

**15-second questions (Fact-based recall):** Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q6, Q7, Q8, Q10 — the idiom is either known or not. If you have revised the core idioms, read → recognise → mark → move. **Never spend more than 20 seconds** re-reading options; the literal trap is designed to make you hesitate.

**Worked example (Q1):** See "burn the midnight oil" → memory hook "oil lamp = late-night study" → mark (b) in under 15 seconds. Do not pause on (c) — it is the literal trap.

**45-second questions (Inference/context-based):** Q5 and Q9 — the idiom sits inside a sentence. Here you may spend up to 45 seconds: (1) read the sentence, (2) ignore the underlined idiom and ask "what is the sentence saying about the person/situation?", (3) match that sense to the options, (4) eliminate the literal option first.

**Worked example (Q9):** Sentence says "lost his job… finds it hard" → the situation is financial difficulty → only (b) matches financial management → mark (b). Total time: ~30–40 seconds.

**Key takeaway:** In this chapter, the great majority of questions are pure recall. Your speed comes from preparation, not from thinking in the exam hall. If an idiom is completely unknown after 20 seconds, eliminate the literal option, make your best guess among the remaining figurative ones, and move on — never leave it blank if there is no negative marking, and never burn 2 minutes on it.
