# Idioms & Phrases

## 🎯 Why This Chapter Matters

Imagine two candidates in SSC CHSL. Both know grammar. But the paper asks: *"He let the cat out of the bag."* One candidate freezes — "Which cat? Which bag?" The other smiles, ticks **"revealed a secret"** in 10 seconds, and moves on. That 10-second answer is the difference between a comfortable attempt and a rushed one.

Idioms are **fixed expressions whose meaning cannot be guessed from the individual words**. "Kick the bucket" has nothing to do with a bucket — it means *to die*. Exams like SSC CGL, CHSL, RRB NTPC and IBPS regularly include idiom-based questions because they test whether you have *read English*, not just *studied* it.

## 📌 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Recognise the most exam-repeated idioms and their exact meanings.
2. Distinguish an **idiom** (fixed figurative phrase) from a **phrasal verb** and a **proverb**.
3. Use word-origin clues (Greek/Latin roots) to guess unfamiliar expressions.
4. Avoid the 3 classic traps examiners set in idiom questions.
5. Answer fact-based idiom questions in under 15 seconds.

## 1️⃣ What Exactly Is an Idiom?

An **idiom** is a group of words whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of its parts. It is a *fixed* unit — you cannot change its words.

- ✅ **Break the ice** = to start a conversation / remove initial awkwardness.
- ❌ You cannot say "break the snow" or "crack the ice" — the idiom dies.

A **phrase** in this chapter means any set expression tested in exams, including **phrasal verbs** (verb + preposition/adverb, e.g., *give up* = quit) and **proverbs** (complete wise sayings, e.g., *A stitch in time saves nine*).

| Type | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Idiom | To burn the midnight oil | To study/work late into the night |
| Phrasal verb | To put off | To postpone |
| Proverb | Don't count your chickens before they hatch | Don't assume success before it happens |

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — (1) What does "let the cat out of the bag" mean? (2) Why can't we say "crack the ice"? (3) Give one example each of an idiom and a phrasal verb.

## 2️⃣ The High-Frequency Idiom Bank (Learn These First)

These idioms appear again and again across SSC, Railway and Banking papers. Group them by theme — the brain remembers *stories*, not lists.

**🟢 Success & Effort**
- **Hit the nail on the head** — say exactly the right thing.
- **Burn the midnight oil** — work/study late at night.
- **Leave no stone unturned** — try every possible method.
- **Rome wasn't built in a day** — great work takes time.

**🔴 Difficulty & Failure**
- **A hard nut to crack** — a difficult problem or person.
- **To face the music** — to accept punishment/criticism.
- **To go to the dogs** — to be ruined.
- **A wild goose chase** — a useless search.

**🟡 Secrecy & Speech**
- **To spill the beans** — to reveal a secret.
- **A red herring** — something that misleads/distracts.
- **To beat about the bush** — to talk indirectly, avoiding the main point.
- **To take with a grain of salt** — to not fully believe something.

**🔵 People & Behaviour**
- **A dark horse** — a person with hidden talents.
- **A wet blanket** — a person who discourages others.
- **A man of straw** — a person of no real substance.
- **To blow one's own trumpet** — to praise oneself.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Cover the right column. What do these mean: *a red herring, a dark horse, to face the music, a wild goose chase*? Which idiom fits: "The minister kept talking around the issue"? *(beat about the bush)*

## 3️⃣ 🧩 Mnemonic: The "CINEMA" Trick for Confusing Pairs

Examiners love idioms that *sound similar* but mean different things. Remember **CINEMA**:

- **C** — **C**all a spade a spade (speak frankly) ≠ **C**all it a day (stop working)
- **I** — **I**n high spirits (happy) ≠ **I**n black and white (in writing)
- **N** — **N**eck and neck (equal in a race) ≠ **N**ip in the bud (stop at the start)
- **E** — **E**at humble pie (apologise humbly) ≠ **E**at one's words (take back what one said)
- **M** — **M**ake both ends meet (live within income) ≠ **M**ake a mountain of a molehill (exaggerate)
- **A** — **A**t sixes and sevens (in disorder) ≠ **A**t the drop of a hat (instantly)

## 4️⃣ Greek & Latin Roots: Your Idiom-Guessing Toolkit

Many idioms and formal phrases in exams come from Latin or contain root words. Knowing roots lets you *decode* unfamiliar options.

| Root | Meaning | Derived Words |
|---|---|---|
| **bene** (L) | good | benefit, benevolent, benediction |
| **mal** (L) | bad | malice, malfunction, malnutrition |
| **dict** (L) | say | dictate, predict, verdict |
| **port** (L) | carry | transport, export, portable |
| **cred** (L) | believe | credible, incredible, credit |
| **vid/vis** (L) | see | visible, vision, evidence |
| **aqua** (L) | water | aquarium, aquatic, aqueduct |
| **chron** (G) | time | chronic, chronology, synchronize |

**📖 Mini Reading Passage (spot the root words):**

> *When the new transport policy was announced, critics called it a **red herring** — a distraction from the city's **chronic** traffic problems. The minister's **prediction** that the **aquatic** metro line would **benefit** thousands seemed hardly **credible** to commuters who had **burned the midnight oil** analysing earlier failed projects. Yet the **evidence** was **visible** within months: travel time fell, and even the **malicious** rumours about corruption could not survive. Sometimes, the optimists **hit the nail on the head**.*

Root words used: *transport (port), chronic (chron), prediction (dict), aquatic (aqua), benefit (bene), credible (cred), evidence (vid), visible (vis), malicious (mal).* Idioms used: *red herring, burned the midnight oil, hit the nail on the head.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** From the passage — which root means "believe"? Which idiom means "a distraction"? Decode: *benevolent* = ?

## 5️⃣ ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Literal Option.** Question: *"To let the cat out of the bag."* Options include "to free a pet animal." The literal meaning is **always** a decoy. Never pick the option that just repeats the words.

**Trap 2 — The Almost-Right Pair.** *"Eat humble pie"* (apologise) vs *"eat one's words"* (withdraw a statement). Examiners place both in the same question. Learn confusing pairs *together* (see CINEMA).

**Trap 3 — The Changed-Word Idiom.** *"To grease the palm"* (to bribe) is correct; *"to grease the hand"* is not. If an option alters one word of a standard idiom, it is wrong — idioms are fixed.

## 6️⃣ 🔁 Linguistic Bridge: Hindi → English

| हम हिंदी में सोचते हैं | गलत English | सही English |
|---|---|---|
| "मुझे बहुत खुशी हुई" (दिल से) | I am very happy from my heart | I am **over the moon** |
| "उसने रात-रात भर पढ़ाई की" | He studied the whole night-night | He **burned the midnight oil** |
| "वह अपनी तारीफ़ खुद करता है" | He does his own praise | He **blows his own trumpet** |
| "बिना मतलब की मेहनत" | Meaningless hard work | A **wild goose chase** |

Hindi idioms rarely translate word-for-word. *"नौ दो ग्यारह होना"* is **not** "nine two eleven" — it is *"to take to one's heels"* (to run away). Always learn the English idiom as a complete unit.
