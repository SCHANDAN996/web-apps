1. Choose the correct improvement: "He is working here since 2015."
(a) He had worked here since 2015. (b) He was working here since 2015. (c) He has been working here since 2015. (d) He works here since 2015.
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Since 2015" with an action continuing up to now needs the present perfect continuous, not the simple present. The tempting simple-present version sounds natural in Hindi-influenced speech but is wrong in English. So the answer is "He has been working here since 2015."

2. Choose the correct improvement: "One of my friend has gone to Delhi."
(a) One of my friends have gone to Delhi. (b) One of my friend have gone to Delhi. (c) One of my friends has gone to Delhi. (d) One of my friend is going to Delhi.
Answer: (c)
Solution: After "one of", the noun must be plural (friends) but the verb stays singular (has). Learners often change the verb to "have" to match the plural noun — that is the trap. So the answer is "One of my friends has gone to Delhi."

3. Choose the correct improvement: "She is more taller than her sister."
(a) She is much more taller than her sister. (b) She is most taller than her sister. (c) She is taller than her sister. (d) She is more tall than her sister.
Answer: (c)
Solution: "Taller" is already comparative, so adding "more" makes a double comparative — a classic error. "More tall" is also wrong because "tall" forms its comparative with -er. So the answer is "She is taller than her sister."

4. Choose the correct improvement: "I have seen him yesterday."
(a) I saw him yesterday. (b) I had seen him yesterday. (c) I see him yesterday. (d) I am seeing him yesterday.
Answer: (a)
Solution: A definite past time word like "yesterday" takes the simple past, never the present perfect. "Had seen" would need another past event to precede. So the answer is "I saw him yesterday."

5. Choose the correct improvement: "He denied to help me."
(a) He refused to help me. (b) He denied to helping me. (c) He denied for help me. (d) He denied for helping me.
Answer: (a)
Solution: "Deny" is followed by a gerund or a that-clause, never an infinitive, so "denied to help" cannot be fixed by merely adjusting the preposition; the natural correction preserving the meaning is "refused to help". The other options keep the faulty "deny + to/for" patterns. So the answer is "He refused to help me."

6. Choose the correct improvement: "Unless you do not work hard, you will fail."
(a) Unless you worked hard, you will fail. (b) Unless you work hard, you will fail. (c) Until you do not work hard, you will fail. (d) Unless you will not work hard, you will fail.
Answer: (b)
Solution: "Unless" is already negative in meaning, so adding "not" creates a double negative — the examiner's favourite trap. "Until" refers to time, not condition. So the answer is "Unless you work hard, you will fail."

9. Choose the correct improvement: "I and Ram went to the market."
(a) Ram and I went to the market. (b) Me and Ram went to the market. (c) Ram and me went to the market. (d) I and Ram go to the market.
Answer: (a)
Solution: In polite English usage the first person comes last, and as a subject it must be "I", not "me". "Me and Ram" is a common spoken error. So the answer is "Ram and I went to the market."

10. Choose the correct improvement: "No sooner did he reach the station when the train left."
(a) No sooner did he reach the station then the train left. (b) No sooner he reached the station when the train left. (c) No sooner did he reach the station than the train left. (d) No sooner had he reach the station when the train left.
Answer: (c)
Solution: "No sooner" pairs with "than", not "when" or "then" — a fixed correlative pair often tested. So the answer is "No sooner did he reach the station than the train left."

21. Choose the correct improvement: "The principal, along with the teachers, have decided to cancel the tour."

(c) The principal, along with the teachers, have been deciding to cancel the tour. (d) The principal, along with the teachers, has decided to cancel the tour.
Answer: (d)
Solution: Examiner's trap — the intervening phrase "along with the teachers" does not change the subject; the verb agrees with "the principal" (singular). Choosing "have" to match the nearer plural noun is the trap. So the answer is "The principal, along with the teachers, has decided to cancel the tour."
