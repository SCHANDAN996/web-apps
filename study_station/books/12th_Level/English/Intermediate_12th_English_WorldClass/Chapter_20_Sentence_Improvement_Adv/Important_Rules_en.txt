# 📋 Important Grammar Rules & Tips — Sentence Improvement (Advanced)

## (a) 12 High-Yield Rules

| # | Rule | Example | Examiner's Trap |
|---|------|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Subject–Verb Agreement (Intervening Phrase):** The verb agrees with the subject, not with the noun nearest to it. | The quality of the mangoes **was** good. (not "were") | A plural noun between subject and verb ("of the mangoes") tempts you to pick "were". |
| 2 | **Each / Every / Either / Neither + singular verb.** | Neither of the two boys **has** done his homework. | "of the two boys" sounds plural — the trap is "have". |
| 3 | **Collective nouns** take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit; plural when members act separately. | The jury **has** given its verdict. / The jury **are** divided in their opinion. | Examiners pair a collective noun with a plural verb even when the group acts as one. |
| 4 | **"One of the + plural noun + singular verb."** | One of my friends **is** a doctor. | The plural noun "friends" lures you into "are". |
| 5 | **Correlative conjunctions must be parallel:** not only…but also, either…or, neither…nor, both…and. | He is **not only** a singer **but also** a dancer. | Wrong pairing: "not only…but also" replaced by "not only…and also" or "as well as" in the wrong slot. |
| 6 | **Proximity rule:** With "either…or / neither…nor / not only…but also", the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject. | Neither the teacher nor the students **were** present. | Students apply the main-subject rule and write "was". |
| 7 | **No double comparative / superlative.** | This is **better** (not "more better") than that. | "More better", "most fastest" — the "sound-right" trap for Hindi speakers ("zyada accha"). |
| 8 | **Prefer, senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior take "to", not "than".** | She is senior **to** me. / I prefer tea **to** coffee. | "Senior than me" sounds natural to Hindi speakers — a classic trap. |
| 9 | **Conditional sentences:** If + past simple → would + V1 (unreal present); If + past perfect → would have + V3 (unreal past). | If he **worked** hard, he **would pass**. / If he **had worked** hard, he **would have passed**. | "If he would have worked…" — "would" in the if-clause is always wrong. |
| 10 | **Parallel structure:** Items in a list must be in the same grammatical form. | She likes **reading, writing and singing**. (not "reading, writing and to sing") | One item quietly breaks the pattern — check every element, not just the first two. |
| 11 | **Misplaced / dangling modifier:** The phrase must logically modify the subject that follows it. | **Walking down the road,** a snake bit him. ❌ → **Walking down the road, he saw a snake.** ✔ | The sentence "sounds fine" until you ask: who was walking — the snake or the man? |
| 12 | **Redundancy:** Do not repeat the same idea twice. | **Return back** ❌ → **Return** ✔; **repeat again** ❌ → **repeat** ✔; **free gift** ❌ → **gift** ✔ | "Return back", "revert back", "enter into the room" are everyday speech — examiners bank on that habit. |

## (b) Time Management — The 15/45-Second Rule

| Type | What it looks like | Time | Strategy |
|------|-------------------|------|----------|
| **Fact-based (15 sec)** | Direct rule test: subject–verb agreement, article, preposition after senior/junior/prefer, double comparative, redundancy. The error is visible the moment you spot the keyword. | ≤ 15 sec | Spot the keyword (each, one of, senior, prefer, return back) → apply the rule → mark and move. Never re-read twice. |
| **Inference-based (45 sec)** | Parallelism across a long list, dangling modifiers, conditional mixing, "no improvement" options, sentences where two options look almost correct. | ≤ 45 sec | Read the full sentence once → identify what is being tested → eliminate two clearly wrong options → compare the remaining two word by word. |
| **Skip signal** | You have eliminated two options but the remaining two both "sound right" after 45 seconds. | — | Mark for review and move on. Sound-right confusion means the trap is working; a fresh look later beats a forced guess now. |

**Rule of thumb:** If you cannot name the rule being tested within 15 seconds, the question is inference-based — give it the full 45 seconds, not a rushed guess.

## (c) Morphology — Root, Affix, Example

| Root / Affix | Meaning | Example Word | Use in Sentence Improvement |
|--------------|---------|--------------|------------------------------|
| **-s / -es** (suffix) | plural noun / 3rd-person singular verb | boy**s** play / he play**s** | Agreement questions: strip the suffix to find the real subject. |
| **-ing** (suffix) | continuous action / gerund | walk**ing** | Dangling modifier questions: "-ing" opener must match the subject. |
| **-ed** (suffix) | past tense / past participle | work**ed**, had work**ed** | Conditional questions: "If + -ed" pairs with "would", "had + -ed" pairs with "would have". |
| **un- / in- / dis-** (prefix) | not, opposite of | **un**happy, **in**correct, **dis**agree | Negative words flip sentence meaning — "neither…nor" already carries a negative; adding "not" creates a double negative. |
| **re-** (prefix) | again, back | **re**turn, **re**peat, **re**vert | Redundancy questions: "re-" already means "back/again", so "return back" and "repeat again" are wrong. |
| **-er / -est, more / most** (comparison affixes) | comparative / superlative | fast**er**, fast**est** | Double-comparative questions: never combine "more + -er" or "most + -est". |
| **-ly** (suffix) | adverb marker | quick**ly**, careful**ly** | Modifier questions: adverbs modify verbs/adjectives; adjectives modify nouns — "He drove quick" ❌ → "quickly" ✔. |
| **pre- / post-** (prefix) | before / after | **pre**pone, **post**pone | Word-choice traps: "prepone" is Indian English; formal exams prefer "advance" / "bring forward". |
