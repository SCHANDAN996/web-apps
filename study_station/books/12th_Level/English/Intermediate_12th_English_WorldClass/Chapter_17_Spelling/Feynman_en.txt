🧒 Feynman Technique — Spelling Check

## The Hardest Concept, Explained to a 12-Year-Old

The hardest thing in Spelling Check is not memorising 5,000 words. It is this: **why does English spelling refuse to match English sound?** Once you understand this, half the chapter becomes easy.

Imagine English is a big joint family. Words came from different relatives — Latin, Greek, French, Germanic languages — and each relative brought their own spelling habits. "Phone" came from Greek (*phōnē* = sound), so it keeps the Greek "ph" for the /f/ sound. "Knight" came from Old English (a Germanic language), where the "k" was actually pronounced many centuries ago — today we dropped the sound but kept the letter, like keeping an old family photo nobody looks at.

So the golden insight is: **English words are spelled by their family history (root), not by their sound.** If you know the root, the spelling stops being a guess and becomes a clue.

## A Daily-Life Example

Think of your school bag. You don't search the whole bag for your maths notebook — you know it's in the "maths section." Roots work the same way:

- You hear the word "necessary." Don't guess letter by letter. Ask: which family? Latin *necesse*. Trick: **a shirt has 1 Collar and 2 Sleeves** → ne**c**e**ss**ary (one C, double S).
- You see "separate." Your tongue wants to say "seperate." But remember: there's **"a rat" in sep-A-RAT-e**. You'll never write "seperate" again.
- "Believe" vs "receive"? Use the rule: **I before E, except after C** → bel**ie**ve, but re**c-ei**ve.

## The Unforgettable Trick: SOUND → FAMILY → SPELLING

Whenever a spelling confuses you, run this 3-step check in your head:

1. **Sound it** — say the word slowly (gov-ern-ment, not "goverment").
2. **Find the family** — is there a root you know? (*govern* + *ment* — the "n" belongs to "govern," so it must stay.)
3. **Spell it** — write the root first, then add the pieces.

This is why "government" keeps its "n": the root *govern* demands it. Many people swallow the first "n" while speaking and then drop it in writing — but the spelling follows the root, not the careless pronunciation. Sound alone would betray you; the family saves you.

## ⚠️ The Trap to Tell Your Younger Sibling About

Examiners love words that *sound* right when spelled wrong: "occured," "tommorrow," "definately," "existance." All four are wrong (occurred, tomorrow, definitely, existence). The trap works because your ear approves them. Your defence: never trust your ear alone — check the root or the mnemonic.
