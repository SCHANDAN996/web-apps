# Spelling Check — Master the Words That Fool Your Eyes

## Why This Chapter Can Win or Lose Your Exam

Every year, thousands of SSC CHSL, CGL and RRB NTPC aspirants lose 2–4 marks not because they don't know English, but because they can't decide between *occassion*, *ocassion* and *occasion*. Spelling questions look like the easiest questions in the paper — and that is exactly why examiners love them. They are pure memory tests with zero calculation, which means **every mark here is a free mark** — if your memory is trained.

The good news: English spelling is not random. Most "difficult" exam words follow a small set of patterns — silent letters, doubled consonants, *ie/ei* confusion, and Greek/Latin roots. Master the patterns, and you master the chapter.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Spot the correctly (or incorrectly) spelt word among four close options in under 15 seconds.
2. Apply the core spelling rules — *i before e*, doubling of consonants, silent letters, *-able/-ible*.
3. Use Greek and Latin roots to decode the spelling of words you have never seen before.
4. Recognise the examiner's favourite trap words (the same 100–150 words repeat across exams).

## Part 1: How Spelling Questions Are Asked

In SSC, Railway and Bank exams, spelling appears in two formats:

- **Format A — "Find the correctly spelt word": Four options, three are misspelt, one is correct.
- **Format B — "Find the incorrectly spelt word": Four options, three are correct, one is wrong. ⚠️ This format is deadlier because your brain wants to pick the *correct* word out of habit.

**Golden habit:** Before looking at options, read the instruction twice and circle "correctly" or "incorrectly" in your mind. Most silly losses happen here, not in vocabulary.

🧠 **Active Recall:** Without looking back — what are the two formats of spelling questions, and which one is more dangerous? Why?

## Part 2: The Core Spelling Rules

### Rule 1: "I before E, except after C"

Write **i before e** (*believe, chief, relief, field*) — **except after c**, where it becomes *ei* (*receive, deceive, ceiling, receipt*).

But beware the famous exceptions: **weird, seize, height, their, foreign, neighbour, weigh** — these break the rule, and examiners know it. *Wierd* and *recieve* are among the most-tested misspellings in SSC papers.

**Mnemonic:** *"I before E, except after C — but weird people seize their foreign neighbour's height!"* (This one silly sentence contains all six exceptions.)

## Part 3: The Root Method — Decode Words You've Never Seen

English borrowed heavily from Greek and Latin. If you know the root, you can often *reason out* the correct spelling instead of guessing.

| Root | Meaning | Derived Words |
|---|---|---|
| **scrib/script** (Latin) | write | describe, prescription, manuscript, inscription |
| **dict** (Latin) | say/speak | dictate, predict, dictionary, verdict |
| **port** (Latin) | carry | transport, portable, import, support |
| **spect** (Latin) | look/see | inspect, respect, spectator, perspective |
| **phon** (Greek) | sound | telephone, microphone, symphony, phonetics |
| **graph** (Greek) | write/draw | photograph, biography, geography, autograph |
| **chron** (Greek) | time | chronology, chronic, synchronize, chronicle |
| **vid/vis** (Latin) | see | video, visible, vision, revise |

Notice how the root fixes the spelling: once you know *phon* = sound, you will never write *teliphone* or *sympony*; once you know *chron* = time, *cronology* looks obviously wrong.

### 📖 Words in Context — Mini Reading Passage

> When the doctor wrote a **prescription**, she began to **dictate** notes into her **microphone**. The hospital had recently decided to **transport** old paper files to a digital archive, where every **manuscript** and case **chronicle** would be stored in **chronological** order. A visiting **spectator** from the health ministry came to **inspect** the new system and praised its **portable** scanners. "This **vision** will **revolutionize** record-keeping," he said — though he had to check the **dictionary** twice before writing that last word in his report.

Every bold word comes from the roots table above. Read the passage twice — seeing words in a story fixes their spelling far better than reading lists.

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The "looks right" double-letter swap.** *Occassion* looks perfectly fine to the eye, but the correct spelling is **occasion** (one c, double s). Similarly: **accommodate** (double c, double m), **embarrass** (double r, double s), **millennium** (double l, double n). Examiners deliberately place the single-letter version as option (a) to catch hurried readers.

**Trap 2 — The pronunciation trap.** Words we mispronounce in everyday Indian English get misspelt: **February** (not *Febuary*), **Wednesday** (not *Wensday*), **government** (not *goverment*), **environment** (not *enviroment*), **restaurant** (not *restaurent*). Spell from memory of the written word, not from how you say it.

**Trap 3 — "Incorrectly spelt" questions.** In Format B, three options are correct and one is wrong. Students often mark the first correct-looking word. Train yourself: in "incorrectly spelt" questions, verify **all four** options before answering.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge — Hindi Speaker, English Spelling

| What Hindi speakers often write/say | Correct English |
|---|---|
| "What you are doing?" | "What **are** you doing?" |
| *seperate* (हिंदी उच्चारण "सेपरेट" से भ्रम) | **separate** (remember: there's "a rat" in sep**a rat**e) |
| *definately* | **definitely** (think: defin**ite**ly — from "finite") |
| *tommorrow* | **tomorrow** (one m, double r) |
| *untill* | **until** (one l) |
| *fourty* | **forty** (the "u" disappears — unlike "four") |

Hindi is written exactly as it is spoken; English is not. Never trust your pronunciation when spelling English — trust the written form you have *seen*.
