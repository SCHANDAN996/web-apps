# Sentence Structure

## The Hook

Every English question in your exam — Spot the Error, Sentence Improvement, Fill in the Blanks, even Comprehension — secretly tests one thing: **do you know how a sentence is built?** A candidate who understands sentence structure doesn't memorise thousands of rules; they *see* the skeleton of every sentence and instantly spot what's broken. This chapter gives you that X-ray vision.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. Identify the **Subject, Verb, Object, and Complement** in any sentence.
2. Recognise the **five basic sentence patterns** of English.
3. Distinguish between **phrases and clauses**, and between **simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences**.
4. Apply the **12 Golden Rules** that examiners test again and again.
5. Avoid the classic traps: intervening phrases, inverted order, and Hindi-influenced word order.

---

## 1. The Skeleton of a Sentence

A sentence is a group of words that expresses a **complete thought**. At its core, every English sentence needs two parts:

- **Subject (S)** — who or what the sentence is about.
- **Predicate** — what is said about the subject; it must contain a **finite verb (V)**.

> **Ravi / plays cricket.**
> (Subject) / (Predicate)

The predicate may also contain:

- **Object (O)** — receives the action: *Ravi plays **cricket**.*
- **Complement (C)** — completes the meaning after a linking verb: *Ravi is **a doctor**.*
- **Adjunct/Adverbial (A)** — tells when, where, how, why: *Ravi plays cricket **in the evening**.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** In the sentence *"The tired soldiers returned home at dawn,"* identify the subject, verb, object, and adjunct. (Answer: Subject = The tired soldiers; Verb = returned; Object = none — "home" is an adverbial of place; Adjunct = at dawn.)

---

## 2. The Five Basic Sentence Patterns

Every English sentence, however long, is built on one of these five patterns:

| Pattern | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | S + V | Birds **fly**. |
| 2 | S + V + O | She **wrote a letter**. |
| 3 | S + V + C | He **became a doctor**. / The soup **tastes good**. |
| 4 | S + V + Indirect O + Direct O | Mother **gave me a gift**. |
| 5 | S + V + O + C | They **elected him president**. |

**Why this matters for exams:** In Spot the Error questions, the error is often a missing or extra element in one of these patterns — e.g., *"He gave to me a book"* violates Pattern 4 (correct: *He gave me a book* / *He gave a book to me*).

🧠 **Active Recall:** Which pattern is *"The jury declared him guilty"*? (Pattern 5: S + V + O + C.)

---

## 3. Phrases vs Clauses

- **Phrase** = a group of words **without** a subject-verb pair: *in the morning, running fast, to win the prize.*
- **Clause** = a group of words **with** a subject and a finite verb.

Clauses are of two kinds:

- **Independent (Main) Clause** — can stand alone: *She passed the exam.*
- **Dependent (Subordinate) Clause** — cannot stand alone; begins with a subordinating conjunction (*because, although, when, if, since*) or a relative pronoun (*who, which, that*): *because she worked hard.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Is *"Walking down the road"* a phrase or a clause? (A phrase — no finite verb, no subject.)

---

## 4. The Four Sentence Types (by Structure)

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | 1 independent clause | *The train arrived late.* |
| **Compound** | 2+ independent clauses joined by **FANBOYS** (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon | *The train arrived late, **so** we missed the meeting.* |
| **Complex** | 1 independent + 1+ dependent clauses | ***Although** it rained, the match continued.* |
| **Compound-Complex** | 2+ independent + 1+ dependent | *Although it rained, the match continued, and the crowd cheered.* |

**Examiner's favourite:** asking you to identify the sentence type, or to spot a **comma splice** — two independent clauses joined by only a comma: ❌ *He came, he sat down.* ✅ *He came, and he sat down.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** *"When the bell rang, the students left, and the teacher smiled."* — which type? (Compound-complex.)

## 5. Word Order: The Rigid Rule of English

English word order is largely fixed: **Subject → Verb → Object**. Unlike Hindi, English cannot freely shuffle words without changing or destroying meaning.

Key word-order rules tested in exams:

1. **Inversion after negative adverbs:** When a sentence begins with *Never, Rarely, Seldom, Hardly, Scarcely, No sooner, Not only*, the auxiliary comes before the subject:
   - ❌ *Never I have seen such a sight.*
   - ✅ *Never **have I** seen such a sight.*
   - ✅ *No sooner **had she** arrived than the show began.*

2. **Question order:** auxiliary before subject — *What **are you** doing?* (not *"What you are doing?"*)

3. **Adverb order (manner → place → time):** *She sang **beautifully (M) at the concert (P) yesterday (T)**.*

🧠 **Active Recall:** Correct this: *"Hardly he had reached the station when the train left."* (Hardly **had he** reached…)

## 6. Subject–Verb Agreement: The Heart of Sentence Structure

The verb must agree with its **true subject** — not with the nearest noun. This is where examiners set their deadliest traps.

- *The **quality** of the mangoes **was** good.* (subject = quality, not mangoes)
- *Each of the boys **has** a pen.*
- *Neither of the answers **is** correct.*
- *Bread and butter **is** my breakfast.* (one combined idea → singular)
- *The poet and philosopher **is** dead.* (one person) vs *The poet and the philosopher **are** dead.* (two people)

---

## ⚠️ Examiner's Trap Box

**Trap 1 — The Intervening Phrase:** A long phrase between subject and verb hides the real subject.
❌ *The **rise** in prices of essential commodities **have** alarmed the public.*
✅ *The rise … **has** alarmed the public.* (subject = rise)

**Trap 2 — Inversion Blindness:** After *Hardly/Scarcely/No sooner/Not only*, students forget inversion.
❌ *Scarcely she had entered when the phone rang.*
✅ *Scarcely **had she** entered when the phone rang.*

**Trap 3 — Sound-Right Errors:** Phrases that "sound correct" because of spoken Hindi-English habits.
❌ *He is more senior than me.* → ✅ *He is senior **to** me.* (Latin comparatives — senior, junior, superior, inferior, prefer — take **to**, not *than*.)

---

## 🏆 The 12 Golden Rules of Sentence Structure

1. **Find the true subject** before choosing the verb — ignore intervening phrases.
2. **Each, every, either, neither, everyone, somebody** → singular verb.
3. **FANBOYS** join independent clauses in compound sentences; never use a comma alone (comma splice).
4. A dependent clause **cannot stand alone** — *"Because he was ill."* is a fragment.
5. After **negative adverbs** at the start (Never, Hardly, No sooner…), invert subject and auxiliary.
6. **No sooner … than**, **Hardly/Scarcely … when** — never mix the pairs.
7. **Senior, junior, superior, inferior, prefer** take **to**, not *than*.
8. In questions, the **auxiliary precedes the subject**: *Where **is he** going?*
9. **There is/are** agrees with the noun that follows: *There **is** a book and two pens on the table.* (proximity rule in standard usage: *There are two pens and a book.*)
10. **Collective nouns** (team, jury, committee) → singular when acting as one unit, plural when members act separately.
11. Adjectives like *the poor, the rich, the blind* (meaning a class of people) take a **plural** verb.
12. A sentence must have a **finite verb** — participles and infinitives alone cannot complete a sentence.

## 🔁 Linguistic Bridge (हिंदी से English)

| In Hindi we think… | ❌ Common Error | ✅ Correct English |
|---|---|---|
| तुम क्या कर रहे हो? | What you are doing? | What **are you** doing? |
| मैं स्कूल जा रहा हूँ ना? | I am going to school, no? | I am going to school, **aren't I**? |
| उसने मुझसे कहा | He told to me | He **told me** / He **said to me** |
| मैंने कल उसे देखा | I yesterday saw him | I **saw him yesterday** |
| वह मुझसे सीनियर है | He is senior than me | He is senior **to** me |
| मेरे पास दो भाई हैं | I am having two brothers | I **have** two brothers |

## 🧩 Mnemonic: **"SVO-CA" — "Smart Verbs Order Clearly, Always"**

Remember the natural English order: **S**ubject → **V**erb → **O**bject → **C**omplement → **A**djunct. If a sentence scrambles this order without a valid reason (question, inversion rule, emphasis), an error is probably hiding there.
