# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Error Spotting (Advanced)

## The Hardest Concept: Subject–Verb Agreement with Intervening Phrases

Imagine you are 12 years old and playing cricket with your friends. Your team captain, Rohan, is the one who decides everything. Now imagine Rohan is standing far away, and between you and Rohan there is a big crowd — his cousins, his neighbours, his dog, everyone. The crowd is noisy, but **the crowd does not make the decision. Only Rohan does.**

A sentence works exactly like this. The **subject** is Rohan — the real boss of the sentence. The **verb** must obey the subject. But examiners love to push a noisy crowd of words between the subject and the verb, hoping you will listen to the crowd instead of the boss.

Look at this sentence:

> ❌ *The quality of these mangoes **are** not good.*

The crowd here is *"of these mangoes"*. "Mangoes" is plural, and it sits right next to the verb, so your ear screams "are!" But wait — who is the real boss? **"The quality."** Quality is singular. So:

> ✅ *The quality of these mangoes **is** not good.*

## The Unforgettable Trick: "Cross the Bridge"

Whenever a sentence feels confusing, do this:

1. **Find the verb.**
2. Ask: *"Who or what is doing/being this?"* — that answer is your real subject.
3. **Cross out the bridge** — everything between the subject and the verb (phrases starting with *of, with, along with, together with, as well as, in addition to, besides*).
4. Now match the verb with the real subject alone.

Try it:

> *The captain, along with his team members, **was/were** felicitated.*

Cross the bridge: ~~along with his team members~~ → *The captain … **was** felicitated.* ✅

Remember: **"along with / as well as / together with" are bridges, not partners.** They never make the subject plural. Only **"and"** makes a true pair:

> *The captain **and** his team members **were** felicitated.* ✅

## Why Your Ear Betrays You (Sound-Right Trap)

Your ear is trained by the nearest word, not the correct word. In *"One of my friends **have**…"* the word "friends" touches the verb, so "have" *sounds* right. But the subject is **"One"** — singular. Correct: *"One of my friends **has** come."*

Rule of thumb: **after "one of / each of / either of / neither of + plural noun", the verb is always singular.** The plural noun is just the crowd; "one / each / either / neither" is Rohan.
