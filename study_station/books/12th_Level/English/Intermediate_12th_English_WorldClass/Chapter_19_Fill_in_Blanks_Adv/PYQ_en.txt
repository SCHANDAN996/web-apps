# 📊 PYQ Analysis — Fill in the Blanks (Advanced)

## How Exams Test This Chapter

Fill in the Blanks appears in **every** SSC (CGL, CHSL, MTS, GD), Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI), Railway (NTPC, Group D) and State exam English paper. Over the years, the pattern has been remarkably stable — examiners rotate the same grammar zones with new sentences.

### What the Pattern Analysis Shows (Qualitative, Not Numbers)

**Consistently high-frequency zones:**
- **Tense & time markers** — "since/for", "by the time", "hardly…when" — the single most repeated area.
- **Subject–Verb Agreement with traps** — intervening phrases ("The quality of apples *was*…"), collective nouns, "each/every/neither".
- **Prepositions** — fixed phrases (abide by, consist of, deal in) and dependent prepositions after adjectives/verbs.
- **Articles** — especially zero article vs. "the" with institutions, meals, and unique nouns.
- **Conjunctions/Connectors** — although/but, because/so pairs; correlative pairs (not only…but also).
- **Vocabulary-in-context** — choosing the word that fits *tone and collocation*, not just meaning (growing weight in Banking exams).
- **Phrasal verbs & idioms in blanks** — put off, look into, call for.

**Exam-wise flavour:**
- **SSC CGL/CHSL:** grammar-rule blanks dominate; vocabulary blanks are synonym-level.
- **IBPS/SBI PO:** sentence-completion with tone and logic; two-blank questions; words must fit *both* grammatically and contextually.
- **Railway/MTS/GD:** direct rule recall — articles, tense, prepositions.

### The Examiner's Four Favourite Traps

| Trap Type | How It Works | Example Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Intervening Phrase** | Extra words push the real subject away from the verb | "The box of chocolates ___ (is/are) on the table." |
| **Sound-Right** | The wrong option *sounds* natural in spoken English | "He is senior ___ me." (to ✓, than ✗) |
| **Over-thinking** | The simple, obvious answer is correct; the fancy option is bait | Blank before "honest man" → "an", not a complicated structure |
| **Time-Marker Confusion** | "Since" vs "for", "hardly…when" vs "no sooner…than" swapped | "No sooner had he arrived ___ the show began." (than ✓) |

## Top 10 High-Yield Questions (PYQ-Style) with Solutions

**Q1.** The committee ___ divided in its opinion on the new policy.
(a) is (b) are (c) were (d) have been
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Collective noun + "its" (singular pronoun) → the committee is acting as one body here in terms of the subject-verb rule tested; with "its opinion", use singular "is". If members act individually, the sentence would say "their opinions".
**Trap type:** Collective Noun.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC pattern)

**Q2.** The quality of the mangoes ___ not good this season.
(a) are (b) were (c) was (d) have been
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** The subject is "quality" (singular), not "mangoes". The phrase "of the mangoes" only intervenes. Singular subject + "this season" → "was".
**Trap type:** Intervening Phrase.
**Source:** PYQ-style (SSC CGL pattern)

**Q3.** He has been working in this bank ___ 2015.
(a) for (b) since (c) from (d) by
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "Since" + point of time (2015); "for" + period of time (for nine years). Present perfect continuous confirms duration up to now.
**Trap type:** Time-Marker Confusion.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q4.** No sooner did the bell ring ___ the students rushed out.
(a) when (b) then (c) than (d) that
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** Fixed correlative pair: "No sooner…than". Compare: "Hardly/Scarcely…when". Examiners swap these deliberately.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right (many learners say "no sooner…when" in speech).
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q5.** One of my friends ___ going to Delhi tomorrow.
(a) are (b) is (c) were (d) have been
**Answer: (b)**
**Solution:** "One of + plural noun" takes a **singular** verb — the real subject is "One". "Tomorrow" rules out past forms.
**Trap type:** Intervening Phrase.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q6.** She is ___ university student, so she travels by ___ bus every day.
(a) an, the (b) a, the (c) a, no article (d) an, no article
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** "University" begins with a /juː/ (consonant) sound → "a". "By bus/train/car" (means of transport) takes **zero article**.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right (spelling vs. sound; article before "by + transport").
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q7.** The manager, along with his assistants, ___ attending the meeting.
(a) are (b) were (c) is (d) have been
**Answer: (c)**
**Solution:** With "along with / as well as / together with", the verb agrees with the **first** subject ("the manager" — singular).
**Trap type:** Intervening Phrase.
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q8.** The government has decided to ___ a new scheme for farmers. *(two-word collocation)*
(a) launch (b) throw (c) begin up (d) make out
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** "Launch a scheme" is the standard collocation. "Begin up" is not idiomatic; "make out" means to discern. Banking exams love collocation blanks.
**Trap type:** Over-thinking (the simple, natural word is correct).
**Source:** PYQ-style (IBPS pattern)

**Q9.** Although he worked hard, ___ he could not clear the cut-off.
(a) but (b) yet (c) still (d) no connector needed
**Answer: (d)**
**Solution:** "Although" and "but" cannot appear together in standard English — a classic double-connector trap. The sentence is complete without a second connector.
**Trap type:** Sound-Right (Hindi "हालाँकि…फिर भी" pushes learners toward "but").
**Source:** PYQ-style

**Q10.** The report highlights a ___ decline in rural employment, which economists find alarming.
(a) sharp (b) hardly (c) strongly (d) high
**Answer: (a)**
**Solution:** Only "sharp" collocates naturally with "decline" (sharp decline/rise). "Hardly" is an adverb of frequency/degree, "strongly" is an adverb, "high" doesn't pair with decline. Tone: negative trend → "alarming" confirms a serious fall.
**Trap type:** Over-thinking / collocation.
**Source:** PYQ-style (Banking pattern)

## Applying the 15/45-Second Rule

**15-second question — Q3 (since/for):** Spot the time expression "2015" (a point of time) → "since". No need to read the sentence twice. Rule-recall = 15 seconds.

**45-second question — Q10 (collocation + tone):** You must (1) read the whole sentence, (2) note the tone word "alarming", (3) test each option inside the blank, (4) reject grammatically possible but unnatural options. Inference + collocation = up to 45 seconds — budget it, don't rush it.

**Strategy takeaway:** In every paper, roughly two-thirds of blanks are 15-second rule questions (tense, articles, prepositions, S-V agreement). Bank those fast and spend the saved time on vocabulary-in-context and two-blank questions.
