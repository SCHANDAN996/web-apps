🧒 Feynman Technique — Explaining Verbs Like You're 12

**The Hardest Concept: Subject–Verb Agreement with "Tricky Middle Words"**

Imagine you are the captain of a cricket team. Your job is simple: count your players and shout "One player IS here!" or "Two players ARE here!" Easy, right?

Now imagine this: while you're counting, a crowd of fans runs between you and your players, shouting, waving banners, distracting you. If you start counting the *fans* instead of your *players*, you'll shout the wrong number.

That's exactly what happens in sentences like:

❌ "The quality of the mangoes **were** good."
✅ "The quality of the mangoes **was** good."

The crowd of fans here is **"of the mangoes"** — a noisy phrase sitting between the real subject (**quality**) and the verb. "Mangoes" is plural, so your brain screams *were*! But the real player you're counting is **quality** — singular. So the verb must be **was**.

**The Unforgettable Trick: "Cross Out the Middle!" 🏏**

Whenever you see a long sentence, take your mental pencil and cross out everything between the subject and the verb:

- "The **quality** ~~of the mangoes~~ **was** good."
- "**One** ~~of my friends who live in Delhi~~ **has** won a prize." ✔️

The verb only listens to the REAL subject. The middle words are just fans making noise.

**Why do examiners love this trap?** Because your ear is trained by Hindi and everyday speech. "The quality of the mangoes were" *sounds* fine because "mangoes" sits right next to the verb. Examiners know your ear will betray you — so they often hide the real subject behind a plural noun.

**One more daily-life picture:** Think of the verb as a magnet 🧲. It is attracted ONLY to the subject, no matter how many shiny metal objects (other nouns) you place in between. The magnet never changes its pull.
