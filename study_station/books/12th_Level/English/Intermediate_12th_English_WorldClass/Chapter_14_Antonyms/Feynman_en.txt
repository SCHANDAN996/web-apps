# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Antonyms Explained to a 12-Year-Old

## The Hardest Concept: "Near-Antonyms" — Words That *Look* Opposite But Aren't

Imagine you and your friend are standing at a bus stop. Your friend says, "I *dislike* this movie." You say, "I *hate* this movie." Are you saying opposite things? **No!** You both don't like the movie — your friend just said it softly, and you said it loudly.

This is exactly the trap in antonym questions. The examiner gives you a word like **"scarce"** and four options:

- (a) abundant
- (b) rare
- (c) meagre
- (d) insufficient

Now look carefully. Options (b), (c), and (d) all *feel* like opposites because they sound negative. But wait — **rare, meagre, and insufficient all mean the SAME thing as scarce!** They are synonyms wearing a disguise. Only **abundant** (plenty) is the true opposite.

**The rule a 12-year-old can remember:** Before picking the opposite, first ask — *"Does this option mean the same as the question word?"* If yes, throw it out, no matter how "opposite-sounding" it feels.

## A Daily-Life Example

Think of a cricket scoreboard. If Team A's score is **"high"**, the opposite is **"low"** — not "big", not "huge", not "massive". Those three are on Team A's side! In every antonym question, the examiner hides 2–3 words on the *same team* as the question word. Your job is to find the one player on the *other* team.

## The Unforgettable Trick: **"Same Team or Other Team?" ⚽**

Draw an imaginary football field in your head:

1. Put the **question word** on the LEFT goal.
2. Test each option: *"Does it score for the LEFT goal (same meaning) or the RIGHT goal (opposite meaning)?"*
3. The answer is the only word that scores for the RIGHT goal.

Try it: **Question word = "expand"**
- (a) enlarge → LEFT goal (same team) ❌
- (b) extend → LEFT goal (same team) ❌
- (c) contract → RIGHT goal (opposite!) ✅
- (d) spread → LEFT goal (same team) ❌

Answer: **(c) contract.** Three same-team players, one opponent. This pattern repeats in almost every exam antonym question.

## One More Trap: Opposites Change with Context

"Light" means *not heavy* → opposite is **heavy**.
"Light" also means *brightness* → opposite is **dark**.

So never answer from memory alone. Read the word, think of its most common exam meaning, *then* find the opposite. If two options look like opposites of two different meanings, pick the one matching the most standard meaning.
