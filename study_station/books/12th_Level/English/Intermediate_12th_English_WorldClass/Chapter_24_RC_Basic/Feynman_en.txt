# 🧒 Feynman Technique — Reading Comprehension (Basic)

## The Hardest Concept, Explained to a 12-Year-Old: **Inference Questions**

Imagine you come home and see muddy footprints on the floor, a wet umbrella by the door, and your sister wearing a raincoat. Nobody told you it rained — but you *know* it rained. You didn't read it anywhere; you **figured it out from clues**.

That is exactly what an **inference question** is.

In a Reading Comprehension passage, the writer is like your house: full of clues, but not always saying things directly. Two types of questions come from this:

- **Fact questions** = "Is the umbrella wet?" → The answer is written *right there* in the passage. Just find it. (15 seconds)
- **Inference questions** = "Did it rain today?" → The answer is *not* written. You must combine two or three clues and conclude. (45 seconds)

### The Unforgettable Trick: **"The Passage Is the Whole Universe" 🌍**

Here is where most students lose marks. They use their *own* knowledge instead of the passage's clues.

Suppose the passage says: *"Ravi skipped breakfast and kept checking his watch."* A question asks: *"What can be inferred about Ravi?"*

- ❌ Wrong thinking: "Ravi is lazy" — maybe YOU think skipping breakfast = lazy. But the passage never said that!
- ✅ Right thinking: "Ravi was in a hurry" — because *skipping breakfast* + *checking the watch* are the two clues, and "hurry" is the only conclusion both clues support.

**Rule of thumb:** An inference answer must be provable using ONLY the passage. If you need outside knowledge, it's a trap option. The correct inference is usually the *smallest, safest* conclusion — not the most dramatic one.

### The Detective Approach 🔍

1. **Find the clues** — underline the 2–3 lines the question points to.
2. **Combine them** — ask: "What MUST be true if all these clues are true?"
3. **Reject the drama** — options with words like *always, never, completely, only* are usually traps. The passage rarely supports such extreme claims.
4. **Reject the copy-paste** — if an option is copied word-for-word from the passage, it's a *fact*, not an *inference*. Inference answers are the passage's idea in *new words*.

### Tone Mapping — The Author's Mood

Inference also hides in **tone**. Ask: is the author happy, worried, or neutral about the topic? Watch the adjectives: *"a promising technology"* (positive) vs *"a risky gamble"* (negative) vs *"a recent development"* (neutral). The author's word choices are the footprints on the floor.
