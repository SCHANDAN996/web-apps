Card 1
Front: What is a Para Jumble?
Back: A set of 4–6 shuffled sentences that must be rearranged into one logical, coherent paragraph. Your job is to find the correct sequence.

Card 2
Front: Which sentence is usually the OPENING sentence of a para jumble?
Back: The one that is independent — it introduces a person, place, or idea with a full noun (not a pronoun), has no connector like "but/however/therefore", and does not refer back to anything.

Card 3
Front: Why can a sentence starting with "He", "It", "This", or "These" rarely open a paragraph?
Back: Pronouns and demonstratives need an antecedent (a noun mentioned earlier). If nothing came before, the reader doesn't know what "He" or "This" refers to — so such a sentence must follow the sentence containing its noun.

Card 4
Front: What is a "mandatory pair"?
Back: Two sentences that must stay together in a fixed order — e.g., a sentence naming "Alexander Fleming" followed by one saying "He discovered penicillin." Finding pairs shrinks 4–6 sentences into 2–3 blocks, making the puzzle easy.

Card 5
Front: 🧠 MNEMONIC — What does "O-P-E-N" stand for when hunting the opening sentence?
Back: O = Original noun (full name, no pronoun), P = Plain (no connector like but/so), E = Establishes the topic, N = No back-reference. A sentence passing all four tests is your opener.

Card 6
Front: If one sentence says "…the government announced a new policy" and another begins "However, the policy failed…", what is their order?
Back: The policy must be introduced first, then criticised. So: announcement → "However, the policy failed…". Contrast words (however, but, yet) always come AFTER the idea they contrast.

Card 7
Front: ⚠️ EXAMINER'S TRAP — A sentence begins with a person's full name. Must it be the opening sentence?
Back: Not necessarily. Trap: a full name can also appear mid-paragraph (e.g., "The award went to Marie Curie, who…"). Check the whole sentence — if it continues an action already started, it is NOT the opener. Independence matters, not just the noun.

Card 8
Front: What do "sequence words" like First… Then… Finally… tell you?
Back: They fix a chronological chain. "First" usually opens the chain, "Then/Next/Later" come in the middle, and "Finally/Eventually" closes it. Spot one, and the others fall into place.

Card 9
Front: What is the role of a CONCLUDING sentence in a para jumble?
Back: It summarises, gives a result, or looks forward — often marked by "Thus", "Therefore", "In conclusion", "As a result", or a general closing remark. It never introduces a brand-new idea.

Card 10
Front: A sentence says "This discovery changed medicine forever." Where does it go?
Back: Immediately after the sentence that names the discovery. "This + noun" is a demonstrative pointer — it must directly follow its antecedent. This is one of the strongest mandatory-pair signals.

Card 11
Front: ⚠️ EXAMINER'S TRAP — Two sequences both "sound smooth" when read aloud. What should you do?
Back: Don't trust your ear — trust the logic links. Check pronoun antecedents, connectors, and chronology. Examiners design one option to sound right but break a reference chain (Sound-Right trap). The logically linked answer wins.

Card 12
Front: What is the "General → Specific" rule?
Back: Paragraphs usually move from a general statement to details, examples, or data. If one sentence defines a concept and another gives statistics about it, the definition comes first.

Card 13
Front: 🧠 MNEMONIC — What does "CHAIN" remind you to check while solving?
Back: C = Connectors (but, so, however), H = He/It/This (pronoun antecedents), A = Article shift (a → the: "a boy" first, "the boy" later), I = Idea flow (general → specific), N = Names (full name first, short name/pronoun later).

Card 14
Front: How does the article shift "a → the" help in para jumbles?
Back: A noun is introduced with "a/an" the first time and referred to with "the" afterwards. So the sentence with "a scientist" comes before the sentence with "the scientist".

Card 15
Front: What should you do FIRST when you see a para jumble in the exam — read all options or find the opener?
Back: Find the opener (or a mandatory pair) first, then eliminate options that don't match. Reading all four full sequences wastes time; elimination by one fixed link usually kills 2–3 options in seconds.

Card 16
Front: A sentence begins "Moreover, the scheme also reduced costs." What does "Moreover" tell you?
Back: It ADDS to a previous point — so a sentence praising or describing the scheme must come just before it. Addition words (moreover, also, furthermore, besides) always follow the point they extend.

Card 17
Front: In a Cause–Effect pair, which sentence comes first?
Back: The cause. "Heavy rain flooded the streets" → "Therefore, schools were shut." Effect markers (therefore, as a result, consequently, hence) signal that the cause sentence precedes them.

Card 18
Front: What is the 15/45-second guideline for para jumbles?
Back: A 4-sentence jumble with clear links (pronouns, sequence words) ≈ 45 seconds of structured work — it is NOT a 15-second fact question. If no link appears in ~45–60 seconds, mark and skip; return later rather than guessing blindly.

Card 19
Front: If a jumble is about a person's life, what ordering clue usually works?
Back: Chronology — birth/early life → achievements → later years → legacy. Time expressions (in 1905…, years later…, today…) lock the sequence.

Card 20
Front: After arranging the sentences, what is the final 10-second check?
Back: Read the full paragraph once in your chosen order. Every pronoun must have a clear antecedent, every connector must connect logically, and no sentence should feel like it restarts the topic. If one sentence "jars", your sequence is wrong.
