Simplification: The Invisible Thread That Holds Math Together

The 3-Second Trap That Costs Students 15 Marks

Riya was confident. She had solved hundreds of simplification problems in her coaching class. In the railway exam, she saw 18 ÷ 3 × 2 + 5 – 1 and instantly wrote 8. The answer key said 16. She had fallen into the exact trap that examiners lay every year — ignoring the invisible hierarchy of operations. That one mistake dropped her below the cut-off.

You think simplification is about speed. It is not. It is about respecting the sequence. The number line has a constitution, and its supreme law is called BODMAS. Break it, and your answer gets disqualified — no appeal.

🧠 Pause & Recall: Without looking ahead, can you write what each letter in BODMAS stands for? Do it now.

Why Your Brain Hates Simplification (And How to Trick It)

Your mind craves left-to-right flow. When you read a sentence, you process it word by word. Numbers feel the same way. That’s why 20 – 2 × 5 screams “18 × 5 = 90” in your head. But math is not a sentence; it’s a layered cake. You must eat the layers in order, not from left to right.

What trips students most isn’t the calculation — it’s the mental clash between natural instinct and mathematical law. Accept this: your first urge is often wrong. And that’s okay, because now we’ll rewire it together.

🧠 Check yourself: Solve 12 + 8 ÷ 4. Did you feel the urge to add first? If yes, congratulations — you are human. Now override it with the rule.

The BODMAS Family: Meet the Rule That Rules All

B – Brackets first (parentheses (), curly {}, square []). Innermost bracket always wins.

O – Of (as in “half of 8”) or Orders (powers/roots). Treat “of” as multiplication but do it before division.

D – Division, M – Multiplication. They are equals; when together, move left → right.

A – Addition, S – Subtraction. Also equals; left → right.

Here lies the biggest myth: “Division always comes before multiplication.” No. They hold hands and dance left to right. The same with addition and subtraction.

🃏Memory Trick (The Weird Mnemonic): Imagine a grumpy gatekeeper named Bhola Of Delhi Mandir. He wears a coat and shouts:

Brackets kholo, Order lo, Divide karo, Multiply karo, Add karo, Subtract karo.

But when you reach D and M, he screams “Left se Right chalo, nahi to bhool jaoge!” Same for A and S.

This absurd image of a gatekeeper yelling “Left se Right!” will stick because your brain loves bizarre pictures. Say it aloud once: Bhola of Delhi Mandir, Left se Right!

Unlocking Brackets Without Fear

Brackets create a miniature world. You must solve that world completely before it can interact with the outside. Types of brackets: ( ) → { } → [ ]. The rule: Innocent until proven solved — start with the innermost.

Example: [ 20 – { 4 + ( 6 ÷ 3 ) } ]

Step 1: Innermost (6 ÷ 3) = 2
Step 2: {4 + 2} = 6
Step 3: [20 – 6] = 14

Notice how the brackets collapse one by one, like a folding umbrella.

The DM & AS Tango: Left-to-Right Priority Demystified

Take 18 ÷ 3 × 2. Many divide 3 × 2 first, getting 18 ÷ 6 = 3. That’s wrong.

Correct: 18 ÷ 3 = 6, then 6 × 2 = 12. Because division came first on the left.

Similarly, 30 – 5 + 2. Don’t add 5+2 first. Go left to right: 30 – 5 = 25, then 25 + 2 = 27.

When the precedence is equal, left-right is the tie-breaker. This single shift in habit will eliminate 40% of simplification errors.

🧠 Spot the error: 24 ÷ 4 × 3. Which answer is correct — 18 or 2? And why does the other feel so tempting?

“Of” — The Silent Marks Killer

“Of” looks innocent but attacks without warning. It sits between Multiplication and Division in priority, meaning: “Of” is executed before Division and Multiplication. In pure BODMAS, O stands for Of / Orders.

Example: 4 ÷ 2 of 2

Step 1: 2 of 2 means 2 × 2 = 4 (do this first)
Step 2: 4 ÷ 4 = 1

Many students treat “of” as normal multiplication and go left to right: 4 ÷ 2 × 2 = 4. That’s wrong. “Of” creates an invisible bracket. Always group the numbers linked by “of”.

🧠 Quick test: 3 of 4 ÷ 6 – what’s the result?

Decimals, Fractions, and How to Not Drown

🧠
When a simplification string mixes fractions and decimals, convert everything into one form — preferably fractions. Why? Fractions show exact relationships, decimals hide them.

0.5 + 1/4 becomes 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 (0.75). Clean, no rounding errors.

For mixed operations like 1/2 ÷ 0.25 × 1/3, convert:

1/2 ÷ 1/4 × 1/3 → (1/2 × 4/1) × 1/3 = 2 × 1/3 = 2/3.

The Surds and Powers Shortcut

When you see square roots or powers, treat them as “Orders” in BODMAS. Solve them right after brackets and “of”.

√16 + 3² → 4 + 9 = 13. Not √(16+9).

Simplification questions often hide square roots inside fractions: 2/√4. Simplify the root first: 2/2 = 1. Rushing will make you write 1/2.

In Simple Words

The Great Math Race: Why Left-to-Right Is the Secret Rule

Imagine a relay race with four friends: Addy, Subby, Multi, and Divvy. Their rule: when two of them are in the same lane, they must run in the order they are standing — left to right. One day, Divvy and Multi are standing together. Divvy is on the left, Multi on the right. The coach blows the whistle. Multi shouts, "Me first! I'm more important!" But the judge says, "No, you are twin powers. When you're together, you run in the order you appear, left to right."

So 18 ÷ 3 × 2 means Divvy runs first (18 ÷ 3 = 6), then Multi takes the baton (6 × 2 = 12). If Multi had jumped ahead, 3 × 2 = 6, then Divvy would be left with 18 ÷ 6 = 3 — a completely different race, and the wrong winner.

This same rule applies to Addy and Subby. In 30 – 5 + 2, Subby is on the left, so he goes first: 30 – 5 = 25, then Addy runs: 25 + 2 = 27. If you let Addy go first (5 + 2 = 7) then 30 – 7 = 23, it's cheating.

The Never-Forget Trick: Draw a horizontal arrow over the expression. Whenever you see Division and Multiplication together, or Addition and Subtraction together, slide your finger along the arrow from left to right, doing each as you meet it. Think of it as a math conveyor belt — you can't grab an item from the right side first.

Story check: Once, a girl named Anju had to split 24 marbles among 4 boxes, then each box's marbles were multiplied into 3 shiny stones. She did 24 ÷ 4 × 3. Her friend said, "Multiply first! 4 × 3 = 12, then 24 ÷ 12 = 2 stones." Anju remembered the left-to-right relay, did 24 ÷ 4 = 6, then 6 × 3 = 18 stones. She got 18 beautiful stones; her friend got only 2. The rule saved her treasure.

Short Tricks

1. The Angry Gatekeeper of BODMAS

Imagine a huge, red-faced gatekeeper at the entrance of a math palace. His name is Bhola Of Delhi Mandir. He shouts: “Brackets kholo! Of lo! Divide karo! Multiply karo! Add karo! Subtract karo!” But whenever you reach Division and Multiplication together, he screams, “Left se Right chalo, nahi to bhool jaoge!” Same for Addition and Subtraction. The absurd shouting gatekeeper image anchors the entire BODMAS sequence and the left-to-right rule.

2. The Cannibal’s Dinner Table (Brackets Order)

Picture a cannibal eating his meal. He first eats the smallest dish ( ), then the medium dish { }, and finally the largest dish [ ]. The rule is: eat from the inside out. Innermost bracket always goes first, just like a hungry cannibal who won’t touch the big pot before finishing the small plate. You’ll never forget that () must be solved before {} and {} before [].

3. The “Of” Monster with Invisible Hands

Visualise a monster named “Of” that hides behind numbers. Whenever you see a phrase like “3 of 4”, the monster grabs those two numbers and wraps them in an invisible bracket, making them untouchable by division or multiplication until you solve it. Picture the monster roaring, “I am stronger than × and ÷!” This trick reminds you that “of” has higher priority than multiplication/division and must be solved immediately after brackets.

4. Left-Right Conveyor Belt (DM and AS Rule)

Imagine a factory conveyor belt carrying numbers and operators. When equal-strength operators (÷ and ×, or + and –) come in a row, the belt moves only from left to right. You can’t pick an item from the right side until it reaches the front. So for 18 ÷ 3 × 2, the belt brings ÷ first, then ×. You process in order. This vivid image kills the habit of blindly multiplying before dividing.

5. The Bhola-of-Delhi-Mandir Acronym Song

Sing this to the tune of a popular local rhyme:

B for Brackets, peel them like a banana,

O for Of, the hidden bracket planner,

D for Divide, M for Multiply, left to right they dance,

A for Add, S for Subtract, they never miss their chance.

The rhyme cements the sequence and the equal-priority left-to-right rule in a musical memory.

6. The Fraction-Decimal Marriage (Convert All to Fractions)

Imagine a wedding between a fraction boy and a decimal girl. The priest says, “For a happy life, always convert decimals to fractions first!” Visualise the decimal 0.5 taking off his disguise to reveal his true form 1/2. Then the couple lives happily as fractions. Whenever you see a mix of fractions and decimals, remember the wedding and convert all to fractions for a smooth calculation.

7. The Three-Headed Percentage Beast

Meet a three-headed dragon. Each head represents a common percentage: 50% (½), 33⅓% (⅓), and 12.5% (⅛). When you see these percentages, imagine the dragon breathing fire and shouting, “I am a fraction in disguise!” This helps you instantly convert percentages to fractions without pen and paper, speeding up simplification.

8. The Invisible Bracket Ghost around “Of”

Close your eyes and picture a ghost named “Of” who wears a bracket-shaped hat. Wherever “of” appears in an expression, the ghost drops an invisible pair of brackets around the two numbers. So 4 ÷ 2 of 2 becomes 4 ÷ [2 of 2] in your mind. The ghost’s whisper: “Solve me first, or I’ll haunt your answer!” This trick stops the common mistake of treating “of” as ordinary multiplication.

9. The BODMAS Family Tree (Loci Method)

Place each BODMAS family member in a room of your house:

Entrance: Brackets – open the door first.

Drawing room: Of/Orders – sit and talk.

Kitchen: Division & Multiplication – cook together, left to right.

Garden: Addition & Subtraction – stroll left to right.

As you mentally walk through your house, you recall the order without effort. The absurd idea of a family doing math around the house makes it stick.

10. The Bursting Firecracker (Surds and Powers First)

Imagine a firecracker with two parts: the root (√) and the power (²,³). Before you can light it, you must handle each part separately. √16 + 3² means you crack the root (4) and pop the power (9) first, then add them. If you light the whole expression at once, the firecracker fizzles. This reminds you that roots and powers are solved individually right after brackets and “of”.

11. The Twin Rule of DM and AS (Left-Right Handshake)

Visualise two pairs of twins: Divvy & Multi, and Addy & Subby. They always hold hands and walk in the order they are standing. The one on the left walks first. So if Divvy is standing to the left of Multi, Divvy goes first. If Addy is to the left of Subby, Addy goes first. The twin handshake image stops you from breaking the left-to-right sequence.

12. The Brackets Family (Eldest to Youngest)

Picture a family with three members: Child ( ), Mother { }, and Father [ ]. The rule is: the youngest (child) must finish eating before the mother can start, and the mother before the father. So solve ( ) first, then { }, then [ ]. The family pecking order ensures you never mess up the bracket order.

13. The Zero-Term Bomb Detector

In a long simplification, imagine you’re a bomb disposal expert. If you spot (a – a) inside any bracket, it’s a zero bomb – it destroys the entire multiplication chain it touches. You shout, “Zero detected! No need to calculate further!” This trick saves time and prevents unnecessary computations when a bracket evaluates to zero.

14. The “DM Left-Right” Arrow Tattoo

Imagine you have a glowing arrow tattoo on your forearm that always points from left to right. Whenever you see ÷ and × together (or + and – together), you glance at your tattoo and slide your finger along the arrow, solving as you move. The physical act of sliding your finger reinforces the left-to-right rule and prevents the common “multiply first” blunder.

15. The “Of is Boss” Office Hierarchy

In an office, the boss “Of” sits in a cabin. Division and Multiplication are employees. When an expression arrives, the boss “Of” demands, “I’ll take these two numbers first. You wait outside.” Only after the boss finishes does he let the employees in, from left to right. This hierarchy picture permanently fixes the priority of “of” over division and multiplication.
