We Know the Pain

🧠
You’ve probably stared at ⅞ + 0.375 and felt your brain freeze. You’re not alone. Most students treat fractions and decimals as two different monsters. The truth? They are the same number wearing different masks. Once you see the mask, the fear vanishes. This chapter won’t just teach you rules; it will rewire the way you think.

1. The Real Meaning of a Fraction

A fraction is simply a division problem that hasn’t been solved yet.

The top number (numerator) tells how many parts you have. The bottom number (denominator) tells how many equal parts make a whole.

Think of a chocolate bar divided into 4 equal pieces. If you pick up 3 pieces, you have ¾. That’s it – nothing scary.

Types of fractions you must instantly recognise:

Proper fraction: Numerator < Denominator (⅔, ⅞) – value less than 1.

Improper fraction: Numerator ≥ Denominator (⁷⁄₄, ¹¹⁄₅) – value 1 or more.

Mixed number: Whole number + proper fraction (1¾, 3⅖).

Like fractions: Same denominator (⅖, ⅘).

Unlike fractions: Different denominators (⅔, ¾).

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Without looking back, define proper and improper fraction in your own words. Give one example of each.

2. Decimals – Fractions in Disguise

A decimal is just a fraction whose denominator is a power of 10 (10, 100, 1000…).

0.7 means 7/10.

0.23 means 23/100.

0.005 means 5/1000.

The number of digits after the decimal point tells you the number of zeros in the denominator. This single idea makes conversion a breeze.

Terminating vs Recurring Decimals

Terminating: ends after a few digits (0.25, 1.4).

Recurring (non-terminating repeating): a digit or block repeats forever (0.333…, 0.142857 142857…).

Recurring decimals are represented with a bar above the repeating part, like 0.6̅ or 0.1̅4̅2̅8̅5̅7̅. In competitive exams, converting them to fractions is a goldmine for quick marks.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Write 0.125 as a fraction in its simplest form. Then check: 125/1000 = 1/8.

3. The Bilingual Trick to Compare Fractions & Decimals

Comparison is a favourite trap. Use these go-to methods:

Method 1 – Cross Multiplication (for fractions)

To compare 5/7 and 3/4:

5 × 4 = 20, 3 × 7 = 21. Since 20 < 21, 5/7 < 3/4. Done in 5 seconds.

Method 2 – Decimal Conversion

When fractions are messy, convert to decimals up to 2-3 places.

5/7 ≈ 0.714, 3/4 = 0.75 → 5/7 is smaller.

Method 3 – The “Gap” Approach

For fractions close to 1, see how far from 1 they are.

11/12 vs 14/15: 11/12 is 1/12 short, 14/15 is 1/15 short. Since 1/12 > 1/15, 11/12 < 14/15. No cross multiplication needed.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Which is larger: 13/14 or 15/16? Use the “gap” approach mentally. (

Answer: 15/16 is larger because 1/14 > 1/16.)

4. Operations That Cost You Marks

Addition/Subtraction – The LCM Trap

To add ⅓ + ¼, do not add numerators and denominators. Find LCM of 3 and 4 = 12.

⅓ = 4/12, ¼ = 3/12, sum = 7/12.

If you ever mistakenly added 1+1 and 3+4 to get 2/7, you’ve just given away marks. Remember: Denominator is the name of the game; change the name before you play.

Multiplication – Simple Pizza Logic

⅔ × 4/5 means ⅔ of 4/5. Multiply straight: (2×4)/(3×5) = 8/15. “Of” means multiply.

Division – The KFC Mnemonic (Weird but Unforgettable)

Dividing fractions: Keep the first, Flip the second, Change sign to multiplication.

KFC – Keep Flip Change. Imagine Colonel Sanders holding a fraction upside down. It’s silly, and you’ll never forget it.

Example: ⅔ ÷ 4/5 → Keep ⅔, Flip 4/5 to 5/4, Change ÷ to × → ⅔ × 5/4 = 10/12 = ⅚.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Solve 2/5 ÷ 3/7 using KFC.

Answer: 2/5 × 7/3 = 14/15.

5. Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions – The MAD Transformation

To convert a mixed number like 3⅖ to an improper fraction, use MAD: Multiply, Add, Denominator stays.

Multiply whole number (3) × denominator (5) = 15. Add numerator (2) = 17. Keep denominator 5 → 17/5.

MAD: Multiply, Add, Denominator (the same). It’s mad, but it works every time.

Reverse: 17/5. Divide 17 by 5 → 3 remainder 2 → 3⅖.

Decimal to Mixed Number:

3.75 = 3 + 0.75 = 3 + 75/100 = 3 + 3/4 = 3¾. Always simplify the fraction part.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Convert 7.2 to a mixed number in simplest form.

Answer: 7 + 2/10 = 7 + 1/5 = 7⅕.

6. Recurring Decimals to Fractions – The VIP Shortcut

You’ll frequently see 0.3̅ , 0.2̅1̅ in exams. Memorise this:

For a pure recurring decimal like 0.a̅b̅c̅… (all digits repeat), put the repeating block over as many 9s as digits.

0.6̅ = 6/9 = 2/3.

0.2̅3̅ = 23/99.

0.1̅4̅2̅8̅5̅7̅ = 142857/999999 = 1/7 (yes, that’s a known magic fraction).

For mixed recurring (some digits non-repeating): Formula exists, but at foundation level, simply practice the direct conversion: 0.1̅6̅ = (16 – 1)/90 = 15/90 = 1/6. Wait – better to present the standard trick:

🧠
For 0.1̅6̅ (one non-repeating digit, two repeating digits): numerator = (entire number after decimal without bar – non-repeating part) = 16 – 1 = 15; denominator = as many 9s as repeating digits (2) followed by as many zeros as non-repeating digits (1) = 900? Actually careful: 0.1̅6̅ has 1 non-repeating (1) and repeating block "6" (length 1). So denominator = one 9 and one 0 = 90. So 16-1=15 -> 15/90 = 1/6. Yes. So numerator = (all digits after decimal up to first repeat) – non-repeating part, denominator = (9s for repeat length)(0s for non-repeat length). I'll include this as a high-yield trick without overcomplicating.

Multiplying decimals: Ignore the decimal point, multiply as whole numbers, then put the decimal back. Count total decimal places in both numbers.

In Simple Words

🧒Feynman Explanation – The Hardest Thing Made Easy
🧠
Imagine you and your friend Riya find a huge chocolate bar. It’s divided into 8 equal pieces, but the wrapper says the whole bar is 1. You ate some earlier, so now you have ¾ of the bar left. That means 6 pieces out of 8. Riya says, “Let’s share these ¾ equally between 3 people.” That’s easy – you just divide 6 pieces by 3, giving each person 2 pieces. But what’s that as a fraction? ¾ ÷ 3 = ¼. Because 3 people each get ¼ of the whole bar. You didn’t even flip anything! You just thought: “How many wholes do I have? 0.75. Divide by 3, get 0.25.” Simple.
🧠
Now here’s the tricky part that makes students scratch their heads until they’re bald: what if you’re dividing a fraction by another fraction? Like, you have ½ a bucket of water, and each small plant needs ⅓ of a bucket. How many plants can you water?
🧠
Suddenly you’re not dividing by a nice whole number like 3. You’re dividing by ⅓. The question is: how many ⅓ buckets fit into ½ bucket? A third of a bucket is smaller than a half bucket, so surely more than one plant? Let’s test: ½ bucket = 0.5, ⅓ ≈ 0.333. 0.5 ÷ 0.333 = 1.5. That means one full plant and a half-plant worth. So 1½ plants. But how do we get that without decimals? Flip and multiply.

The Unforgettable Trick: The KFC Recipe (Keep, Flip, Change)

Keep the first fraction exactly as it is.

Flip the second fraction upside down.

Change the division sign to multiplication.

½ ÷ ⅓ → Keep ½, Flip ⅓ to ³⁄₁ (which is 3), Change ÷ to ×. Now: ½ × 3 = ³⁄₂ = 1½. It works! And here’s the story: Colonel Sanders isn’t just for chicken—he’s the guardian of fraction division. Picture him in his white suit, holding a bucket with a fraction inside, flipping it over a frying pan. The “Keep” part is the chicken you don’t touch, the “Flip” is the piece you toss, and the “Change” is the secret spice turning division into multiplication.

🧠
Why does flipping work? (The gut-feel explanation)

Dividing by a number is asking “how many of that number fit into the other.” When you divide by a fraction smaller than 1, the answer is bigger than the original. Flipping the divisor and multiplying is just a quick math trick that does the same job as slicing and counting pieces. It’s like using a map: instead of walking the whole path, you scale it. The flip is the scale factor.

Story: The Pani Puri Party

🧠
You’re making pani puri for your 12 friends. You have ⅔ of a litre of spicy water. Each puri needs ⅙ of a litre to burst perfectly. How many puris can you serve? ⅔ ÷ ⅙. Keep ⅔, Flip ⅙ → 6/1 (which is 6), Change to ×. ⅔ × 6 = 12. Exactly 12 puris! Your friends cheer. You didn’t even break a sweat.
