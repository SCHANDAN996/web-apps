Chapter Notes

Chapter Outline & Learning Goals

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

The ₹20 Sweet Trap (Your Hook)

🧠
A young boy bought a box of sweets for ₹200 and sold it to his friend for ₹220, thinking he made a ₹20 profit. The friend later sold the same box for ₹240. The boy said, “I made ₹20, he made ₹20 — so both earned equally.” Is that true? The boy’s cost was ₹200, profit ₹20 → 10% profit. The friend’s cost was ₹220, profit ₹20 → only about 9% profit. Same profit in rupees, but completely different in percentage. This tiny shift in viewpoint causes most of the confusion in Profit & Loss. Let’s clear it forever.

I Know You Find It Tricky — And That’s Okay

🧠
If you ever thought “Profit % is on SP” or “Discount is on CP,” you are not alone. Almost every learner makes these mistakes at first. Our goal is to turn this into an unbreakable mental habit, with weird memory tricks and bite‑sized logic. Ready? Let’s walk step by step.

1. The Two Pillars: CP and SP

Cost Price (CP): The price you pay to acquire or make something.

Selling Price (SP): The price at which you finally sell it.

Profit = SP − CP (when SP > CP)

Loss = CP − SP (when CP > SP)

2. The Percentage Rule That Everyone Gets Wrong

The percentage of profit or loss is always calculated on the Cost Price — never on the Selling Price.

Profit % = (Profit ÷ CP) × 100

Loss % = (Loss ÷ CP) × 100

🧠
Example: CP = ₹200, sold for ₹250. Profit = ₹50, Profit % = (50/200)×100 = 25%. Why 25%? Because the ₹50 extra is a quarter of the original cost.
🧠 Active Recall: If CP = ₹500 and SP = ₹400, what is the loss %? (Loss = ₹100, so (100/500)×100 = 20% loss.)

Weird Memory Trick (Mnemonic):

Think of CP as “Child’s Piggy bank” and SP as “Show‑off Price.” A child knows exactly how much money was in the piggy bank (CP). Whatever extra she gets is a percentage of that starting money, not of the flashy price tag. So chant: “Percent always on the Piggy bank, not the Show‑off!” — weird but sticky.

3. The Super Shortcut: Assume CP = 100

When you see a percentage like “10% profit” or “25% loss,” immediately imagine CP = ₹100. Then:

At 10% profit, SP = ₹110.

At 25% loss, SP = ₹75.

This unlocks ratios: CP : SP = 100 : (100±P). You can now solve most problems without ever touching fractions.

🧠 Active Recall: A shopkeeper sells an item at 20% profit. If SP is ₹480, find CP. (Assume CP=100, SP=120. So 120 → ₹480, thus 100 → CP = (480/120)×100 = ₹400.)

4. Marked Price & Discount — The Dressing Room Drama

Marked Price (MP) is the price tag a seller puts on an item — often inflated. Discount is always given on MP.

SP = MP − Discount (or SP = MP × (100 − Discount%)/100)

Profit/Loss is still calculated on CP.

Mnemonic: “Discount De‑tags the MP, but Profit measures on CP.” Imagine the price tag (MP) is a balloon; discount pricks it to SP, but your cost (CP) never changes its core.

🧠 Active Recall: CP = ₹600, MP = ₹800, Discount = 10%. Find Profit %.

SP = 800 × 90% = ₹720. Profit = 720−600 = ₹120. Profit % = (120/600)×100 = 20%.

5. The Deadly Trap: Same Selling Price, Equal % Profit & Loss

If you sell two items at the same price, one at x% profit and the other at x% loss, you ALWAYS suffer an overall loss. The loss % = (x²/100)%.

Example: x = 20%, loss = (20²/100)% = 4% loss. Try it — you’ll never get a no‑profit‑no‑loss.

🧠
Why? Because the loss is on a higher CP (the item sold at loss has a larger CP than the one sold at profit). The math hides a little monster.
🧠 Active Recall: Two watches sold for ₹990 each, one at 10% profit, the other at 10% loss. Overall result? (Loss % = (10²/100)% = 1% loss)

6. When Profit Equals Loss in Rupees (Two Different SPs)

Sometimes the question says: “Selling at ₹800 gives as much profit as selling at ₹600 gives loss.” Then CP is simply the average of the two SPs: CP = (800+600)/2 = ₹700. A gift from algebra.

🧠 Active Recall: Selling a book for ₹450 gives as much loss as selling for ₹510 gives profit. CP? ( (450+510)/2 = ₹480 )

In Simple Words

🧒Feynman – English

Imagine two brothers, Ravi and Raj, who each have a box of Pokémon cards. They decide to sell their boxes. Ravi sells his box for ₹100. Raj also sells his box for ₹100. They both get the same money.

Now, here's the curious part. Ravi had bought his box for ₹80, so he made a ₹20 profit. That's a 25% profit because ₹20 extra on ₹80 is a quarter extra. But Raj had bought his box for ₹125, so he sold it for ₹100 and lost ₹25. That's a 20% loss.

🧠
Ravi says, "I made 25% profit," and Raj says, "I lost 20%." If we just average the percentages, we might think they broke even, or maybe made a little. But let's actually count the real money. Total money they put in: ₹80 (Ravi) + ₹125 (Raj) = ₹205. Total money they got: ₹100 + ₹100 = ₹200. They lost ₹5 together! Even though they both sold at the same price, and the profit and loss percentages were different here. But what if the percentages were the same number? Like both 20%? That's the sneaky exam trick.
🧠
Let's try a simpler story. A girl, Maya, has two identical teddy bears. She sells each for ₹120. On one teddy, she makes a 20% profit. On the other, she has a 20% loss. She thinks, "I sold both for the same price, profit 20% and loss 20%, so I must have come out even." Is that true? No, she lost money!
🧠
Why? Let's find the secret. For the teddy she sold at 20% profit, ₹120 is 120% of what she paid. So her cost for that teddy was ₹100 (because 100 + 20% = 120). For the teddy she sold at 20% loss, ₹120 is 80% of what she paid. So her cost was ₹150 (because 150 – 20% = 120). Total cost: ₹100 + ₹150 = ₹250. Total selling price: ₹120 + ₹120 = ₹240. She lost ₹10. That's a 4% loss on her total cost (10/250 = 4%).

Notice the magic: 20% profit and 20% loss gave a 4% loss. 4 is the square of 2 (since 20% is 20, square of 20 is 400, but the square of the number (20) divided by 100 gives 4). This always happens: if you have x% profit and x% loss on two items with the same selling price, you always lose (x²/100)%. Because the cost of the loss item is higher, the loss in rupees is bigger than the profit in rupees.

Never-forget trick: Imagine a seesaw. The item with loss sits closer to the fulcrum but is heavier (higher cost), and the profit item sits farther but is lighter (lower cost). They sell for the same price, but the heavy side (loss) tips the balance, so overall it's a loss. Or just remember: "Same SP, opposite same %; square the number and lose that %". Or a rhyme: "Profit and loss same percent, money spent won't be what you get. Square the rate, divide by a hundred, that's the loss that always blunders."

So next time you see a question like "Two articles sold for ₹960 each, one at 20% profit, the other at 20% loss," you can instantly shout: "Loss = (20×20)/100 = 4% loss!" No need to find costs. That's the trick.

🧠
Question for you: If a shopkeeper sells two kites for ₹625 each, one at 25% profit and the other at 25% loss, what is his overall loss percentage? (Think squares, don't calculate costs.)

Short Tricks

🪄Short Tricks – English
🪄
Trick 1: The Piggy Bank Rule – “Percent always on CP, not on Show‑off SP”

Imagine a piggy bank (your Cost Price) is the only place you count your extra pennies. The flashy Selling Price (Show‑off SP) is just the outside sticker. So whenever you hear “profit %”, immediately think “on the piggy bank”. This kills the biggest exam trap — calculating % on SP.

🪄
Trick 2: The “100” Magic Pillow

Whenever you see a percentage, lay your head on CP = 100.

Then SP = 100 ± P (profit/loss). This turns any problem into a simple ratio.

“CP 100 is my nap time, all numbers fall in line.”

🪄
Trick 3: The Seesaw of Doom – Same SP, equal % profit & loss
🧠
Picture two kids on a seesaw: one is light (profit item, lower CP) and the other heavy (loss item, higher CP). They sell for the same price, but the heavy kid slams the ground. Result? Always a loss.

Loss% = (x²/100)%. Remember: “Square the rate, divide by a hundred, that’s the loss that always thunders.”

🪄
Trick 4: The Balloon and the Pig – Discount on MP, Profit on CP

Imagine a balloon (Marked Price) floating high. Discount is a pin that pricks it, bringing it down to Selling Price. But the pig (CP) stays on the ground, and you measure profit only from the pig’s belly.

“Prick the balloon, not the pig; profit comes from the pig, discount deflates the balloon.”

🪄
Trick 5: Cheater’s Scale – Dishonest Shopkeeper

A shopkeeper uses a false weight. He gives you 900 gm but charges for 1000 gm. Profit % = (error / true weight – error) × 100.

Remember “What he steals (error) over what he gives (true – error)”. Imagine a thief stealing sugar from a bag — the profit is on the amount he actually handed over.

🪄
Trick 6: The Article Swap – CP of x = SP of y

When you see “Cost price of 20 articles = selling price of 15 articles”, don’t sweat.

Think: “CP of many = SP of few” → Profit% = (many – few)/few × 100.

🧠
Or SP/CP = many/few. So if 20 CP = 15 SP, then SP/CP = 20/15 = 4/3 → 33.33% profit? Wait, check: SP/CP = 20/15 = 1.333, profit = 33.33%? Actually earlier we used formula profit% = (x - y)/y *100 where x is CP count, y SP count. So (20-15)/15=33.33%. Yes. Mnemonic: “Big CP number, small SP number — profit is (big – small) over small.”
🪄
Trick 7: The Average Bridge – Equal profit & loss in Rupees

If selling at two different prices results in equal amount of profit and loss, the CP is simply the average of the two selling prices. CP = (SP1 + SP2)/2. Imagine a bridge exactly in the middle of two hills — that’s your CP.

🪄
Trick 8: The Discount Chain – Successive Discounts
🧠
Two discounts? Don’t add them. Multiply the leftovers. Like a pizz

Back: take 20% off, then 10% off the remaining. Equivalent single discount = 100 – (100 × 0.8 × 0.9). “Remove, then remove again — multiply what’s left, then subtract from 100.”

🪄
Trick 9: The Marked Monster – MP to SP with profit crossing

MP → (discount) → SP → (profit) → CP. Walk backward: MP = SP / (1 – D%), CP = SP / (1 + P%). “MP is the giant, SP is the middle man, CP the root.”

🪄
Trick 10: Profit in Rupees = Selling Price of Given Number

“By selling 33 meters, profit = SP of 11 meters.” Instantly, profit% = (profit in units / (total units – profit units)) × 100. So 11/(33-11) = 11/22 = 50%. “Profit units over (total – profit) units.”

🪄
Trick 11: CP from two SP with different %

When one SP gives loss and another gives profit, set up equations: SP1 = CP – L, SP2 = CP + P. If given differences, CP = (difference in rupees) / (difference in percentages). Example: SP1 gives 10% loss, SP2 gives 5% profit, and difference is ₹x, then CP = x / 0.15. “Difference in cash over difference in percent.”

🪄
Trick 12: CP:MP Ratio from Profit and Discount

If profit% = P, discount% = D, then CP:MP = (100 – D) : (100 + P). “(100 – Discount) brother, (100 + Profit) mother, the ratio is undercover.”

🪄
Trick 13: Milk and Water – Profit from Adulteration

When a milkman adds water and sells at CP, profit% = (added quantity / original pure quantity) × 100. If profit% = 25%, then water:milk = 25:100 = 1:4. “Water is profit, milk is cost — ratio is profit percent over 100.”

🪄
Trick 14: Fake Weight – Gain% = (Error / Given) × 100

If using weight 800 gm instead of 1000 gm, error = 200, given = 800, gain% = 25%. “The gain is the missing piece over the piece you actually handed.”

🪄
Trick 15: Overall Profit after Partial Sales

If you sell part of stock at different profits, use weighted average. Think of each part as a separate character with its own profit percentage, then combine them like a recipe. The weighted SP = Σ (fraction × SP factor). “Mix the parts, don’t just average the percents.”
