📖Content — Percentage (English)

In this chapter, you will learn:

  • The real meaning of "percent" (hint: it’s just a fraction with a bottom of 100).
  • How to switch effortlessly between percentages, fractions, and decimals.
  • How to find a percentage of any number without fear.
  • How to handle percentage increase and decrease like a pro.
  • One weird memory trick that makes the whole chapter stick permanently.

The Sale That Almost Tricked You

🧠
Picture this: It’s Diwali. A shop screams “50% off.” Next to it, another shop says “Buy one, get one free.” They look identical. But are they? One savvy shopper quickly calculates: 50% off means price becomes half. Buy one get one free also means two items for the price of one — again, price per item becomes half. For the single-item buyer, they are the same. For a bulk buyer, the second might have a hidden trap (you must buy two!). This is the subtle magic of percentage — it’s everywhere, and once you truly understand it, you turn from a confused buyer into a money-saving ninja. Today, you’ll gain that power.

You are not alone if your brain freezes at the sight of “25% of 160”. Many learners see the % symbol and panic because it feels abstract. In truth, percentage is nothing but a “fraction spoken differently.” It’s a language of comparison. Think of it this way: If I say “I scored 17 out of 20,” it’s not instantly clear how good that is. But if I say “85%”, your mind immediately cues: “Excellent!” Percentage standardizes everything into a scale of 100. It’s like measuring height in centimetres — a common ruler for every number. The moment you treat percentage as “out of 100,” the mystery dissolves.

🧠 Pause and Recall: Before reading further, speak aloud: What does “per cent” literally mean? (

Answer: per hundred. “Cent” comes from Latin centum, meaning hundred — like century, centipede.)

Chunk 1: The Per-100 Machine

Imagine a magical machine. You feed it any fraction, and it transforms the denominator to exactly 100. The numerator then becomes the percentage. That’s exactly what percentage does.

  • To convert a fraction into percentage: Multiply by 100 → 100.
  • Example: ⅗ = (⅗) × 100% = (3×100)/5 % = 300/5 % = 60%.

In the opposite direction, to get a percentage back to its simplest fraction, you just drop the % sign and write the number over 100, then simplify.

  • 40% = 40/100 = 2/5.

It’s that mechanical. No magic — just a reliable machine.

🧠 Quick Recall: Convert 12% into a fraction in its lowest terms right now. (

Solution: 12/100 = 3/25.)

Chunk 3: Finding Percentage of a Quantity

The phrase “X% of Y” translates to (X/100) × Y. The word “of” means multiplication.

🧠
Example: What is 30% of 240?

30% of 240 = (30/100) × 240 = (3/10) × 240 = 3 × 24 = 72.

🪄
Shortcut when Y ends with zeroes: cancel zeroes first. 20% of 350 = (20/100)×350 = (1/5)×350 = 70. You’ll feel the speed.
🧠 Recall Check: Without a calculator, find 15% of 200. (Hint: 10% = 20, 5% = 10, so 15% = 30. Or use fraction: 15/100 × 200 = 15 × 2 = 30.)

Chunk 4: Percentage Change (Increase & Decrease)

When a price goes from old to new, the percentage change is always calculated on the original quantity.

Formul

Back: % change = (Change / Original) × 100.

If the result is positive, it’s increase; if negative, it’s decrease.

Example: A phone’s price jumped from ₹12,000 to ₹15,000. The increase is ₹3,000 on the original ₹12,000.

% increase = (3000/12000) × 100 = (1/4) × 100 = 25%.

Now try decrease: A salary is reduced from ₹50,000 to ₹40,000. Change = -10,000 on 50,000 → (-10000/50000) × 100 = -20%. So 20% decrease.

🧠 Recall Scenario: A fruit seller’s sales rose from 80 kg to 100 kg. What is the percentage increase? (

Answer: (20/80)×100 = 25%.)

Chunk 5: Quick Successive Change Intuition (Foundation Visual)

If a number first increases by 10% and then the new number increases by another 20%, the total effect is not 30%. Use the formul

Back: Net effect = A + B + (A×B)/100. So 10 + 20 + (10×20)/100 = 30 + 2 = 32% increase. For two successive discounts of 20% and 10%, the net discount = -20 -10 + (200)/100 = -30 + 2 = -28% (i.e., 28% discount). Memorise the “plus AB over 100” formula, good for multiple changes.

🧠 Apply: Find the net change when a value first increases by 25% and then decreases by 20%. (25 - 20 + (25×-20)/100 = 5 - 5 = 0%. So no change, back to original.)
🧠
Chunk 6: Checking the Trap — “Percent of What?”
🧠
The most dangerous word in percentage is “of.” Changing the base changes everything. If a student scored 60% marks in Test A (out of 50) and then 40% in Test B (out of 100), what’s the overall percentage? You cannot simply average 60% and 40%. You must find total marks obtained: 60% of 50 = 30, 40% of 100 = 40. Total marks = 70 out of 150 → (70/150)×100 = 46.67%. The average (60+40)/2 = 50% is wrong because the bases are unequal. Always find actual values when bases differ. Exams love this trap.
🧠 Think: A shirt’s price was increased by 25% and then the new price decreased by 20%. Is the final price more, less, or equal to the original? (Equal, as we saw earlier.)

In Simple Words

🧒Feynman — The Yo-Yo Trap (English)

Imagine your grandmother gives you ₹100 as pocket money. You’re thrilled. Then your father says, “Good job on your exams! I’m increasing your pocket money by 20%.” Wow! You now get ₹120.

🧠
Next month, however, you leave the TV on all night. Your father frowns. “Punishment: your pocket money is reduced by 20%.” You think, “No problem, 20% up, 20% down — I’m back to ₹100.” You count the money: ₹120 minus 20%. 20% of 120 is 24, so ₹120 – ₹24 = ₹96. Wait — ₹96? That’s ₹4 less than what Granny gave you! You just got Yo-Yoed.
🧠
Why did this happen? Because the 20% increase was on ₹100, but the 20% decrease was on the new, bigger amount ₹120. So the decrease took away more chips from a bigger pile. The percent rule is: never add or subtract percentages like ordinary numbers when they happen one after the other on different bases. It’s like eating half a small pizza and then half a large pizza — the halves aren’t equal because the pizzas changed size.

Here’s the trick that will stick forever: convert percentages into multipliers.

  • For a p% increase, multiply by (1 + p/100).
  • For a q% decrease, multiply by (1 – q/100).

Then just chain-multiply the starting number.

🧠
So ₹100 → 20% up → multiply by 1.20 → ₹120. Then 20% down → multiply that by 0.80 → ₹120 × 0.80 = ₹96. The two multipliers (1.20 × 0.80 = 0.96) tell you that you end up at 96% of the original — a net 4% loss every time, no matter what you start with. If both up-down percentages are the same number r, the net loss is always (r²/100)%. Here r=20, so r²=400, divided by 100 gives 4% loss. Magic, right?

Remember this image: a Yo-Yo that goes up 20% of its string length and then down 20% — but it never comes back to your hand exactly; it stops a little lower. That’s the Yo-Yo Trap. Whenever you see back-to-back percent changes, don’t trust your gut — trust the multiplier chain.

🧠
Now, try this: If a video game price first drops by 50% and then, in a strange twist, increases by 50% on the sale price, does it return to the original? Calculate with multipliers and tell me the net change.

Short Tricks

🪄Short Tricks — Percentage (English)
🪄
Trick 1: Centipede’s Century Board

Picture a bright-yellow centipede with 100 tiny legs, each wearing a sock labeled 1/100. Whenever you see %, the centipede lifts a wooden board that says "/100". So 45% becomes 45/100 instantly. “Cent” = 100 (century, centipede). The weird image of a centipede lifting a board makes the word “percent” unforgettable.

🪄
Trick 2: The 100-Floor Elevator

To convert any fraction to percent, imagine the numerator is a person and the denominator is the elevator’s floor. The person wants to go to floor 100. The elevator multiplies the fraction by 100. Example: 3/5 — the person (3) takes the elevator: (3×100)/5 = 300/5 = 60%. Always “multiply by 100 and divide.” The elevator always reaches the 100th floor.

🪄
Trick 3: The Quarterback’s Throne

25% = 1/4. Imagine a quarterback (American football) sitting on a throne, because a quarter is 25 cents, and he’s the “quarter” back. So whenever you see 25%, you see a quarterback throwing a 1/4-shaped ball. Similarly, 50% = 1/2: a half-eaten cake at a 50th birthday party with “Half-century” written on it. 75% = 3/4: three quarters of a pizza, so a pizza with 3 slices left out of 4.

🪄
Trick 4: The Pizza Slicer for 12.5%

12.5% = 1/8. Imagine a giant pizza cut into 8 slices. You take one slice, and the waiter shouts, “You took 12.5 percent!” Because an 8-slice pizza makes each slice 1/8. Remember 12.5 is half of 25 (1/4), and twice 4 gives 8. So 12.5% = 1/8. If in doubt, picture the pizza slicer dividing the circle into 8.

🪄
Trick 5: The Three-Eyed Monster for 1/3 and 33.33%

1/3 = 33.33%. Visualize a three-eyed monster; each eye represents 33.33% of its face’s vision. Together they make 100%. When you think 1/3, immediately see that monster’s three floating 33.33s. And 2/3 = 66.67% — two of its eyes open.

🪄
Trick 6: “X’s of Y” Become a Multiplication Dance

“X% of Y” translates to (X/100) × Y. Imagine the percentage sign as a dance instruction: “Divide me by 100, then multiply with Y.” Picture a dance floor where the % symbol spins, cuts itself into a fraction over 100, and then grabs Y and multiplies. So 30% of 200: 30/100 × 200 = 30×2 = 60. The “of” becomes multiplication music.

🪄
Trick 7: The Jealous Original (for Increase/Decrease)

In % change, the denominator is always the original number. The original is “jealous” and demands that all changes are measured relative to it. If price goes from 50 to 70, change = 20, original = 50, % = (20/50)×100 = 40%. The story: “The original says, ‘You left me, but I’m still the reference point!’” Always base on the jealous original.

🪄
Trick 8: A + B + AB/100 — The “AB on the Hundredth Floor”

For two successive changes of A% and B%, net = A + B + (A×B)/100. Imagine A and B are two friends who meet on the 100th floor. First they add normally (A+B), then they multiply their initials (A×B) and put that product on floor 100 (divide by 100). If either is negative, the product will be negative. For +20% and -10%, net = 20 -10 + (20×-10)/100 = 10 -2 = +8%. The “AB elevator” always stops at the 100th floor.

🪄
Trick 9: Yo-Yo Trap — The r²/100 Ghost

If a value increases by r% and then decreases by the same r%, you always lose (r²/100)%. Think of a Yo-Yo that goes down and up with the same string length, but never lands exactly in your palm; it stops a tiny bit lower. For r=20, loss = 400/100 = 4%. The ghost of the square steals that percent. Every time you see equal up-down, whisper “r-square ghost steals 4 percent” (adjust to r).

🪄
Trick 10: Multiplier Masks

Instead of thinking in % changes, think in multipliers. An increase is a “growth mask”: (1 + p/100). A decrease is a “shrink mask”: (1 - q/100). To string them, just wear masks in sequence. For +25% put on mask 1.25, for -20% put on mask 0.80. Net mask = 1.25 × 0.80 = 1.00 = no change. Imagine a masquerade ball where each change gives you a mask; multiply to see your final face.

🪄
Trick 11: Decimal Tango — Two Steps Right, Two Steps Left

Decimal to percent: shift decimal two places right (like dancing two tango steps right). 0.45 → 45%. Percent to decimal: shift two steps left (back). 7% → 0.07. Add zeros if needed. Picture a couple dancing: “Right-right for % rise, Left-left for decimal disguise.”

🪄
Trick 12: 1% Lazy Walker

To find 1% of any number, the decimal point walks two lazy steps left. 1% of 2500: from 2500. → 25.00 (25). It’s as if the number goes to sleep and the decimal tiptoes two steps to the left. Then for 2%, double that, etc.

🪄
Trick 13: The Bounce-Back Boiler

If a price is reduced by p%, to restore it you must increase by not p%, but (p/(100-p))×100%. Think of a basketball: you compress it by a percentage, and to bounce back to original height, you need a bigger percentage because the new base is smaller. For 20% drop, bounce-back = (20/(80))×100 = 25%. So a “20% off” sticker means to undo the discount, you’d need a 25% hike. The ball bounces back higher on a percentage scale.

🪄
Trick 14: Apple-Orange Slow Dance (Different Bases)

You can’t average percentages with different bases; you must get actual values. Two dancers—Apple and Orange—hold cards with different “out of” numbers. They can only slow dance if you first convert to actual scores, sum them, then find total %. Otherwise, mixing them is a forbidden fruit salad. Exam traps: always find the real fruits.

🪄
Trick 15: Golden Key Rings

Memorize these fraction-percent pairs by putting them on imaginary key rings:

  • 1/2 = 50% (half a century key)
  • 1/4 = 25% (quarterback key)
  • 1/8 = 12.5% (pizza slice key)
  • 1/10 = 10% (dime key)
  • 1/20 = 5% (nickel key)
  • 1/12 = 8.33% (clock key: a clock has 12 hours, each hour is 8.33% of the day)
  • 1/11 ≈ 9.09% (two large 9s like twin towers on a 11-shaped base).

Whenever you see these percentages, grab the right key from your mental keychain.

Previous Year Questions

Q1. [SSC CGL Pattern] Income, Expenditure & Savings Trap A man spends 75% of his income. His income increases by 20% and his expenditure also increases by 10%. What is the percentage increase in his savings? Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Let initial income = ₹100. Initial expenditure = 75% of 100 = ₹75. Initial savings = 100 – 75 = ₹25. New income = 100 × 1.20 = ₹120. New expenditure = 75 × 1.10 = ₹82.50. New savings = 120 – 82.50 = ₹37.50. Increase in savings = 37.50 – 25 = ₹12.50. Percentage increase in savings = (12.50 / 25) × 100 = 50%. Hook/Trap Analysis: Candidates erroneously net the percentages (20% – 10% = 10%). The trap exploits the urge to apply direct percentage difference instead of computing absolute values for the components (savings here is a remainder, not a directly given sum). ---
Q2. [SSC CGL 2023] Successive Change — The Yo-Yo Trap The price of a book is first increased by 10% and then decreased by 5%. What is the net change in the price? Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Net change formul Back: A + B + (A × B)/100. Here, A = +10, B = –5. Net % change = 10 + (–5) + (10 × –5)/100 = 5 – 0.5 = 4.5% increase. Hook/Trap Analysis: The mental shortcut "10 – 5 = 5% increase" overestimates the result. The trap is ignoring the interaction term (A×B)/100 — the 5% decrease applies to an already inflated base, so its absolute effect shrinks slightly. The multiplier approach (1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045) confirms. ---
Q3. [Banking Exam Pattern] Election & Invalid Votes Trap In an election between two candidates, one got 55% of the total valid votes. 20% of the total votes were invalid. If the total number of votes was 7500, what was the number of valid votes that the other candidate got? Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Total votes = 7500. Invalid votes = 20% of 7500 = 1500. Valid votes = 7500 – 1500 = 6000. Candidate 1 got 55% of valid votes = 0.55 × 6000 = 3300. Candidate 2 got the remaining: 6000 – 3300 = 2700. Hook/Trap Analysis: Candidates mistakenly calculate 55% of the total (7500 × 0.55 = 4125) and subtract from 7500, ignoring the invalid votes entirely. The word "valid" is deliberately placed to deceive. Always apply the percentage to the correct sub-base (valid votes). ---
Q4. [SSC CGL Pattern] Fraction-Percent & Reverse Calculation A student multiplied a number by 3/5 instead of 5/3. What is the percentage error in the calculation? Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Let the number be x. Correct result = x × (5/3) = 5x/3. Wrong result = x × (3/5) = 3x/5. Error = |5x/3 – 3x/5| = (25x – 9x)/15 = 16x/15. Percentage error = (Error / Correct) × 100 = (16x/15) ÷ (5x/3) × 100 = (16x/15) × (3/5x) × 100 = (48/75) × 100 = 0.64 × 100 = 64%. Hook/Trap Analysis: The common mistake is placing the error over the wrong value as denominator instead of the correct value. Some candidates also incorrectly add/subtract fractions instead of multiplying. The 64% result often surprises, highlighting the large impact of a small fractional inversion. ---
Q5. [SSC GD Pattern] Venn Diagram & Two-Subject Pass Percentage In an examination, 30% of total students failed in Hindi, 45% failed in English, and 20% failed in both subjects. Find the percentage of those who passed in both subjects. Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Failed in at least one subject: 30 + 45 – 20 = 55%. Passed in both = 100% – 55% = 45%. Hook/Trap Analysis: The trap is forgetting to subtract the intersection (both subjects) when adding failure percentages. If candidates add 30 + 45 = 75, then calculate 100 – 75 = 25, leading to a wrong answer. The set theory principle "Union = A + B – Intersection" is essential. ---
Q6. [SSC CGL Pattern] Marks-Based Pass/Fail Trap A student has to obtain 33% of the total marks to pass. He got 125 marks and failed by 40 marks. What are the maximum marks? Step-by-Step
Show Solution
✅ Answer:
💡 Let boys = 200, girls = 300 (total 500 students). Scholarship boys = 25% of 200 = 50. Scholarship girls = 30% of 300 = 90. Total scholarship holders = 140. Non-scholarship holders = 500 – 140 = 360. Percentage = (360/500) × 100 = 72%. Hook/Trap Analysis: First, candidates must convert the ratio to actual numbers (choosing a convenient total for percentage calculation). Second, the question asks for the percentage of non-scholarship holders, while the data given is for scholarship holders — a classic "reverse calculation" trap. Missing the complement (100% – scholarship%) will yield the wrong answer.
