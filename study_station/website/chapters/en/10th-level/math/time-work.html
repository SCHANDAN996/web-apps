I Know It Feels Tricky

🧠
You’re not alone. Most students look at “A does in 12 days, B does in 8 days” and their brain goes fuzzy. Fraction addition? Invert the answer? Multiply? Relax. The confusion is not you — it’s the way it’s usually taught. We are going to rebuild the concept from your own common sense.

Chunk 1: One Day’s Work – The Magic Ingredient

🧠
Think of the whole job as one juicy pizza. If you alone can finish that pizza in 6 days, how much do you eat in one day? Exactly 1/6 of the pizza. That’s your “one day’s work”. Everything in Time & Work orbits around this tiny number.

If your friend finishes the same pizza in 3 days, then her one day’s work is 1/3.

🧠
Together in one day you both eat 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2 of the pizza. So together you will finish the whole pizza in 2 days (because 1 ÷ (1/2) = 2). See? No magic.
🧠 Active Recall: A alone can do a job in 5 days, B alone in 10 days. Without looking, find their one day’s work individually, then together, then total time. Do it mentally now.

Chunk 2: The LCM–Total Work Superpower

Fractions are great, but when numbers get messy (like 7 and 13 days), adding fractions feels like chewing glass. That’s why exam masters invented the Total Work (LCM) Method.

Step-by-step:

Take the LCM of the given days. That becomes our “Total Work” — imagine it as the number of bricks to lay.

Divide Total Work by each person’s days to get their per-day Efficiency (bricks per day).

Now you’re just playing with whole numbers, no fractions.

Example: A takes 12 days, B takes 18 days. LCM of 12 and 18 is 36. Let total work = 36 units.

A’s efficiency = 36/12 = 3 units/day.

B’s efficiency = 36/18 = 2 units/day.

Together = 5 units/day → time = 36/5 = 7.2 days. Easy numbers, happy brain.

🧠 *Try it: A = 15 days, B = 10 days. Find LCM, efficiencies, and total time.

Answer: LCM 30, A eff=2, B eff=3, together 5, time 6 days.*

Chunk 3: Efficiency Ratio – The Silent Shortcut

🧠
Often data comes as “A is twice as efficient as B”. Immediately, efficiency ratio A : B = 2 : 1. And what is the relationship between efficiency and time? Efficiency is inversely proportional to time. So if A : B efficiency = 2 : 1, then time ratio A : B = 1 : 2. This one insight solves many ‘workers replaced’ problems in seconds.
🧠 Active Recall: If A is three times as efficient as B, and together they finish a job in 10 days, can you set up the ratio and find their individual days? Try before reading further.

Chunk 4: Alternate Days and Leaving/Joining

These are just logical extensions. If A and B work on alternate days, calculate work done in a cycle (usually 2 days) and then see how many full cycles fit. Remainder work is then done by the person whose turn it is. When a worker leaves before completion, simply add the work done together until the exit, then finish with the remaining person’s work. Always fall back on the LCM-Total Work method; it makes remainders child’s play.

🧠 *Active Recall: A does a job in 20 days. He works for 5 days and leaves. B finishes the remaining in 12 days. What is B’s individual time? (Hint: Find total work = 20, A’s eff=1, work left=15, B’s eff=15/12=1.25, so B’s time=20/1.25=16 days.) Do it now.*

Chunk 5: Wages and Work-Share

Wages are divided in the ratio of work done, not time spent. Since work done = efficiency × time spent, the ratio of wages equals the ratio of (efficiency × days) for each worker. If efficiencies are given, you can directly find each person’s share of money.

🧠 *A and B work together with efficiencies 3 and 2, finish a job in 5 days. Total wage is ₹500. What is A’s share? (Work ratio = 3:2, A gets 3/5 × 500 = ₹300.) Simple!*

In Simple Words

🧒Feynman Explains: The “Speed-Eater” Secret of Time & Work
🧠
Imagine you and your friend are having a pizza-eating race. Your friend can eat a whole pizza in 2 minutes flat. You, on the other hand, like to enjoy your food — you take 6 minutes to finish the same pizza. Who is the faster eater? Obviously, your friend. He is three times faster than you. But here’s the weird part: he spends less time, not more. So the faster someone works, the less time they take. That’s the magic rule — efficiency and time are like a seesaw. When one goes up, the other goes down.
🧠
Now let’s turn this into a real-life story. I once had a leaky garden hose. I needed to fill a bucket to water my plants. I had two taps: a fast kitchen tap that could fill the bucket in 3 minutes, and a slow garden tap that took a lazy 9 minutes. One day, I was in a hurry and used both taps together. How fast did the bucket fill? Let’s think like a smart kid. In 1 minute, the fast tap fills 1/3 of the bucket. The slow tap fills 1/9. Together, they fill 1/3 + 1/9 = 4/9 of the bucket in a minute. So the whole bucket fills in 9/4 = 2.25 minutes. But wait—that’s less than 3 minutes! How? Because the fast tap was doing most of the work, and the slow tap just helped a tiny bit. The faster someone (or something) is, the less time they need, and when they work together, the overall time becomes even shorter.
🧠
But here’s a trick that will blow your mind and make you never forget the seesaw. Think of two brothers, A and B. A is twice as fast as B. So A’s efficiency is 2, B’s is 1. Now, since A is faster, he should take half the time of B for the same job, right? Yes! If B takes 20 days, A takes 10 days. So the ratio of their times is the inverse (flipped) ratio of their efficiencies. Efficiency 2:1 → Time 1:2. That’s the trick: Efficiency Ratio : Time Ratio = 1:2 :: 2:1? No—just flip the fraction. Always flip. You can use this to solve almost any “A is x times more efficient” problem in 5 seconds.

Let’s make it unforgettable: Imagine a very unfair seesaw. On one side sits the fat cat “Efficiency” (big number), way up high. On the other side sits the small mouse “Time” (small number) down low. If the cat gets heavier (more efficient), the mouse flies even higher (time reduces). They are inverse buddies — always opposite. So next time you see “A is twice as efficient as B”, immediately picture the fat cat and the tiny mouse on a seesaw, and flip those numbers.

🧠
Now, a question for you: If A is three times as fast as B, and together they can finish building a Lego castle in 4 hours, how long would B take all by himself? Think with the seesaw in your mind. The answer might surprise you.

Short Tricks

🪄Unforgettable Mnemonics for Time & Work

1. The "WET" Formula

Work = Efficiency × Time

Imagine a WET cat that refuses to leave the bathtub. The longer it stays (Time) and the more vigorously it splashes (Efficiency), the more water (Work) it spills on the floor. So, Work equals Efficiency × Time — the WET cat makes a mess.

2. The Pizza Slice – One Day’s Work

One Day’s Work = 1 / Total Days

Picture a giant pizza cut into D slices. If you take D days to finish it, each day you eat exactly one slice — 1/D of the pizza. That’s your daily bite, and the whole pizza is the job.

3. Lazy Cat Method (LCM-Total Work)

L – LCM of the days

C – Calculate efficiency (Total Work ÷ Days)

F – Find combined efficiency (sum them)

T – Time = Total Work ÷ Combined efficiency

Visualize a Lazy Cat sleeping on a pile of bricks (Total Work). It yawns, counts the bricks (LCM), and lazily divides them among workers to see how fast they can finish. The cat’s name is LCFT (pronounced “licked”).

4. The Cookie Equation (Man‑Days Formula)

M₁D₁H₁ / W₁ = M₂D₂H₂ / W₂

Think of a massive cookie with layers: Men, Days, Hours, and Work. You must balance the cookie on a fork — both sides must be equal otherwise it crumbles. So, (Men¹ × Days¹ × Hours¹) / Work¹ equals the other side. That’s the Cookie Equation.

5. ET the Inverse (Efficiency & Time)

Efficiency ∝ 1/Time

Recall the movie E.

T. — the alien and Elliott are inseparable but inverse buddies: when one is up, the other is down. Here, E (Efficiency) and T (Time) are the same. If efficiency shoots up, time crashes down. "E.

T. is inverse."

6. The Seesaw of Doom (Ratio Inversion)

Efficiency Ratio a : b → Time Ratio b : a

Picture a seesaw with a fat cat (Efficiency) and a tiny mouse (Time). If the cat’s number is bigger, the mouse’s number is smaller. Just flip the ratio to switch between efficiency and time. The cat laughs, the mouse screams, but they always balance.

7. The Wage Whisperer

Wages ∝ Work Done (not time spent)

A rich boss pays workers by the weight of the bricks they laid, not by the hours they sat around. So, shout “Work = Wealth” — the one who does more work gets more money. Never split wages based on time; split them in the ratio of (Efficiency × Days worked).

8. Alternate Days – The Tango Trick

2‑day cycle work = (A + B)

Picture a couple dancing the Tango: A and B alternate turns. In one complete dance (2 beats), they cover a certain number of steps (work). To find how many such dances (cycles) fit into the full song, count the steps per dance, then add the leftover solo steps. “Tango in twos, then solo.”

9. MWB – My Weird Brother (Men, Women, Boys)

Set up the equation: (Men × M + Women × W + Boys × B) × Days = Total Work

Imagine your weird brother collects Marbles, Walnuts, and Buttons. He fills boxes by mixing them and asks you to calculate how many boxes he can make. Always equate the total collection from different days using the same weird formula. So, whenever you see men, women, boys — it’s My Weird Brother’s problem.

10. The Half‑Efficiency Headache

“Times more efficient” handles on efficiency, not time.

If someone says “A is 50% more efficient than B,” don’t halve the days! Instead, imagine a speed knob on a mixer: turning it to 150% increases the revolutions per minute, not the time. So, efficiency multiplies by 1.5, then time divides by that factor (inverse). Use the knob for efficiency, not time.

11. The Leaving/Joining Locomotive

Work done = (segment₁) + (segment₂) + …

Picture a train journey where people get on and off. At each station, the number of passengers changes. The total distance covered is the sum of distances each group travels. In work terms, each “leg” is (number of men × days they worked). So, “Train of work segments” — add them up to reach the destination.

12. Fraction‑Flipper for Together

If A+B together do 1/T work a day, total time = T

When you add fractions like 1/a + 1/b, the result is the combined one‑day work. Then just flip it to get the total days. Think of it as a pancake: the recipe says “mix 1/a cup and 1/b cup, then flip the pancake to cook the whole job.” The flip gives you the time.

13. The Unit‑less Wonder (LCM Method)

Work = LCM of days

When days are messy (like 7, 13), fractions become a nightmare. Instead, turn the work into bricks — the LCM of the days gives the exact number of bricks that each person’s pace fits perfectly. Now you’re in a brick‑counting world with no fractions, only whole numbers. Bricks are always better than fractions!

14. The Three‑Ring Circus (A, B, C Sums)

2(A+B+C) = Sum of the three pairs

For problems like “A and B together take 12 days, B and C 15, C and A 20,” imagine three acrobats holding hands in a circle. The sum of their pair‑holds, when added, gives twice the triple‑hold. So, add all pairs → halve it → individual = triple – complementary pair. It’s a circus trick you’ll never forget.

15. The “More is Less” Mantra

More efficiency → Less time; More men → Less days

Stand before a mirror and chant: “The more I am, the faster we finish.” If the number of workers multiplies, the days shrink proportionally. It’s the simple inverse‑proportion mirror. Whatever boosts the work‑rate on one side shrinks the time on the other. Mantr

Back: “More power, fewer hours.”
