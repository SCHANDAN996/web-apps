The Queen, the Bull’s Hide, and a City – A True Story of Mensuration

Around 800 BCE, a clever queen named Dido fled her homeland and arrived on the shores of North Africa. She asked the local ruler for a tiny piece of land — just as much as could be covered by a bull’s hide. The ruler laughed and agreed.

Queen Dido then cut the hide into extremely thin, long strips, tied them into a single rope, and used that rope to encircle a whole hill. That enclosed land became the heart of the great city of Carthage.

She didn’t just know shapes — she used the secret that for a given perimeter, a circle encloses the maximum area. That is the power of mensuration: the right shape can change your destiny.

Chunk 1: Perimeter – The Fence of Any Shape

Rectangle and Square

A rectangle has two lengths (l) and two breadths (b). Walk along its boundary, and you cover l + b + l + b = 2(l + b). For a square, all four sides are equal (side = a), so just walk 4 times the side: perimeter = 4a.

Triangle

Just add the three sides (a, b, c). Simple as that.

Circle (The Fancy Shape)

Now, a circle doesn’t have straight sides. The perimeter of a circle is called the circumference. Imagine wrapping a string exactly once around a bangle — the length of that string is the circumference.

Ancient people found that no matter the size of the circle, if you divide the circumference

(C) by the diameter (d), you always get a mysterious number slightly larger than 3 — which we now call π (pi, approx. 22/7 or 3.14).

Thus: C = πd, and since diameter = 2 × radius (r), we write C = 2πr.

Chunk 2: Area – The Carpet Inside

Area of a Rectangle

If you tile a rectangular floor, you multiply rows by columns: Area = length × breadth = l × b.

A square is just a special rectangle, so Area = a × a = a².

Area of a Triangle

Every triangle is exactly half of a rectangle that shares the same base and height. So, Area = (1/2) × base × height.

For a right-angled triangle, the two legs can be taken as base and height. For an equilateral triangle of side ‘a’, the height is (√3/2)a, so a shortcut formula pops up: Area = (√3/4) a².

Area of a Circle

Cut a circle into many thin slices (like pizza slices) and rearrange them alternately — they almost form a rectangle. The width becomes the radius (r), and the length becomes πr (half the circumference). Multiply them, and you get Area = πr². That’s why you see r², not r.

In Simple Words

Feynman-Technique Explanation: The Circle's Magic Number π

🧠
Alright, kid. You want to know the hardest thing in mensuration? It’s not really hard — it's just that nobody ever told you the story behind it. So sit with me. The hardest idea is this: Why on Earth does the area of a circle equal π times the radius squared? And why is the circumference 2πr? They look so similar that even grown-ups mix them up all the time. But I’ll show you a secret, and after today, you’ll never forget it — because we’re going to discover the truth just like the ancient pizza makers did.

The Pizza Chef Who Discovered π Without Knowing It

🧠
Once upon a time, there was a pizza chef named Giuseppe. He made perfect round pizzas every day. One day a new customer came and said, “I want a pizza, but I only pay for the actual cheese surface. How do you measure the amount of cheese?” Giuseppe scratched his head. He could measure the outer edge of the pizza — that’s the circumference — but the cheese covers the area inside. How are they connected?

Giuseppe had a brilliant idea. He took a pizza of radius r. He cut it into plenty of thin slices — just like cutting a pizza for a party — except he made them as thin as possible. Then he arranged the slices alternately: one pointing up, one pointing down, side by side. The weird shape that came out looked almost like a rectangle.

🧠
Now, what’s the length of that rectangle? The top zigzag edge is made up of all the crust pieces. But those crust pieces, taken together, are exactly half the circumference of the full pizza. (Because you arranged half facing up, half facing down.) So the length is half of the full outer circle, i.e., half of 2πr, which is πr.
🧠
And what’s the width of that rectangle? If you look closely, the height of each slice is just the distance from the crust to the pointy tip — that’s exactly the radius r of the pizza.

So Giuseppe ended up with a rectangle of length πr and width r. If you want the area of a rectangle, you multiply length by width. So the cheese-covered area is:

Area = πr × r = πr²

🧠
That’s it! The circle area formula is just a rectangle hiding in disguise. And the circumference? That’s simply the full outer boundary, which is 2πr. The factor 2 appears because the full circle edge is twice that half we used for the rectangle.

Now, here’s the trick you’ll never forget. Picture this silly scene:

For Are

Back: You bake a pie and you square it (even though pies are round). You look at it and shout, “These pies are squared!” That sounds exactly like π r² (pie r squared). So area = pie are squared, πr².

For Circumference: Now you need to protect two pies from your hungry dog. So you build a round fence around them. You think: “Two pies – I need a round fence.” That’s 2 π r (two pies r). Circumference = 2πr.

So whenever you see a circle, silently say:

🧠
“Is it the cheese (area)? Then pie are squared (πr²).
🧠
Is it the fence (circumference)? Then two pies round the fence (2πr).”

It is weird, it is funny, and it will stick in your brain like glue.

Short Tricks

1. Rectangle Area — "Long Bread"

Imagine a long loaf of bread (l × b). Whenever you need the area of a rectangle, picture yourself slicing a Long Bread into equal squares.

Area = L × B.

2. Rectangle Perimeter — "Two Long, Two Broad"

Walk all around a rectangular field. You'll pass two Long sides and two Broad sides. So you shout: "Two Longs, Two Broads; add them all!"

Perimeter = 2(L + B).

3. Triangle Area — "Half a Sandwich"

A triangle is exactly half of a rectangle sandwich with the same base and height. The recipe: take the whole sandwich (base × height) and eat half.

Area = ½ × base × height.

4. Circle Circumference — "Two Pies Are Round the Fence"

Two delicious pies (2π) are placed on a Round fence (r). So the fence length is 2πr.

Circumference = 2πr.

5. Circle Area — "Pie Are Squared"

For the area of the pie, shout "These pies are squared!" although pies are round. That sounds exactly like π r².

Area = πr².

6. Semicircle Perimeter — "Half a Pie, Plus the Line"

When taking half a pie (πr), never forget to add the straight cut (diameter = 2r). Picture a half-eaten pizza with its crust and the straight knife cut.

Perimeter of semicircle = πr + 2r.

7. Cone Volume — "One-Third its Cylinder Brother"

A cone is jealous of a cylinder with the same base and height because the cylinder holds three times as much. Remember, it takes exactly three cones to fill the cylinder.

Volume of cone = ⅓ πr²h.

8. Sphere Volume — "Four-Thirds Pie Are Cubic"

The sphere is a fat, cubic pie. Say it weirdly: "Four thirds pie are cubic" (4/3 π r³). The 'cubic' reminds you of the ³ on radius.

9. Sphere Surface Area — "Four Pie Are Square"

The surface of a sphere is made of four circular areas (πr²) laid out flat. Imagine unrolling the globe into four round pizzas.

Surface area = 4πr².

10. Cylinder CSA — "Two Pie Are High"

The curved surface is like a label wrapped around: its length is the circumference (2πr) and its height is h. So "two pie are high".

CSA = 2πrh.

11. Cylinder TSA — "Two Pie Are (Radius plus Height)"

The total area is the label (2πrh) plus two circular lids (2 × πr²). Factor 2πr out: 2πr (r + h). Think: "Two pie are r plus h".

12. Cone Slant Height & CSA — "Pie Roll"

The curved surface of a cone is a pie crust rolled out to a radius equal to slant height (l). So CSA = π r l. To find l, remember the right triangle inside the cone: l² = r² + h² (Pythagoras).

13. Equilateral Triangle Area — "Root Three Over Four, Side Square No More"

Sing this rhyme: "Root three over four, side square no more." It directly gives (√3/4) a². Picture a triangle with a square inside getting root-three treatment.

14. Wire Bending — "Perimeter is Permanent"

When a wire changes shape, the length remains the same — perimeter or circumference is permanent. Area changes, but perimeter stays. Think: "Perimeter is Permanent, Area is Adjustable".

15. Percentage Change in Area/Volume — "Don't Add, Multiply the Factors"

If dimensions change by a%, b%, never add percentages. Convert to multiplication factors (1 ± a/100), multiply them, then find percentage change. A weird mental image: two sales percentages don't add, they multiply — like stacking discounts.

Q1. Wire Bending – Square to Circle (SSC CGL Tier 2, 2018) A cord in the form of a square encloses an area of 121 cm². If the same cord is bent into a circle, find the area of that circle.
A 154 cm²
B 308 cm²
C 77 cm²
D 196 cm²
Show Solution
✅ Answer: (a) 154 cm²
💡 The area of the square = a² = 121 → a = 11 cm. The length of the wire = perimeter of square = 4a = 44 cm. When bent into a circle, this same length becomes the circumference. So 2πr = 44 → 2 × (22/7) × r = 44 → r = 7 cm. Then, area of the circle = πr² = (22/7) × 7 × 7 = 154 cm². 🧠 Psychological Hook — "The Identity Trap": The most common mistake is equating the area of the square to the area of the circle. Your brain sees "121 cm²" and wants to set πr² = 121, which is completely wrong. The trap exploits a cognitive shortcut called "attribute substitution" — your mind unconsciously replaces the hard question ("What stays constant?") with an easier one ("What number did I just see?") and tries to reuse 121. Lesson: Always ask, "What is invariant?" Here, the perimeter (length of the wire), not the area, remains constant- .

Q3. Melting & Recasting (SSC CGL) A solid metallic cone of radius 30 cm and height 40 cm is melted and recast into metallic spheres of radius 10 cm. Find the number of spheres formed.
A 5
B 9
C 6
D 12
Show Solution
✅ Answer: (b) 9
💡 Volume of cone = (1/3)πr²h = (1/3) × π × 30² × 40 = π × 900 × 40/3 = 12000π cm³. Volume of one sphere = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3) × π × 10³ = (4000π/3) cm³. This is "analogical overgeneralisation" — applying a familiar pattern (volume ≈ π × something) without processing the precise structure. The setter rewards precision, punishes approximation.

A 2:3
B 3:2
C 4:9
D 9:4
