The Hook – A Fact That Will Make Your Jaw Drop

Take a standard deck of 52 cards. Shuffle it well. The exact order you now hold has almost certainly never existed in the history of the universe. In fact, the number of possible arrangements (52!) is so huge it dwarfs the number of atoms on Earth. That’s the insane power of Permutation — the simple act of arranging things.

🧠
Now imagine you’re selecting 5 cards for a poker hand. The number of possible hands is much smaller because the order you hold them doesn’t matter — that’s Combination. Two ideas, both born from the same everyday question: “How many ways?” This chapter turns that question into your strongest weapon.

1. The Mother of All Counting: The Fundamental Counting Principle

🧠
Suppose you’re getting ready for a party. You have 3 shirts and 4 pairs of trousers. How many different outfits can you make? You don’t need a formula — just multiply: 3 × 4 = 12 outfits.

That’s the Fundamental Counting Principle: If one task can be done in m ways and another in n ways, both together can be done in m × n ways. This simple idea is the engine underneath everything we’re about to learn.

🧠
Let’s extend it. You also have 2 pairs of shoes. Total outfits? 3 × 4 × 2 = 24. The principle chains as long as you want.

Answer: 3 × 5 × 2 = 30)

3. A Weird Mnemonic to Nail the Difference Forever

Imagine you’re at a crazy party.

Permutation Parade — people are marching in a row. The order of the marchers is everything; swap two people and you get a whole new parade. So P = Parade, order Priority.

Combination Curry — you’re throwing ingredients into a pot. Once they’re in, it’s just a mix. No one cares which went in first. So C = Curry, order Chill.

Picture a loudly dressed man shouting: “Please Carry!”

P for Permutation (Please – position matters).

C for Combination (Carry – put together, sequence doesn’t matter).

6. The Traps and How to Sidestep Them

Trap 1: And vs Or. “And” means multiply; “Or” (mutually exclusive) means add.

Example: A password has 3 letters (from 26) followed by 2 digits (from 0-9). The number of passwords = (26 × 25 × 24) and (10 × 9) = 26

P3 × 10

P2 (no repetition). If repetition is allowed, it becomes 26³ × 10². Spot the “and” — multiply.

Trap 2: Repetition allowed. When repetition is allowed, the number of arrangements of r items from n is simply n^r (if order matters). For combination with repetition, it’s a different formula, but at foundation level simply note that most exam questions will specify “without repetition” unless stated otherwise.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt – A lock has a 3-digit code (0-9, repetition allowed). How many codes? (

Answer: 10³ = 1000)

7. Permutation & Combination in One Problem

Sometimes a problem requires both. Example: From 5 boys and 4 girls, we need to form a team of 4 with exactly 2 girls.

– Choose 2 girls from 4: 4

C2 = 6.

– Choose 2 boys from 5: 5

C2 = 10.

– Now combine these selections (and) → 6 × 10 = 60 ways.

Notice, we used combination for selection; no arrangement here. If the problem asked to line them up after selection, you’d multiply by permutation.

In Simple Words

Alright, imagine you and your best friend are at the craziest ice cream shop in the world. They have 31 flavors. Now, there are two ways to enjoy your treat:

🧠
Story #1: You’re building a massive cone with three different scoops stacked one on top of another. The guy behind the counter asks, “What flavor on top, middle, bottom?” You pick chocolate on top, vanilla in the middle, strawberry on bottom. Your friend says, “No, no — strawberry on top, chocolate middle, vanilla bottom.” The ice cream melts the same, but you both argue because the order of the scoops changes how you taste them bite by bite. That’s Permutation — arrangement matters, just like when you stack scoops. Even if the three flavors are the same, changing the order gives you a different cone.
🧠
Story #2: Now you’re making a milkshake. You throw three scoops of chocolate, vanilla, strawberry into the blender and hit the button. Whirrrrrr. Now, does anyone care if chocolate went in before vanilla? Nope. It’s all one messy, delicious mush. It’s the same milkshake no matter the order you dumped the scoops in. That’s Combination — you only care about which flavors are chosen, not the order.
🧠
The hardest idea? It’s simply asking: “If I shake the box, does the result change?” If yes, it’s Permutation. If no, it’s Combination.

Here’s the forever trick: Every time you see a counting problem, paint a mental picture. If you’re arranging books on a shelf, each book’s position defines the view — like a Parade of books. P → Parade, order Priority. If you’re picking a team for a game and everyone gets the same jersey, they’re going into a Curry pot — C → Curry, order Chill. So: P for Parade, C for Curry.

🧠
Now go answer this: You have 4 different colored toy cars and a parking lot with 3 numbered spots. How many different ways can you park the cars (one per spot, no repeats)? And is that a Parade or a Curry?

3. The Supermarket Factorial (n

Pr Formula)

n

You have n items on the shelf. You pull r of them. The unwanted ones (n–r) are thrown away — their factorial is the denominator.

Memory: “Cousins don’t care who sits first — blend them!”

Imagine a playground seesaw. On one side sit r chosen children, on the other side the remaining n–r rejected children. The seesaw balances perfectly.

9. The Detective’s Complement (At Least One)

A detective fails to count “at least one” directly. He whispers: “Total minus the opposite.”

If you need at least one boy, count all ways, subtract the no boy (all girls) case.

10. The Crossroad of AND/OR

At a traffic junction:

AND road has a giant multiplication sign (×) carved on the pavement.

OR road has a plus sign (+) painted in yellow.

You must MULTIPLY for independent events that happen together (AND), ADD for mutually exclusive alternatives (OR).

Story: “AND is a chain, OR is a branch.”

14. The Vowel Block Playpen (Together Condition)

To keep vowels together, build a playpen (block). Put all vowels inside. Now arrange the playpen + consonants. Then let the vowels rearrange inside.

Q1. Forming Numbers (Without Repetition) Question: How many 3-digit numbers can be formed using the digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 without repeating any digit? (1) 120 (2) 100 (3) 90 (4) 80
Show Solution
✅ Answer: (2) 100
💡 Since the first digit (hundreds place) cannot be 0, it has only 5 choices. The second digit can be any of the remaining 5 digits, and the third can be any of the remaining 4 digits. Total numbers = 5 × 5 × 4 = 100.
📚 SSC-CGL 2019, IBPS Clerk Mains 2018*
Q2. Seating Arrangement with a Condition (Cohort Grouping) Question: 4 boys and 2 girls are to be seated in a row so that the two girls always sit together. In how many different ways can this be done? (1) 720 (2) 240 (3) 120 (4) 480
Show Solution
✅ Answer: (2) 240
💡 Treat the 2 girls as a single "block." Now there are 4 boys + 1 block = 5 units to arrange, which can be done in 5 ! = 120 5!=120 ways. Within the block, the 2 girls can be arranged in 2 ! = 2 2!=2 ways. Total ways = 120 × 2 = 240.
📚 SSC CHSL 2024, RRB NTPC 2021
Q3. Letter Arrangement — Vowel Cohort Question: In how many different ways can the letters of the word 'DIRECTOR' be arranged such that all the vowels always come together? (1) 4320 (2) 2160 (3) 720 (4) 1080
Show Solution
✅ Answer: (2) 2160
💡 The word 'DIRECTOR' has 8 letters: vowels I, E, O (3 vowels) and consonants D, R, C, T, R (5 consonants including the repeated R appearing twice). Treat the 3 vowels as a single block. Now we have: 5 consonants + 1 vowel block = 6 units. However, since R repeats twice, the arrangement of these 6 units is 6 ! 2 ! = 360 2! 6! =360. Within the vowel block, the 3 distinct vowels can be arranged in 3 ! = 6 3!=6 ways. Total arrangements = 360 × 6 = 2160.
📚 IBPS PO Pre 2021, SSC CHSL 2023
Q4. Committee Selection — "At Least" Condition Question: In a group of 6 boys and 4 girls, four children are to be selected. In how many different ways can the selection be made such that at least one boy is included? (1) 159 (2) 194 (3) 205 (4) 209

✅ Answer: (4) 209
💡 Total unconstrained selections from 10 children: 10 4 = 210 10 C 4 =210. The only selection that violates "at least one boy" is the one with 0 boys (all 4 girls): 4 4 = 1 4 C 4 =1. Valid selections = 210 − 1 = 209.
