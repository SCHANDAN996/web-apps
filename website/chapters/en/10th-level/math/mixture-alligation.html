📖Mixture & Alligation

The Milkman’s Secret

Imagine a clever milkman who wants to earn a little extra. He has pure milk that costs ₹50 per litre. He mixes some water (cost ₹0 per litre) into it. If he sells the mixture at ₹50 per litre, customers won’t suspect anything, but his profit shoots up because part of that mixture was free. Now here’s a shocking fact: you can calculate exactly how much water he added without a single x or y, in under 30 seconds, using a technique traders have used for centuries. That technique is Alligation. By the end of this chapter, you’ll be faster than the milkman himself.

🧠 Before you read on: If the milkman mixes 3 litres of water with 7 litres of milk and sells the whole 10 litres at ₹50 per litre, what is his profit compared to selling 10 litres of pure milk? (Keep that thought.)

Meet the Alligation Cross — Your New Superpower

We have two ingredients: one cheaper (C), one dearer (D). We mix them to get a mean price (M). The magical alligation rule gives the ratio in which they must be mixed:

(Quantity of cheaper) : (Quantity of dearer) = (D – M) : (M – C)

Yes, just subtract crosswise. Draw a cross:

Weighted Average — The Mother Concept

Alligation is nothing but an application of Weighted Average. When you mix 2 kg of ₹40/kg rice and 3 kg of ₹60/kg rice, the average price = (2×40 + 3×60) / (2+3) = 260/5 = ₹52/kg. The alligation cross simply reverses this: if average is given, it finds the weights.

🧠
So if a question says, “In what ratio must a grocer mix two pulses costing ₹15 and ₹20 per kg to get a mixture worth ₹16.50 per kg?” you draw the cross, subtract, and you’re done. The ratio of ₹15 to ₹20 will be (20 – 16.50) : (16.50 – 15) = 3.50 : 1.50 = 7:3. That’s it. Not even a single equation was harmed.
🧠 Recall prompt: A mixture of two liquids costs ₹30/L. The cheaper liquid is ₹24/L, the dearer is ₹36/L. Without a pen, what ratio do you get?

Replacement Problems — The “Take Out and Top Up” Dance

🧠
A classic trap: “A container has 40 litres of milk. 4 litres are withdrawn and replaced with water. This is repeated twice more. How much milk is left?” Students panic and start step-by-step messy calculations. But there’s a golden formul

Back:

Amount of pure substance after n such operations = Initial quantity × [1 – (amount replaced each time / total quantity)]^n

Here, milk left = 40 × (1 – 4/40)^3 = 40 × (0.9)^3 = 40 × 0.729 = 29.16 litres. Done. Notice that the replacement is always the same amount each time. If different volumes are removed, apply sequentially, but competitive exams usually stick to equal replacements.

🧠 Recall: A jar has 80 L alcohol. 8 L is drawn and replaced with water, and the process is done 2 times. Find alcohol left. Use the formula.

When Mixture of Mixtures Confuses You

🧠
Sometimes you mix two mixtures that already have components in different ratios. For instance, Vessel A has milk and water in ratio 2:3, Vessel B has 4:1. They are mixed to get a final mixture with ratio 1:1. What ratio of contents must be taken? Simply compute the concentration of one component (say milk) in each vessel: A = 2/5 milk, B = 4/5 milk. Target concentration = 1/2 milk. Then apply alligation on these fractions: (2/5, 4/5, and 1/2). Multiply by LCM to avoid fractions if you wish. The ratio becomes (4/5 – 1/2) : (1/2 – 2/5) = (3/10) : (1/10) = 3:1. So you take 3 parts from A and 1 part from B. Neat.

The "Never Forget" Seesaw Trick

Picture a seesaw in a playground. The expensive lemonade sits on one end, the cheap lemonade on the other. The mix price is the balancing point in the middle.

To lift the heavy expensive end down to the middle, you need a light person sitting far away on the cheap side.

The distance from the expensive price to the middle (40¢) is the length of the cheap side’s seesaw arm.

The distance from the cheap price to the middle (60¢) is the length of the expensive side’s arm.

So, the difference of the expensive one tells you how many cheap ones you need, and vice versa. Differences swap sides, just like the lengths of a seesaw from the balance point.

Remember: The rich kid’s price difference buys the poor kid’s quantity. Or as I like to say, “Extra money pays for more cheap stuff.”

7. The Replacement Wave (Successive Removal)

text

Copy

Download

Think of the pure substance as a sandcastle on a beach. Each removal is a wave that washes away a fixed fraction of what remains. After n waves, the castle's height shrinks exponentially.

Formul

Back: Remaining = Initial × (1 – wave strength)^n.

Wave strength = Volume removed / Total volume.

This visual of a crumbling sandcastle locks the (1 – r/t)^n formula forever.

8. Profit Alligation: "Same Base or Bust"

text

Copy

Download

When using alligation for profit percentages on mixing, remember:

"Same Base or Bust!"

If the cost price per unit of the two goods is NOT the same, you cannot directly alligate profit percentages. You must first convert to actual profit per unit (e.g., profit per kg).

Otherwise, your answer will "bust" – like a piñata that explodes with wrong numbers.

9. The Concert Stage (Weighted Average)

text

Copy

Download

Imagine a concert stage where each item is a singer. The loudness of each singer is their price, and the time they sing is their weight (quantity).

The average loudness (weighted average) = (Loudness1 × Time1 + Loudness2 × Time2) / Total Time.

Alligation is simply the reverse: given the average loudness and individual loudness, find how long each singer performed.

The stage balances the performance!
