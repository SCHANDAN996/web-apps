The Traffic Light That Broke the Town’s Sleep

Let me tell you a true-to-life story. In a small town, there were three traffic signals on a straight road. The first turned red every 60 seconds, the second every 48 seconds, and the third every 72 seconds. At 5:00 AM sharp, they all turned red together. A boy named Aryan asked his math teacher, “When will they all turn red together again?” The teacher smiled and said, “You just asked for the LCM, my boy.” Aryan found the answer (it was 720 seconds, or 12 minutes), and the town’s folks finally knew when to expect that annoying synchronized honking.

Here’s the shocker: Most students confuse when to use HCF and when to use LCM. They are not interchangeable. One helps you divide things, the other helps you multiply events. If you mess it up, you might end up with a party where guests don’t get equal sweets, or a warehouse where boxes don’t stack perfectly. But by the end of this chapter, you’ll be the master of both.

Step 1: The Language of Factors and Multiples (Chunk 1)

Imagine you have a chocolate bar with 12 pieces. You can break it into groups of 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, or 12. These numbers are called factors — they divide the bar completely without leaving a crumb. So, a factor of a number divides it exactly. In contrast, if you keep adding 12 repeatedly, you get multiples: 12, 24, 36, 48... These are multiples — the number’s family that grows endlessly.

Factor: A number that divides another number perfectly. (e.g., 3 is a factor of 15 because 15 ÷ 3 = 5)

Multiple: The result of multiplying a number by an integer. (e.g., 21 is a multiple of 7 because 7 × 3 = 21)

Now, HCF (Highest Common Factor) is simply the biggest factor that two or more numbers share. LCM (Least Common Multiple) is the smallest multiple they share. That’s it — you just unlocked the core idea.

Step 2: Why Not Just Look at a Multiplication Table? (Chunk 2)

You can list factors and multiples by hand, but that’s slow for big numbers. That’s where prime factorization steps in like a superhero. Every number can be broken down into a product of prime numbers (a number that has exactly two factors: 1 and itself). For example, 12 = 2 × 2 × 3 = 2² × 3.

Prime numbers are the building blocks of all numbers. Just as letters form words, primes form numbers. Once you break numbers into primes, LCM and HCF become a breeze.

🧠 Active Recall: What is the prime factorization of 18? (Hint: 18 ÷ 2 = 9, then 9 = 3 × 3). Pause and do it mentally.

Step 3: HCF — The Biggest Cheesecake That Fits All Plates (Chunk 3)

I’ll give you a weird but unforgettable mnemonic: HCF thinks like a hungry crocodile that only eats the smallest power of common primes. When you have two numbers, list their prime factors. For HCF, take only the common prime factors, and pick the LOWEST power of those common primes. Why? Because the HCF must divide both numbers; if you take a higher power, it might not fit into the smaller number.

Example: Find HCF of 24 and 36.

24 = 2³ × 3¹

36 = 2² × 3²
Common primes: 2 and 3. For 2, lowest power is 2² (from 36). For 3, lowest power is 3¹ (from 24). So HCF = 2² × 3¹ = 4 × 3 = 12.

Check: 24 ÷ 12 = 2, 36 ÷ 12 = 3. It works perfectly. The largest number that divides both is 12. If you try a bigger number like 18, 24 ÷ 18 doesn’t work. So the hungry crocodile was right.

Step 4: LCM — The Lazy Camel That Wants the Highest Dunes (Chunk 4)

If HCF is the minimal common eater, LCM is the maximal collector. Here’s a funny image: A lazy camel named LCM wants to cross the desert by hopping on sand dunes. To avoid effort, he always picks the highest dune (power) among all prime factors present in the numbers. Why? Because the LCM must be a multiple of both numbers; it needs enough primes to cover the largest power from each.

Take the same numbers: 24 = 2³ × 3¹, 36 = 2² × 3².

For prime 2, the highest power is 2³ (from 24).

For prime 3, the highest power is 3² (from 36).
LCM = 2³ × 3² = 8 × 9 = 72.

Check: 72 ÷ 24 = 3, 72 ÷ 36 = 2. It works. And you can’t find a smaller common multiple (like 36? 36 ÷ 24 doesn’t work). So the lazy camel took the highest dunes and found the smallest possible common rest-stop that works for both.

The Love & Hugs Connection (Mnemonic)

Here’s the most beloved trick of all: LCM × HCF = Product of the two numbers (works only for two numbers). I teach this as “Love and Hugs Multiply the Pair” — L for LCM, H for HCF, multiply them, and you get the product of the two numbers. Use this to cross-check your answers. For 24 and 36: LCM 72 × HCF 12 = 864, and 24 × 36 = 864. Magic? No, math.

You can use this formula when one value is missing. If HCF is known and product is known, LCM = Product ÷ HCF. This relationship has saved countless students in exams.

🧠 Active Recall: For 12 and 18, the HCF is 6. What is the LCM? Use the relationship and check.

Step 5: Real-World Decoder — When to Use What (Chunk 5)

HCF problems: Whenever you need to divide something into equal parts with nothing left over — maximum size of a tile, maximum number of groups, greatest length of a measuring tape. Keyword clues: “greatest”, “largest”, “maximum”, “exactly divide”.

LCM problems: Whenever you need to find when events repeat or something happens simultaneously again — bells ringing together, lights flashing, wheels meeting after rotations, smallest length divisible by given lengths. Keywords: “least”, “smallest”, “next time together”, “simultaneously”.

Example: You have 40 apples and 60 oranges. You want to pack them into boxes containing the same number of apples and same number of oranges per box, with no fruit left. Maximum boxes? That’s HCF (of 40 and 60 = 20 boxes, each containing 2 apples + 3 oranges). If you were asked “what is the smallest number of fruits that can be arranged in either groups of 8 or 12 exactly?” — LCM (24 fruits). See the difference?

🧠 Active Recall: Your friend says, “Three bells ring at intervals of 18, 24, and 32 seconds. When will they ring together again?” Which concept do you use and why? Answer mentally.

Step 6: Tricky Twists — More Than Two Numbers (Chunk 6)

The methods scale seamlessly. For three numbers, HCF is the product of common primes with the lowest power across all three. LCM is the product of all primes with the highest power appearing in any of them. Example: 12 (2²×3), 18 (2×3²), 60 (2²×3×5). HCF = lowest common powers: 2¹ (since 18 has only 2¹) and 3¹ (12 has 3¹) => 2×3=6. LCM = highest powers: 2² (from 12 or 60), 3² (from 18), 5¹ (from 60) => 4×9×5=180.

SSC often asks: “Find the greatest number that divides x, y, and z leaving the same remainder.” That’s an HCF problem in disguise. The trick: If numbers are a, b, c and same remainder r, then (a-r), (b-r), (c-r) are exactly divisible by the required number. So take the differences of the numbers after subtracting remainder; if remainder is not given, take differences between the numbers. But we’ll save that for the Tricks section.

Summary Table: LCM vs. HCF — The Cheat Sheet

Aspect	HCF (Highest Common Factor)	LCM (Least Common Multiple)
What it finds	Biggest number that divides all given numbers	Smallest number that is a multiple of all given numbers
Prime factorization rule	Take COMMON primes with LOWEST power	Take ALL primes with HIGHEST power
Mnemonics	Hungry Crocodile (eats lowest)	Lazy Camel (climbs highest)
Relationship for 2 numbers	LCM × HCF = Product of numbers	Product ÷ HCF = LCM
Real-life signal words	Greatest, maximum, exactly divide, share equally	Least, smallest, next simultaneous event, smallest common length
Use when	Cutting, grouping, packing into max size containers	Aligning events, finding common multiples, sync times
