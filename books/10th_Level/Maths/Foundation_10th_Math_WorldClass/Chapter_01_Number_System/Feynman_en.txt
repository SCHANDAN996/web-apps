Why Some Numbers Just Can’t Be Fractions

Imagine you and your friend have a pizza. You cut it into two equal slices – each is ½. Half is a perfect fraction. That’s rational. It fits nicely as one number over another.

Now, let’s do something completely different.

Draw a square on the ground, exactly 1 meter on each side. Take a piece of string and stretch it from one corner to the opposite corner – that’s the diagonal. How long is that string? Pythagoras told us: diagonal² = 1² + 1² = 2, so diagonal = √2.

Measure it. You get about 1.414213562... and the digits never stop, and they never start repeating a pattern. No matter how hard you try, you can’t find a fraction that gives exactly that length. If you divide any two integers, you’ll always get a decimal that either ends or repeats. But √2 won’t do that. It’s a rebel.

That’s the big idea: Rational numbers can be written as a fraction of two integers. Irrational numbers cannot. The “irrational” doesn’t mean crazy – it just means “no ratio.”

The story gets wild. About 2,500 years ago, a Greek named Hippasus figured this out on a boat. His friends, who believed everything in the universe was a perfect fraction, got so angry at him for breaking their perfect picture that they threw him overboard. All because √2 refused to be a fraction.

The Unforgettable Trick

Whenever you meet a number, ask yourself one question: “Can I write it as a simple fraction?”

Yes? → Rational.

No, because the decimal goes on forever without repeating? → Irrational.

For square roots: Is the number inside a perfect square? (1, 4, 9, 16, 25...) → If yes, it’s rational. If no, it’s irrational.

Quick test: √25 = 5 = 5/1, rational. √26? Not a perfect square, so irrational. π? Its decimal 3.14159... never repeats – irrational.

Now, let me leave you with a mystery to solve:

A right triangle has legs 1 and 2. Use Pythagoras to find the hypotenuse. Will that number be rational or irrational? Try the trick.
