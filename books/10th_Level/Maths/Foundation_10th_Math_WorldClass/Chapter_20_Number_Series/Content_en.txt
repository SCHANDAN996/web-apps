Chunk 1: The Magic Formula — “What Did You Do to Get the Next Number?”

The only question you ever need to ask: “What operation changes the first number into the second? And is that same operation repeated?”

Start with the three primary suspects:

Addition/Subtraction (a constant or increasing/decreasing number)

Multiplication/Division (a constant or a pattern like ×2, ×3, ×2.5…)

Square / Cube / Prime (the numbers themselves belong to a special family)

Example:
10, 17, 26, 37, ?
Difference: +7, +9, +11, +13 → Next difference is +15 → Answer = 37+15 = 52.
Here the differences themselves form an arithmetic progression (odd numbers). This is a “difference of difference” pattern, and it’s a favourite in exams.

Chunk 2: When Differences Aren’t Enough — Look Deeper

Sometimes the first difference gives you nothing. See this:
2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?
Difference: +4, +6, +8, +10 → So next difference +12, answer 42. Simple.
But what if it’s: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36? These are squares (1^2, 2^2, 3^2…). If you only look at differences (+3, +5, +7, +9, +11) you’ll still get it, but recognising the square pattern saves 10 seconds.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt
Identify the pattern in one glance: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, ?
(Yes, these are cubes. Now, make a funny face and say “1 ate 27, 64 ate 125” — the next cube is 6^3 = 216. Silly? Yes. Memorable? Absolutely.)

A Weird Mnemonic That Works Like Magic

To remember the common cube series forever, learn this ridiculous sentence:
“One ATE twenty-seven, sixty-four ATE one-twenty-five, two-sixteen ate three-forty-three.”
Here, “ATE” sounds like “8”. So 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343. Picture a monster named “One” who eats 27, then a monster “Sixty-four” eats 125, and a monster “Two-sixteen” eats 343. The brain loves bizarre images — you’ll never forget it.

Chunk 3: The 5 Pattern Types You’ll Meet Every Time

1. Constant Addition/Subtraction
12, 17, 22, 27, ? (+5 each time) — Child’s play.

2. Increasing/Decreasing Difference
5, 7, 10, 15, 23, ? (+2, +3, +5, +8… these differences are prime numbers? No, +2,+3,+5,+8? Actually +2,+3,+5,+8 is increase by 1,2,3… so next diff +12, answer 35. But always double-check: 2,3,5,8 differences of differences: +1,+2,+3, next +4, so diff +12. Yes.)

3. Multiplication/Division Series
3, 6, 18, 72, 360, ? (×2, ×3, ×4, ×5 → next ×6 = 2160)

4. Alternating Patterns (Two-in-one series)
2, 9, 5, 12, 8, 15, 11, ?
Here, separate the odd and even positions:
Odd positions: 2, 5, 8, 11 (+3)
Even positions: 9, 12, 15, ? (+3) → Answer 18.
Always suspect alternation when numbers don’t seem to connect.

5. Combination of Operations
3, 8, 23, 68, ?
Pattern: ×3 –1? 3×3 –1=8, 8×3 –1=23, 23×3 –1=68, next 68×3 –1=203.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt
What is the next term in: 2, 3, 5, 9, 17, ?
(Hint: differences +1, +2, +4, +8 → powers of 2, next diff +16 → 17+16=33. Also can see as multiply by 2 and subtract 1? 2×2–1=3, 3×2–1=5, 5×2–1=9, 9×2–1=17, 17×2–1=33. Both work.)

Chunk 4: The Examiner’s Traps & How to Outsmart Them

Trap 1: Giving a perfect square/cube series but with a subtle offset.
Example: 5, 10, 17, 26, 37, ?
Looks like squares+1: 2^2+1=5, 3^2+1=10, 4^2+1=17, 5^2+1=26, 6^2+1=37, next 7^2+1=50.
If you only checked differences, you’d still get it, but knowing the offset saves time.

Trap 2: Decimal or fraction series. Don’t panic. Treat the numerator and denominator separately.
1/2, 3/4, 5/8, 7/16, ? — Numerator: 1,3,5,7 (odd) → 9; Denominator: 2,4,8,16 (×2) → 32; answer 9/32.

Trap 3: A series that uses both multiplication and addition alternately.
2, 5, 11, 23, 47, ?
Pattern: ×2+1, ×2+1, ×2+1, ×2+1 → Next 47×2+1=95.

Your Final Number Series Cheat Sheet
Pattern Type	Example	How to Spot Instantly	Typical Trick
Constant Difference	8, 13, 18, 23	Subtract adjacent terms; difference stays same	Might be a cleverly disguised square/cube series
Increasing Difference	4, 6, 10, 16, 24	Difference of differences is constant or follows a pattern	Check if differences are prime, odd, or squares
Multiplication	5, 10, 30, 120	Consecutive terms have a clear multiplicative factor	Factor might be fractional (0.5) or follow a sequence
Squares/Cubes	0, 1, 9, 36, 100 (cube of 0,1,2,3,4? No. Actually 0=0^3? 1=1^3? 9 is 2^3+1? This is misleading. Better use clear example: 1, 8, 27, 64)	Check if numbers near perfect squares/cubes	Add or subtract 1 from each term

Fibonacci-like	1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13	Each term is sum of previous two	Sometimes sum of three terms, or product, etc.
