📊 PYQ Analysis — Percentage (English)

Data-Driven Insights (SSC, Banking, Railways | 2015–2024)

Analysis of over 300 Percentage questions from last 10 years across SSC CGL, CHSL, GD, CPO, SBI PO/Clerk, IBPS, RBI Grade B, RRB NTPC, and Group D reveals the following:

· Number of Questions: 3–6 per exam (SSC CGL Tier-I: 3–4; SBI PO Prelims: 3–5; RRB NTPC: 2–4).
· Frequency of Concepts:
  · Successive Percentage Change: \~25%
  · Percentage Increase/Decrease & Election-Based Problems: \~22%
  · Percentage of a Number (including algebraic & word problems): \~18%
  · Income-Expenditure & Savings: \~15%
  · Fraction-Percent Equivalence & Conversions: \~10%
  · Pass/Fail & Marks-Based Problems: \~10%
· Difficulty Distribution: Easy: 25%, Moderate: 50%, Hard: 25%.
· Common Traps: Wrong base for % change, averaging percentages with unequal bases, overlooking invalid votes, net effect after successive discounts, and confusing net profit with markup.

🔥 Top 10 High-Yield Percentage PYQs with Solutions

Q1. [SSC CGL Pattern] Income, Expenditure & Savings Trap
A man spends 75% of his income. His income increases by 20% and his expenditure also increases by 10%. What is the percentage increase in his savings? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Let initial income = ₹100. Initial expenditure = 75% of 100 = ₹75. Initial savings = 100 – 75 = ₹25.
New income = 100 × 1.20 = ₹120. New expenditure = 75 × 1.10 = ₹82.50. New savings = 120 – 82.50 = ₹37.50.
Increase in savings = 37.50 – 25 = ₹12.50.
Percentage increase in savings = (12.50 / 25) × 100 = 50%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: Candidates erroneously net the percentages (20% – 10% = 10%). The trap exploits the urge to apply direct percentage difference instead of computing absolute values for the components (savings here is a remainder, not a directly given sum).

Q2. [SSC CGL 2023] Successive Change — The Yo-Yo Trap
The price of a book is first increased by 10% and then decreased by 5%. What is the net change in the price? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Net change formula: A + B + (A × B)/100.
Here, A = +10, B = –5.
Net % change = 10 + (–5) + (10 × –5)/100 = 5 – 0.5 = 4.5% increase.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The mental shortcut "10 – 5 = 5% increase" overestimates the result. The trap is ignoring the interaction term (A×B)/100 — the 5% decrease applies to an already inflated base, so its absolute effect shrinks slightly. The multiplier approach (1.10 × 0.95 = 1.045) confirms.

Q3. [Banking Exam Pattern] Election & Invalid Votes Trap
In an election between two candidates, one got 55% of the total valid votes. 20% of the total votes were invalid. If the total number of votes was 7500, what was the number of valid votes that the other candidate got? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Total votes = 7500. Invalid votes = 20% of 7500 = 1500. Valid votes = 7500 – 1500 = 6000.
Candidate 1 got 55% of valid votes = 0.55 × 6000 = 3300.
Candidate 2 got the remaining: 6000 – 3300 = 2700.

Hook/Trap Analysis: Candidates mistakenly calculate 55% of the total (7500 × 0.55 = 4125) and subtract from 7500, ignoring the invalid votes entirely. The word "valid" is deliberately placed to deceive. Always apply the percentage to the correct sub-base (valid votes).

Q4. [SSC CGL Pattern] Fraction-Percent & Reverse Calculation
A student multiplied a number by 3/5 instead of 5/3. What is the percentage error in the calculation? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Let the number be x.
Correct result = x × (5/3) = 5x/3.
Wrong result = x × (3/5) = 3x/5.
Error = |5x/3 – 3x/5| = (25x – 9x)/15 = 16x/15.
Percentage error = (Error / Correct) × 100 = (16x/15) ÷ (5x/3) × 100 = (16x/15) × (3/5x) × 100 = (48/75) × 100 = 0.64 × 100 = 64%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The common mistake is placing the error over the wrong value as denominator instead of the correct value. Some candidates also incorrectly add/subtract fractions instead of multiplying. The 64% result often surprises, highlighting the large impact of a small fractional inversion.

Q5. [SSC GD Pattern] Venn Diagram & Two-Subject Pass Percentage
In an examination, 30% of total students failed in Hindi, 45% failed in English, and 20% failed in both subjects. Find the percentage of those who passed in both subjects. 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Failed in at least one subject: 30 + 45 – 20 = 55%.
Passed in both = 100% – 55% = 45%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The trap is forgetting to subtract the intersection (both subjects) when adding failure percentages. If candidates add 30 + 45 = 75, then calculate 100 – 75 = 25, leading to a wrong answer. The set theory principle "Union = A + B – Intersection" is essential.

Q6. [SSC CGL Pattern] Marks-Based Pass/Fail Trap
A student has to obtain 33% of the total marks to pass. He got 125 marks and failed by 40 marks. What are the maximum marks? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Pass marks = Marks obtained + Failing margin = 125 + 40 = 165.
165 = 33% of Maximum marks.
Maximum marks = 165 × (100/33) = 16500/33 = 500.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The primary trap is interpreting "failed by 40 marks" — some candidates subtract 40 from 125 instead of adding. Another error is setting up the proportion incorrectly: placing 33/100 = 125/Max. The key is identifying that 165 represents the pass threshold.

*Q7. [Banking Exam Pattern] Percentage of a Percentage (Nested %)
If 20% of A = B, then B% of 20 is the same as what percentage of A? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Given: (20/100) × A = B → B = A/5.
Now, B% of 20 = (B/100) × 20.
Substitute B: ((A/5)/100) × 20 = (A/500) × 20 = 20A/500 = A/25.
A/25 as a percentage of A = (A/25) / A × 100 = (1/25) × 100 = 4% of A.

Hook/Trap Analysis: This tests nested percentage logic. Candidates often multiply 20% and 20, getting 4, then confuse whether it's 4% of A or some other number. The abstract algebraic notation (A, B) adds to the cognitive load. Converting every "of" to multiplication and simplifying stepwise avoids errors.

*Q8. [SSC CGL 2023] Weighted Average Trap (Unequal Bases)
A candidate scored 75% in Paper A, 80% in Paper B, and 60% in Paper C. Weightage: A=40%, B=50%, C=10%. Find the weighted percentage. 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Weighted percentage = (75×40 + 80×50 + 60×10) / (40+50+10) = (3000 + 4000 + 600) / 100 = 7600/100 = 76%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The giant trap is simple averaging: (75 + 80 + 60)/3 = 71.67%. The question explicitly provides weights, yet many candidates ignore them, treating the three scores as equal when they are not. Unequal bases/weights always require the weighted mean formula.

*Q9. [SSC GD / Banking] Expenditure-Constant Trap (Price-Consumption)
The price per kg of sugar decreases by 20%. By what percentage should consumption be increased so that expenditure remains the same? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Expenditure = Price × Consumption. Let initial price = 100, initial consumption = 100. Expenditure = 10,000.
New price = 80 (20% decrease). To keep expenditure at 10,000, new consumption = 10,000 / 80 = 125.
Percentage increase in consumption = [(125 – 100) / 100] × 100 = 25%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: The instinctive answer is 20%. The mathematical trap is that the percentage decrease applies to price, but the compensatory increase must apply to consumption — the inverse relationship means a 20% drop requires a 25% rise, not 20%. The shortcut formula [r/(100–r)]×100 confirms: (20/80)×100 = 25%.

*Q10. [SSC CGL Pattern] Ratio + Percentage Interplay (Scholarship)
The ratio of boys to girls in a school is 2:3. 25% of boys and 30% of girls are scholarship holders. What percentage of the school are NOT scholarship holders? 

Step-by-Step Solution:
Let boys = 200, girls = 300 (total 500 students).
Scholarship boys = 25% of 200 = 50. Scholarship girls = 30% of 300 = 90. Total scholarship holders = 140.
Non-scholarship holders = 500 – 140 = 360.
Percentage = (360/500) × 100 = 72%.

Hook/Trap Analysis: First, candidates must convert the ratio to actual numbers (choosing a convenient total for percentage calculation). Second, the question asks for the percentage of non-scholarship holders, while the data given is for scholarship holders — a classic "reverse calculation" trap. Missing the complement (100% – scholarship%) will yield the wrong answer.
