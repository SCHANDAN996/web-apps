The Sale That Almost Tricked You
Picture this: It’s Diwali. A shop screams “50% off.” Next to it, another shop says “Buy one, get one free.” They look identical. But are they? One savvy shopper quickly calculates: 50% off means price becomes half. Buy one get one free also means two items for the price of one — again, price per item becomes half. For the single-item buyer, they are the same. For a bulk buyer, the second might have a hidden trap (you must buy two!). This is the subtle magic of percentage — it’s everywhere, and once you truly understand it, you turn from a confused buyer into a money-saving ninja. Today, you’ll gain that power.

---

You are not alone if your brain freezes at the sight of “25% of 160”. Many learners see the % symbol and panic because it feels abstract. In truth, percentage is nothing but a “fraction spoken differently.” It’s a language of comparison. Think of it this way: If I say “I scored 17 out of 20,” it’s not instantly clear how good that is. But if I say “85%”, your mind immediately cues: “Excellent!” Percentage standardizes everything into a scale of 100. It’s like measuring height in centimetres — a common ruler for every number. The moment you treat percentage as “out of 100,” the mystery dissolves.

🧠 Pause and Recall: Before reading further, speak aloud: What does “per cent” literally mean? (Answer: per hundred. “Cent” comes from Latin centum, meaning hundred — like century, centipede.)

Chunk 1: The Per-100 Machine
Imagine a magical machine. You feed it any fraction, and it transforms the denominator to exactly 100. The numerator then becomes the percentage. That’s exactly what percentage does.

· To convert a fraction into percentage: Multiply by 100 → 100.
· Example: ⅗ = (⅗) × 100% = (3×100)/5 % = 300/5 % = 60%.

In the opposite direction, to get a percentage back to its simplest fraction, you just drop the % sign and write the number over 100, then simplify.

· 40% = 40/100 = 2/5.
  It’s that mechanical. No magic — just a reliable machine.

🧠 Quick Recall: Convert 12% into a fraction in its lowest terms right now. (Solution: 12/100 = 3/25.)

The Weird Memory Trick (Mnemonic): The Centipede with a Percentage Hat
Picture a giant centipede wearing a top hat that has a shining “%” symbol on it. Why a centipede? Because “cent” = 100, and a centipede has about 100 legs (okay, not exactly, but your brain won’t forget the association). Whenever you see the % symbol, imagine that centipede handing you a board that reads “/100”. So 7% immediately becomes the fraction 7/100. Whenever you have to convert a percentage to a fraction, just let the centipede whisper: “Over hundred, over hundred, simplify if possible.” Next time you get stuck in an exam, simply bring that weird centipede image alive — and the formula will pop back. Weird? Absolutely. Effective? 100%.

Chunk 3: Finding Percentage of a Quantity
The phrase “X% of Y” translates to (X/100) × Y. The word “of” means multiplication.
Example: What is 30% of 240?
30% of 240 = (30/100) × 240 = (3/10) × 240 = 3 × 24 = 72.

Shortcut when Y ends with zeroes: cancel zeroes first. 20% of 350 = (20/100)×350 = (1/5)×350 = 70. You’ll feel the speed.

🧠 Recall Check: Without a calculator, find 15% of 200. (Hint: 10% = 20, 5% = 10, so 15% = 30. Or use fraction: 15/100 × 200 = 15 × 2 = 30.)

Chunk 4: Percentage Change (Increase & Decrease)
When a price goes from old to new, the percentage change is always calculated on the original quantity.
Formula: % change = (Change / Original) × 100.
If the result is positive, it’s increase; if negative, it’s decrease.

Example: A phone’s price jumped from ₹12,000 to ₹15,000. The increase is ₹3,000 on the original ₹12,000.
% increase = (3000/12000) × 100 = (1/4) × 100 = 25%.

Now try decrease: A salary is reduced from ₹50,000 to ₹40,000. Change = -10,000 on 50,000 → (-10000/50000) × 100 = -20%. So 20% decrease.

🧠 Recall Scenario: A fruit seller’s sales rose from 80 kg to 100 kg. What is the percentage increase? (Answer: (20/80)×100 = 25%.)

---

Chunk 5: Quick Successive Change Intuition (Foundation Visual)
If a number first increases by 10% and then the new number increases by another 20%, the total effect is not 30%. Use the formula: Net effect = A + B + (A×B)/100. So 10 + 20 + (10×20)/100 = 30 + 2 = 32% increase. For two successive discounts of 20% and 10%, the net discount = -20 -10 + (200)/100 = -30 + 2 = -28% (i.e., 28% discount). Memorise the “plus AB over 100” formula, good for multiple changes.

🧠 Apply: Find the net change when a value first increases by 25% and then decreases by 20%. (25 - 20 + (25×-20)/100 = 5 - 5 = 0%. So no change, back to original.)

---

Chunk 6: Checking the Trap — “Percent of What?”
The most dangerous word in percentage is “of.” Changing the base changes everything. If a student scored 60% marks in Test A (out of 50) and then 40% in Test B (out of 100), what’s the overall percentage? You cannot simply average 60% and 40%. You must find total marks obtained: 60% of 50 = 30, 40% of 100 = 40. Total marks = 70 out of 150 → (70/150)×100 = 46.67%. The average (60+40)/2 = 50% is wrong because the bases are unequal. Always find actual values when bases differ. Exams love this trap.

🧠 Think: A shirt’s price was increased by 25% and then the new price decreased by 20%. Is the final price more, less, or equal to the original? (Equal, as we saw earlier.)
