Part 1: Figure Series – The Art of "What Comes Next?"

What is a Figure Series?
You are given 4 or 5 problem figures in a sequence. A hidden rule connects them. Your task: find that rule and choose the correct next figure from the answer options.

Think of it like a dance. The shapes are moving, turning, adding, or deleting parts according to a fixed choreography. Your job is to learn the dance step.

The 5 Golden Pattern Types (Memorize These)

1. Rotation (The Spinning Wheel)
The entire figure rotates by a fixed angle: 45°, 90°, 135°, or 180°. Direction can be clockwise or anticlockwise. Spot it by checking the tilt of one distinct element like an arrow or a corner mark.

2. Movement / Shifting (The Travelling Dot)
A specific element (a dot, a line, a small triangle) moves from one position to another: corner to corner, side to side, inside to outside.

3. Number Change (The Counting Game)
Lines, dots, circles, or sides increase or decrease systematically. Examples: +1 line every step, –2 dots every step, or even a sequence like 1,2,4,8…

4. Shading / Fill Change (The Light Switch)
Parts of the figure flip between white, black, striped, or dotted in a predictable cycle. Often follows a clockwise or alternate pattern.

5. Addition / Deletion (The Builder)
Every step adds or removes a small line, curve, or shape. Look at the outermost or innermost part first.

🧠 Active Recall Pause: Close your eyes. Name the 5 pattern types without looking. Now, open your eyes and draw a tiny doodle next to each one for memory.

Combined Patterns – The Real Challenge

Examiners love mixing two types. For example: Rotation + Number Change, or Shifting + Shading. Your strategy: always identify the dominant change first, then look for the secondary change.

Step-by-Step Solving Method:

Compare Figure (i) and (ii): What exactly changed? Write it mentally.

Compare (ii) and (iii): Is the change repeated?

If yes – you have the rule. If no – check alternate figures (i) & (iii); there might be two parallel sequences.

Eliminate answer options that clearly break the rule.

Verify the chosen figure with the last step of the sequence.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Explain to an imaginary friend what "alternate pattern" means. If you can't, reread the last point – it's a top exam trap.

Part 2: Figure Classification – Who is the Odd One?

Classification is the reverse game. You get a group of figures; 4 of them follow a common property. One is the impostor. You must find it.

Common Bases for "Oddness":

Shape type (all triangles except one square).

Number of sides / lines.

Open vs closed figures.

Symmetry: 1 line vs 2 lines, or symmetrical vs asymmetrical.

Shading proportion: equal shading, one has less.

Position of inner elements: dot inside vs outside.

Intersection count: crossing points differ.

Your 3-Second Scan Rule:
When you see a classification question, instantly ask yourself:

"Are they all closed?"

"Do they have the same number of corners?"

"Is the shading in the same area?"
This rapid checklist reveals the odd one in most Foundation-level questions.

Example:
Figures: (a) 5-point star (closed), (b) hexagon, (c) square, (d) open pentagon, (e) equilateral triangle.
→ Instantly, (d) is open, rest are closed. (d) is odd.

🧠 Active Recall Prompt: Create your own odd-one-out question using cups, books, and a pencil sharpener on your desk. Find a common property and one violator. Teaching your brain to categorize is the goal.

Your "SUPER-S" Mnemonic for Figure Patterns

Remember the word "SHAPES" (each letter stands for a pattern type):

S – Shading / Shifting

H – Half-turn / Rotation (Hint: "H" for Half-turn)

A – Addition/Alternate

P – Position change (Movement)

E – Element count (Number change)

S – Symmetry / Size change

"SHAPES rule the non-verbal world." Say it once, and you'll never blank out in the exam.

⚠️ Caution: Examiner's Trap

1. The "Screen Blur" Illusion
In computer-based tests, low resolution can make a striped shading look solid, or a tiny opening look closed. Always zoom your eyes on corners and thin gaps. If an option looks "almost perfect" but feels slightly off, it's probably a trap.

2. The Twin Figures
Two answer options look nearly identical – only one micro-detail differs (a missing dot, a line 1 mm shorter). Your excited brain picks the first familiar shape. Solution: Force yourself to compare those twin options side-by-side, element by element, before finalizing.

3. Rotation + Reflection Mix-Up
Examiners often show a figure rotated 90° clockwise in step 1, then vertically mirrored in step 2. Students assume "rotation continues" and pick the wrong next figure. Rule: If step 2 looks flipped, check whether it's a water/mirror image instead of a turn. Use the "fold test" mentally: does it look like a reflection? If yes, the pattern has changed – handle accordingly.

Your Shield: Whenever you spot a pattern, pause and ask, "Is the examiner using a mix of two rules?" That single question saves you from 80% of traps.
