Front: You have five items: four of them share a secret handshake, one doesn’t. What is this reasoning game called?
Back: Classification – finding the odd one out by identifying a common rule among the others.

Front: What is the magic number to instantly find any letter’s opposite in the alphabet?
Back: 27. Opposite letter position = 27 – original position. Example: A(1) ↔ Z(26), D(4) ↔ W(23).

Front: You see the groups JMP, LOR, NQT, PRU, RTW. What’s the first thing you should write beneath each letter?
Back: The position numbers (A=1,…, Z=26). Then check the gap pattern. Here all groups follow +3 and +3, except one breaks it.

Front: In number classification, what is the smallest trap number that looks prime but isn’t?
Back: 1. It is neither prime nor composite. Examiners often include it to trick students who count it as prime.

Front: You spot the numbers 121, 144, 169, 196, 225. Two patterns exist – perfect squares and digit sums. Which one should you trust first?
Back: The most direct, primary base – perfect squares. If all five follow the primary base, that base is useless; look deeper only if the question demands exactly one odd one out.

Front: Four friends nod, one shakes their head. What is this trick called, and how does it find the odd one?
Back: The “Four-Yes, One-No” dance. Ask each option a yes/no question about a property. Exactly four must answer yes, one no (or vice versa) to identify the odd one.

Front: In letter classification, what’s the quick way to spot a vowel-consonant pattern?
Back: Simply note which groups mix vowels and consonants. If four groups have exactly one vowel and two consonants, and one group has all consonants, that’s your odd one.

Front: What’s the examiner’s favourite trick when giving word-based classification with difficult vocabulary?
Back: Unfamiliar words (like “flamingo” or “penguin”) that make you panic and choose them as odd, when they actually belong to the same category as the rest.

Front: Among 17, 19, 23, 27, 29, which number is the impostor and why?
Back: 27. It is composite (3×9), while all others are prime numbers.

Front: How can you quickly recall the prime numbers from 1 to 100 without memorizing a dull list?
Back: Use the sieve trick: write numbers 2-100, cross out multiples of 2,3,5,7,11,13. Surviving numbers are primes. Also remember 1 is not prime.

Front: What is the common examiner trap in mixed classification with letters, numbers, and symbols?
Back: Dual-relationship confusion – they make two different operations look valid. Always apply the same operation to every option; only one option will fail the primary operation.

Front: In the word list “chair, table, almirah, spoon, bed”, what’s the immediate category that makes spoon the odd one?
Back: Furniture vs. utensil. The first four are furniture, spoon is a utensil.

Front: How do you handle a word-based question when the meaning isn’t clear?
Back: Switch to structure – count letters, vowels, or check starting letter patterns. If four words have 5 letters and one has 6, that’s your odd one.

Front: What’s the fatal mistake students make when two patterns both seem to give a valid odd one?
Back: They hesitate and overthink. The rule: pick the most obvious, primary category (e.g., fruit-vegetable over color) and trust the question designer hasn’t given both as options.

Front: If you see D4G, H8K, L12O, P16S, T21W, what’s the broken rule?
Back: Middle number should equal the position of the first letter. T=20, so middle should be 20, but it’s 21. Hence T21W is the odd one out.

Front: What quick mantra helps you remember that opposite letters always sum to 27?
Back: “27 से घटाओ, विपरीत पाओ” (Subtract from 27, find the partner). Works every time.

Front: In GK-based classification, why is “litre” a sly trap when SI units are listed?
Back: Litre is an accepted unit of volume but not an official SI unit. The SI unit for volume is cubic metre (m³).

Front: What is the single most important question to ask each option during classification?
Back: “Do you follow this rule? Yes or No?” If exactly four answer differently from the fifth, you’ve found the rule and the odd one.
