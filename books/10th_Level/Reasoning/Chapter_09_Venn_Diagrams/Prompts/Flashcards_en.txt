📋 Flashcards – Venn Diagrams

1. 🃏 Front:
What is the one question you must ask before drawing a Venn diagram for any three given words?
Back:
“Is one word completely inside another, or are they just overlapping?” This instantly tells you which of the three fundamental relationships (subset, partial overlap, or no overlap) you are dealing with.

2. 🃏 Front:
How many total distinct regions are always formed when three circles overlap in the most general way?
Back:
7 regions. Three for “only one”, three for “exactly two”, and one central region for “all three”.

3. 🃏 Front:
Why does “Only Hindi” mean a completely different region from “Hindi” in a counting question?
Back:
“Only Hindi” refers to the part of the Hindi circle that does not touch any other circle. “Hindi” alone could include those who also speak other languages. The word “only” locks you into the exclusive zone.

4. 🃏 Front:
What is the “PIG” mnemonic for solving word-to-diagram questions quickly?
Back:
P – Part (check if one is subset of another)
I – Intersect (can any two overlap?)
G – Gap (must any two remain separate?)
Use this mental checklist to choose the correct Venn diagram option in seconds.

5. 🃏 Front:
If “All cats are mammals” and “Some mammals are wild”, can you conclude “Some cats are wild”?
Back:
No. The cat circle sits inside the mammal circle, but it can be placed entirely in the part of mammal that does not overlap with wild. Since we must build the minimum diagram, the conclusion is not definite.

6. 🃏 Front:
What is the “Chewing Gum” trick for testing a conclusion in syllogism questions?
Back:
Imagine a wad of gum that represents one person. Stick it only where the statements force it. To test if “Some A are C” is definite, try placing the gum on A in a way that avoids C. If you can avoid it, the conclusion is not certain. If you can’t avoid it, it follows.

7. 🃏 Front:
Why is the phrase “at least two” a magnet for silly mistakes in diagram counting?
Back:
Because “at least two” includes the central “all three” region. Many students forget to add that central number and count only the three “exactly two” regions, falling straight into the examiner’s trap.

8. 🃏 Front:
Examiner’s Trap:
What is the classic “Some Not” switcheroo that test-setters love?
Back:
Statement: “Some students are not athletes.”
Trap conclusion: “Therefore, some students are athletes.”
Reality: “Some not” only guarantees an existence outside the athlete circle; it says nothing about the inside. Never reverse a “some not” statement.

9. 🃏 Front:
If “No A is B”, and “All B are C”, can “Some A are C” ever be true?
Back:
Yes, it can be true — A and C can still overlap because A and B are separate, but C is a bigger circle that contains B and may extend to overlap with A. However, it is not definite; the minimum diagram would keep A and C separate unless forced otherwise.

10. 🃏 Front:
What mistake do most students make when choosing a Venn diagram for “Paper, Book, Printer”?
Back:
They think “Printer” prints books so it must overlap with Book. But a Printer is a device, not a type of book or paper. The correct diagram: Paper is subset of Book (paper used in books), while Printer is a completely separate circle. This is a hidden double-relationship trap.

11. 🃏 Front:
How can you quickly check if one circle is a subset of another just by looking at the words?
Back:
Ask: “Is every X necessarily a Y?” For example, is every Surgeon a Doctor? Yes. So Surgeon is a subset of Doctor. If the answer is not 100% yes, then it’s not a subset. This one question separates nested circles from overlapping ones.

12. 🃏 Front:
What does “Some A are B” actually guarantee in a Venn diagram?
Back:
It guarantees that there is at least one member that belongs to both A and B. It does NOT guarantee that there are members of A that are outside B, nor that all B are in A. The overlap must exist, but its size is unknown.

13. 🃏 Front:
Mnemonic trick:
What is the “Some = Maybe All” rule?
Back:
When you hear “Some A are B”, never lock yourself into a partial overlap. The possibility “All A are B” is still allowed (unless another statement prohibits it). Keeping the “Maybe All” possibility alive prevents you from incorrectly rejecting conclusions.

14. 🃏 Front:
In the “Chewing Gum” game, how would you represent “All pens are books” and “No book is a table”?
Back:
The “Pen” gum must always be inside “Book”. The “Book” and “Table” circles have a permanent wall between them — no gum can ever sit in both. So the “Pen” gum automatically stays away from “Table”.

15. 🃏 Front:
What is the difference between “Exactly two” and “At most two” in a three-circle Venn diagram?
Back:

“Exactly two” includes only the three regions where exactly two circles overlap (not the centre).

“At most two” includes all regions except the very centre (the “all three” zone).
Examiner often uses these phrases in the same set to confuse you.

16. 🃏 Front:
Why is the “minimum possible Venn diagram” so important in testing syllogism conclusions?
Back:
Because a conclusion must be true in every possible diagram that satisfies the statements. The minimum diagram (the one with the least assumed overlaps) is the easiest to fail — if the conclusion holds there, it holds everywhere. If it fails there, it’s not a valid conclusion.

17. 🃏 Front:
Trap Card:
Question says, “Number of people who speak only Hindi and English.” Which part of the diagram do you pick?
Back:
You must pick the intersection of Hindi and English that does not intersect with the third circle. Many students mistakenly include the central “all three” region. Always underline the word “only” before counting.

18. 🃏 Front:
When you see “Fruit, Mango, Banana”, which Venn diagram is correct, and why?
Back:
A large circle “Fruit” containing two smaller separate circles “Mango” and “Banana”. Both are fruits, but a mango cannot be a banana. So they are disjoint subsets inside a universal set.

19. 🃏 Front:
What is the fastest way to double-check your answer in a logical Venn diagram question?
Back:
Read the chosen diagram and ask: “Is every member of word A placed exactly where it should be according to common sense?” Check for forced subsets, impossible overlaps, and missing gaps. This 5-second sanity test catches most errors.

20. 🃏 Front:
If “All teachers are graduates” and “Some graduates are players”, can we say “Some teachers are players” definitely?
Back:
No. Teachers sit fully inside graduates. The overlap of graduates with players may lie completely outside the teacher part. The minimum diagram keeps them separate, so the conclusion is not definite. Only a possibility.
